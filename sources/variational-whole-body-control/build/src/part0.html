From the Remaining Action to the Forces on a Task

A variational-mechanics view of receding-window stabilization and hierarchical passivity-based whole-body control:
Lagrangians, momenta, Gauss’s principle and the multipliers of tasks
November 2026
Abstract. Model predictive control (MPC) stabilizes a system by repeatedly solving a finite-time optimal control problem and applying only its first move; the classical stability argument uses the optimal value function as a Lyapunov function, made to decrease by a terminal equality constraint (Keerthi & Gilbert, 1988) or, as here, by a terminal cost and a terminal set (Chen & Allgöwer, 1998; Mayne et al., 2000). Whole-body control shares one torque vector among prioritized tasks, one instant at a time. This document reads both in the language of analytical mechanics. It builds the receding-window results from one starting point, Bellman’s principle of optimality; the whole-body results add Gauss’s principle, with tasks as constraints, and a block factorization. The stage cost becomes a Lagrangian and the value function the “remaining action” (Hamilton’s principal function plus the boundary term). For a window of $N$ steps the principle of optimality is a monotone recursion, so a boundary term that passes a one-step test makes the remaining action fall, by at least the action just spent, for every window length. Letting the step shrink turns the recursion into the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, its minimization into a Legendre transform whose variable is the momentum $p=-\nabla V$, and the one-step test into the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality $H(q,-\nabla W)\ge0$; its characteristics are Hamilton’s equations. Discretizing again shows, with numbers, that exactness of one step is a fixed-point property of the pair of discrete Lagrangian and boundary term: the continuous-time Riccati boundary term makes one step exact with the exact discrete Lagrangian (Marsden & West, 2001) and also with the midpoint rule, but not with the rectangle rule, which needs its own discrete-Riccati boundary term. For mechanical plants the Lagrange multipliers carry the physics. The multipliers of the equations of motion are the momenta (Ostrogradsky’s momenta, the defect multipliers of multiple shooting); with Gauss’s weight the infinitesimal-window torque is the velocity momentum per unit time; and a task imposed as a constraint in Gauss’s principle has the operational-space force as its multiplier (Bruyninckx & Khatib, 2000). Three distinctions recur. Argmin versus certificate: a two-link arm whose infinitesimal-window rule, for a cost on the gravity-compensated torque $u=\tau-g$, is PD control with gravity compensation, but whose classical Slotine–Li gain matching fails the certificate, and the mechanical energy, which certifies only damping, show that a certificate belongs to the pair of Lagrangian and boundary term. Impressed versus constraint forces: passivity-based control with natural inertia versus operational-space control. Invariant versus attributed: the window length leaves the certificate unchanged and, for a given torque, the priority order leaves the total task power unchanged, while the split of the remaining action between window and tail, and of the power among priority levels, depends on the choice. For hierarchies, one block factorization of the stacked mobility, a Gram–Schmidt process in the $M^{-1}$ metric, yields the dynamically consistent extended Jacobians, the decoupled inertias and the dictionary $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ between decoupled task forces and stacked multipliers, with consequences for energy tanks at active-set switches. Inequalities (speed and torque limits, terminal regions, joint limits; contacts in outline) keep this structure for each active set, or contact mode without frictional sliding, and a feasible stack: multipliers acquire signs and active sets switch. Frictional sliding, impacts and friction-cone switching are not treated, and infeasible hierarchies only in outline (Section SEC(sec-soft)). An active terminal constraint acts as an inflated boundary term; the sign of a unilateral multiplier survives neither a change of basis nor a pairing with velocity; and an activation anywhere in a hierarchy, in the constraint view, re-attributes forces and power at and below its level while the torque and the total power are unchanged, whereas with prescribed (impressed) forces both jump in general unless the forces are re-mapped through $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ at the switch. Part I · Receding windows as mechanics

SECNUM Setting and Road Map

SECNUM The idea in one picture

A receding-window scheme looks ahead over a window of $N$ steps, finds the cheapest admissible path that ends inside a small terminal region $\Omega$ around the rest point, pays a boundary term $W$ for wherever that path ends, and then follows only the first segment. At the next instant it does the same again from the new point (Fig. 1).

The first question this document answers is why this converges. The answer will be that the remaining action, the least action still to be paid from the current point, can only go down, and by at least the action of the step just taken, so that it is a Lyapunov function. The argument needs a single principle, and every quantity it produces on the way (momentum, Legendre transform, Hamilton–Jacobi equation) has a name in analytical mechanics. The second question is what the same quantities say about whole-body control, where one torque vector has to serve several tasks at once. There the Lagrange multipliers of the tasks will turn out to be the forces acting on them, and the priority order of a hierarchy a choice of basis for those forces.

SECNUM The variational problem

Part I is written for a first-order (“kinematic”) plant, in which the velocity is the decision variable:

$$ \dot q=v,\qquad v\in\mathcal V(q),\qquad q\in\mathbb R^n . \tag{EQDEF(plant1)} $$

The set of admissible velocities $\mathcal V(q)$ is closed; it encodes unilateral constraints such as a speed limit, and it is all of $\mathbb R^n$ when there are none. This keeps the mechanics visible with the least notation. A robot is a second-order plant, in which the decision variable is a torque that sets an acceleration; Part II redoes the construction for that case.

A path is scored by its action: the integral of a variational Lagrangian $L(q,v)$ plus a boundary term $W$ paid where the path ends, which must be inside the terminal region $\Omega$:

$$ S=\int_t^{t_f}L\big(q,\dot q\big)\,dt'+W\big(q(t_f)\big),\qquad q(t_f)\in\Omega . \tag{EQDEF(action)} $$

In MPC terms $L$ is the stage cost, $S$ the cost over the horizon, $W$ the terminal cost and $\Omega$ the terminal set. The variational Lagrangian is a cost we choose; it is not the Lagrangian of the plant, and the two will be kept apart throughout. A function $\alpha\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is of class $\mathcal K$ if it is continuous, strictly increasing and $\alpha(0)=0$; it is of class $\mathcal K_\infty$ if, in addition, $\alpha(\rho)\to\infty$ as $\rho\to\infty$. These are the comparison functions of Lyapunov theory (Rawlings et al., 2017, App. B). The standing assumptions are:

Standing assumptions.
  • $L(0,0)=0$, $L(q,v)\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$ with $\alpha_L\in\mathcal K_\infty$; $L$ is continuously differentiable, strictly convex in $v$ and superlinear in $v$: $L(q,v)/|v|\to\infty$ as $|v|\to\infty$, uniformly for $q$ in compact sets (superlinearity is not needed where $\mathcal V(q)$ is compact);
  • $W$ is continuous, $W\ge0$ and $W(0)=0$; wherever $\nabla W$ is used (from Section SEC(sec-infinitesimal) on), $W$ is continuously differentiable on a neighbourhood of $\Omega$;
  • $\Omega$ is closed and contains a neighbourhood of the rest point $q=0$.

Running example. The quadratic Lagrangian $L=\tfrac12q^\top Qq+\tfrac12v^\top Rv$, $Q,R\succ0$, meets these assumptions, and its scalar case

$$ L=\tfrac12m v^2+\tfrac12\kappa \,q^2,\qquad m,\kappa >0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\kappa /m}, \tag{EQDEF(example)} $$

(that is, $Q=\kappa$, $R=m$) is the simplest one. It penalizes speed and distance from the rest point. Every result of Part I will be computed on it as soon as it is derived.

SECNUM Steps of a digital controller

A digital controller works in steps of length $h$. Over one step the action is replaced by a discrete Lagrangian $L_d(q,q')$, the cost of moving from $q$ to $q'$ in one step (Marsden & West, 2001): a function of the two endpoints, finite on admissible pairs (those that a velocity in $\mathcal V$ can join) and $+\infty$ otherwise. The simplest choice evaluates $L$ once per step, the rectangle rule:

$$ L_d(q,q')=h\,L\Big(q,\frac{q'-q}{h}\Big),\qquad\text{for the example}\quad L_d=\frac{m}{2h}(q'-q)^2+\frac h2\,\kappa q^2 . \tag{EQDEF(rect)} $$

Section SEC(sec-ld) will single out a distinguished choice, the exact discrete Lagrangian, which needs the solution of the continuous problem; nothing before it depends on which $L_d$ is used. Positivity carries over to steps: we assume $L_d(q,q')\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$ on admissible pairs, with a class-$\mathcal K_\infty$ function again written $\alpha_L$ (for EQ(rect) it is $h$ times the one of $L$).

SECNUM Road map and reading guide

Part I derives everything from Bellman’s principle of optimality. Applied to a window of steps it gives a recursion (Section SEC(sec-core)), whose monotonicity yields descent of the remaining action for every window length (Section SEC(sec-proof)). Letting the step shrink turns the recursion into the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the descent condition into a pointwise certificate (Section SEC(sec-hj)); solving the equation along paths gives Hamilton’s equations and the worked example of a hilltop (Section SEC(sec-extremals)). Going back to steps answers when one step is exact (Section SEC(sec-discrete)), and inequalities are added last (Section SEC(sec-ineq1)). Part II repeats the construction for mechanical plants, where it needs Lagrange multipliers, identifies them as momenta and, through Gauss’s principle, as forces on tasks, and works it out on a two-link arm. Part III treats several tasks at once: a priority hierarchy turns out to be a change of basis for the task forces, with consequences for inequality tasks, power and energy tanks. Part IV collects the recurring threads and the verification.
