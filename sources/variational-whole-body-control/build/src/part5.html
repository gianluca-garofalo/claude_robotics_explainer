SECNUM Gauss’s Principle: Multipliers Are Forces

Section SEC(sec-second-order) ended with a torque set by a momentum. This section shows that the infinitesimal-window rule with Gauss’s weight is Gauss’s principle of least constraint, and that a task imposed as a constraint in it has a force as its multiplier. This is where whole-body control enters.

SECNUM Gauss’s principle as the infinitesimal-window rule

Gauss’s principle of least constraint says that, among all accelerations compatible with the constraints, a mechanical system realizes the one closest to its free acceleration, measured in the kinetic-energy metric (Udwadia & Kalaba, 1996):

$$ \begin{gathered} \ddot q=\arg\min_{\ddot q}\ \tfrac12\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})^\top M\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free}) \quad\text{s.t. the constraints},\\ \ddot q_{\rm free}=M^{-1}(\tau_{\rm imp}-b). \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(gauss)} $$

The free acceleration is what the impressed torque $\tau_{\rm imp}$, a given function of the state (gravity compensation, springs, dampers), would produce on its own. Write the actual acceleration as the free one plus a correction produced by an extra torque, $M{(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})}=\tau_c$, so that $\tau=\tau_{\rm imp}+\tau_c$. Then the Gauss function is

$$ \tfrac12\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})^\top M\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=\tfrac12\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c , \tag{EQDEF(gaussfn)} $$

which is the torque cost of Section SEC(sec-so-manipulator) centred on $\tau_{\rm imp}$, with weight $M^{-1}$. To be added to $\dot W$, a power, the cost needs a time constant: $\ell=\tfrac{T_0}2\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c$, i.e. the weight $R=T_0M^{-1}$, which we call Gauss’s weight. So Gauss’s principle is a one-step variational rule: over an infinitesimal step, choose the correction torque of least Gauss effort that achieves what is required. What is required can be said in two ways.

  • By a boundary term. Minimizing $\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c+\dot W$ over $\tau_c$ gives, by EQ(manip) with $R=T_0M^{-1}$, $$\tau_c=\frac{\pi_v}{T_0},\qquad \pi_v=-\nabla_vW:\tag{EQDEF(gausstorque)}$$ the inertia cancels, and the correction torque is the velocity momentum of $W$ per unit time. The multiplier of the dynamics is the force, up to $T_0$.
  • By a constraint. Prescribe what a task must do; then the multiplier of the task is the force (Section SEC(sec-gauss-task)).

In discrete time. Identity EQ(gausstorque) is exact in continuous time. In a multiple-shooting transcription with the stage cost $h\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_j^\top M^{-1}\tau_j$, the stationarity condition of EQ(mskkt) reads $hT_0M^{-1}\tau_j=\partial_\tau\Phi_h^\top\pi_{j+1}$, with $\pi_{j+1}=-\nabla V_{N-j-1}(z_{j+1})$, so, written with the momenta at the end of the step, the position momentum enters as soon as the torque moves the position within the step:

  • Zero-order hold (exact if $M$ and $b$ are both constant, as for the free mass of the example below; to leading order in $h$ otherwise): $\partial_\tau\Phi_h=\big(\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1},\,hM^{-1}\big)$, so $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+\tfrac h2\,\pi_{q,j+1}$. A constant $M$ alone is not enough: it leaves $b=g(q)$, and a change of torque moves $q$ within the step and with it $g$. Differentiating the flow of $\dot q=v$, $\dot v=M^{-1}(\tau-b)$ with respect to the held torque and expanding in $h$ gives in general the position and velocity blocks $\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1}+O(h^3)$ and $hM^{-1}-\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1}(\dot M+\partial_vb)M^{-1}+O(h^3)$, with $M$, $\dot M$ and $\partial_vb$ at $z_j$, so that $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+\tfrac h2\big[\pi_{q,j+1}-(\dot M+\partial_vb)^{\!\top}M^{-1}\pi_{v,j+1}\big]+O(h^2)$. For constant $M$ and $b=g(q)$ the bracket is $\pi_{q,j+1}$ and the identity is off only at $O(h^2)$; for a configuration-dependent $M$ in motion, or a damper, the second term of the bracket is of the same order as the first, and the position momentum is not the only $O(h)$ correction.
  • Symplectic Euler, $v_{j+1}=v_j+hM^{-1}(\tau_j-b)$, $q_{j+1}=q_j+h\,v_{j+1}$, written as an explicit map: $\partial_\tau\Phi_h=\big(h^2M^{-1},\,hM^{-1}\big)$, so $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$. Transcribed instead with $v_{j+1}$ as a node variable in the position defect, $q_{j+1}-q_j-hv_{j+1}=0$, the torque enters only the velocity defect, and $T_0\tau_j$ equals that defect’s multiplier exactly; the two transcriptions differ by a change of the defect coordinates, under which that multiplier is $\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$.

The costate recursion EQ(mskkt) carries these end-of-step momenta back to the node where the torque acts. Under symplectic Euler, $\partial v_{j+1}/\partial v_j=I-hM^{-1}b_v$ and $\partial q_{j+1}/\partial v_j=h\,(I-hM^{-1}b_v)$, with $b_v=\partial b/\partial v$ and $M$ evaluated at $z_j$, so the velocity part of EQ(mskkt) and the stationarity condition give, exactly, $$\pi_{v,j}=\big(I-h\,b_v^\top M^{-1}\big)\big(\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}\big)-\nabla_v\ell_d =\big(I-h\,b_v^\top M^{-1}\big)\,T_0\tau_j-\nabla_v\ell_d(z_j,\tau_j).$$ When neither $b$ nor the stage cost depends on $v$ (for instance Gauss’s weight alone and $b=g(q)$, with no Coriolis, centrifugal or damping terms), this is $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j}=-\nabla_vV_{N-j}(z_j)$, exactly, for $j=0,\dots,N-1$; for $j=0$, $\pi_0$ is the multiplier of the initial value (Section SEC(sec-so-discrete)). The torque is the velocity momentum at the node where it is applied: the discrete counterpart of $\tau^*=\pi_v/T_0$, EQ(manip) with Gauss’s weight. This holds in either transcription, because it involves only the remaining action, which does not depend on how the defects are written; in the implicit one, the velocity-defect multiplier of step $j$ is $\pi_{v,j}$. Coriolis terms, damping or a velocity cost change it by $O(h)$. Under zero-order hold with constant $M$ and $b$, $\partial_z\Phi_h=\begin{pmatrix}I&hI\\0&I\end{pmatrix}$, and with Gauss’s weight alone $\nabla_z\ell_d=0$; so EQ(mskkt) gives $\pi_{v,j}=\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$, and the first bullet becomes $T_0\tau_j=\tfrac12(\pi_{v,j}+\pi_{v,j+1})$: the held torque is the mean of the velocity momenta at the two ends of its step.

Take one degree of freedom, a free mass ($b=0$) with $M=2$ kg, the boundary term $W=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12K_eq^2$ with spring $K_e=30$ N/m, $T_0=0.1$ s and a window of 10 ms, starting at rest; since $M$ is constant and $b=0$, both exact identities hold. The end-of-step position-momentum term, $\tfrac h2\pi_{q,1}$ under zero-order hold and $h\pi_{q,1}$ under symplectic Euler, is then 110%, 35.5% and 5.5% of $T_0\tau_0$ under zero-order hold for $(h,N)=(10\ \text{ms},1)$, $(5\ \text{ms},2)$ and $(1\ \text{ms},10)$, and 54% and 10.5% under symplectic Euler for the last two. These numbers measure where the momentum is read, not a part of the torque supplied by the position momentum: the term is the difference $\pi_{v,0}-\pi_{v,1}=h\,\pi_{q,1}$ between the velocity momenta at the two ends of the first step (half of it under zero-order hold), and it is large only because a window started from rest, where $\nabla_vW=0$, produces a first torque of the order of the window length, so the ratio is of order $1/N$. “Torque equals velocity momentum” is exact in continuous time. In discrete time it is exact at the node where the torque is applied under symplectic Euler, and exact for the mean over the step under zero-order hold, under the conditions just stated; otherwise it holds to $O(h)$.

SECNUM A task as a constraint: operational-space control

The second answer imposes the task acceleration: $J\ddot q=a_x$, where $J=\partial x/\partial q$ is the Jacobian of a task coordinate $x(q)$ and $a_x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}-\dot J\dot q$, with $\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$ a commanded task acceleration (for instance a PD law plus feedforward): since $\dot x=J\dot q$ gives $\ddot x=J\ddot q+\dot J\dot q$, the constraint says $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$. With multiplier $\lambda$ for this constraint, the KKT conditions of EQ(gauss) are $M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J^\top\lambda$ and $J\ddot q=a_x$. Eliminating $\ddot q$:

$$ \lambda=\Lambda\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big),\qquad \tau_c=J^\top\lambda,\qquad \Lambda=(JM^{-1}J^\top)^{-1}. \tag{EQDEF(osc)} $$

This is Khatib’s operational-space control (Khatib, 1987): $a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}$ is the task acceleration that is missing from the free motion, and the task inertia $\Lambda$ converts it into the force that supplies it. The value of the Gauss function at the optimum is

$$ \min\ \tfrac12\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c=\tfrac12\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big)^\top\Lambda\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big), \tag{EQDEF(oscvalue)} $$

so $\Lambda$ is the Gauss metric pushed forward to task space (a Schur complement). The connection between Gauss’s principle and operational-space control was made explicit by Bruyninckx & Khatib (2000), building on the constrained-motion formulation of Udwadia & Kalaba (1996); the same family of control laws arises as minimizers of $\tfrac12\tau^\top R\,\tau$ subject to a task constraint, with $R=M^{-1}$ giving the operational-space law (Udwadia, 2003; Peters et al., 2008).

SECNUM The dual is the task force

Reading. In the constraint view, the force acting on the task is the Lagrange multiplier of the task constraint: $\lambda$ in EQ(osc) is a generalized force in task coordinates, and its power is $\lambda^\top\dot x$. This is the whole-body counterpart of Section SEC(sec-so-augmented): there the multipliers of the dynamics were momenta; here the multipliers of the tasks are forces. Both are duals, and both are read off a KKT system.

Units make the parallel exact. In the units of Section SEC(sec-gauss-free), where the stage cost $\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c$ is a power, the task-constrained problem has the multiplier $T_0\lambda=\Lambda\,T_0(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free})$: the task inertia times the velocity that the missing acceleration would build up over $T_0$, an impulse. Its torque is $J^\top(T_0\lambda)/T_0$, exactly as the torque of EQ(gausstorque) is $\pi_v/T_0$. Written with a cost in units of power, both multipliers are momenta, and dividing by $T_0$ turns both into forces.

Contacts. The analogy with contact is exact: a bilateral contact is a task whose acceleration is prescribed so that the contact point does not move ($\ddot x_c=0$, i.e. $a_x=-\dot J_c\dot q$), and its multiplier is the contact force. A unilateral contact turns the equality into a complementarity condition, the subject of the next subsection.

SECNUM Unilateral constraints: forces that can only push

A contact that may open, a joint limit or a distance to an obstacle is a unilateral constraint on a task coordinate, $x_i(q)\ge\underline x_i$. On the boundary it is imposed at the acceleration level, $J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q\ge a_{\mathcal I}$ (with $\dot J\dot q$ absorbed), next to equality rows $J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E}$. Gauss’s principle EQ(gauss) with such rows has the KKT conditions

$$ M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J_{\mathcal E}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal E}+J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I},\qquad J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E},\qquad 0\le\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ \perp\ J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q-a_{\mathcal I}\ge0 . \tag{EQDEF(unikkt)} $$

The constraint force can only push ($\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ge0$), and only where the bound is reached (the complementarity $\perp$: each component of $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ or of the margin vanishes). Eliminating $\ddot q$ leaves $\lambda_{\mathcal E}$ and $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ coupled through the mobility of all rows (the Delassus matrix of contact mechanics), whose blocks are $J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$, $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$ and $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top$. The equality rows can be eliminated as well. Together with the second, $J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E}$, the first condition of EQ(unikkt) is the KKT system of Section SEC(sec-gauss-task) for the equality rows, with the free acceleration $\ddot q_{\rm free}+M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I}$; so, for $J_{\mathcal E}$ of full row rank, EQ(osc) gives $\lambda_{\mathcal E}=\Lambda_{\mathcal E}\big(a_{\mathcal E}-J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q_{\rm free}-J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I}\big)$, with $\Lambda_{\mathcal E}=(J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top)^{-1}$ the task inertia of the equality rows. What remains is a linear complementarity problem for $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ alone, $$0\le\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ \perp\ \big(J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top-J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top\Lambda_{\mathcal E}J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\big)\lambda_{\mathcal I}+J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q_{\mathcal E}-a_{\mathcal I}\ \ge0 ,$$ where $\ddot q_{\mathcal E}=\ddot q_{\rm free}+M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top\Lambda_{\mathcal E}\big(a_{\mathcal E}-J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q_{\rm free}\big)$ is Gauss’s acceleration with the equality rows alone, so that the offset is the margin at $\lambda_{\mathcal I}=0$. Its matrix is the mobility of the unilateral rows while the equality rows are held: the Schur complement of $J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$ in the mobility of all rows, which is also the pivot of the unilateral rows when the stack $[J_{\mathcal E};J_{\mathcal I}]$ is factored block by block as in Section SEC(sec-ldl). It is positive definite when this stack has full row rank, and the complementarity problem then has a unique solution. It reduces to the unilateral block $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top$ exactly when $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top=0$, in particular when there are no equality rows. Optimality in the convex problem EQ(gauss) can also be written as Fourier’s inequality, the unilateral form of d’Alembert’s principle: the gradient of the Gauss function, $M{(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})}=\tau_c$, makes a non-negative product with every admissible direction,

$$ \tau_c^\top\big(\ddot q'-\ddot q\big)\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{for every admissible acceleration }\ddot q' . \tag{EQDEF(fourier)} $$

In a fixed-seed example ($n=7$, a two-row equality task, three unilateral rows) two rows end up active with $\lambda=1.770$ and $1.609$, the third is inactive with $\lambda=0$ and a margin of $0.375$, and EQ(fourier) holds on 1407 sampled admissible accelerations.

At which level is a unilateral force workless? Complementarity can be written at three levels, and only one of them speaks about power.
  • Position (Signorini): gap $x_c\ge0$, force $f_n\ge0$, $f_nx_c=0$. The force acts only at contact.
  • Velocity (Moreau): on a closed contact, $\dot x_c\ge0$, $f_n\ge0$, $f_n\dot x_c=0$. The power $f_n\dot x_c$ vanishes identically; at an impact the velocity jumps and, with Moreau’s impact law and restitution coefficients up to one, energy is lost, not gained (Moreau, 1988; Brogliato, 2016).
  • Acceleration (Gauss, EQ(unikkt)): the complementarity pairs force with acceleration. A contact force is workless here only because $\dot x_c=0$ on a persistent contact.
For an inequality task whose acceleration bound $a_i(x_i,\dot x_i)$ is a feedback law, nothing ties $\dot x_i$ to zero while the row is active, and $\lambda_i\ge0$ does not make $\lambda_i\dot x_i\le0$. In the example above, at a velocity for which one active row stays on its bound ($\dot x=0$) and the other recedes from it ($\dot x=0.5$), the powers are $0$ and $+0.805$: the second force pushes in the allowed direction and injects energy. The power is non-positive only if a row can be active solely while its coordinate does not recede ($\dot x_i\le0$ whenever $\lambda_i>0$).

Passivity. Contacts with complementarity at the velocity level are passive constraints: no work while smooth, dissipation at impacts. Feedback-defined inequality tasks are not passive by construction; their power belongs in the energy bookkeeping like that of any task (Section SEC(sec-ineq-power)). A sign on a force is not a sign on its power.

SECNUM Impressed versus Constraint Forces

Section SEC(sec-gauss) gave two ways to say what a task requires: through a boundary term (Section SEC(sec-gauss-free)) and through a constraint (Section SEC(sec-gauss-task)). This section compares them as impressed and constraint forces, and asks what certifies each.

SECNUM Two ways to act on a task

Analytical mechanics distinguishes impressed forces, which are given functions of the state, from constraint forces, which take whatever value the constraint requires. A controller can play either role; in the notation of Section SEC(sec-gauss-free), it acts through $\tau_{\rm imp}$ or through $\tau_c$.

  • Constraint (Section SEC(sec-gauss-task)). Impose $J\ddot q=a_x$. The controller supplies whatever force $\lambda$ that takes. The closed-loop task dynamics are $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$: the task behaves as a unit mass, i.e. the controller shapes the inertia. An unmeasured external force $J^\top F_{\rm ext}$, which the controller does not see in $\ddot q_{\rm free}$, still acts through the natural inertia: the plant obeys $M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J^\top(\lambda+F_{\rm ext})$ with $\lambda$ from EQ(osc), so $J\ddot q=a_x+\Lambda^{-1}F_{\rm ext}$, i.e. $\Lambda(\ddot x-\ddot x_{\rm cmd})=F_{\rm ext}$, and shaping the apparent inertia against $F_{\rm ext}$ requires force feedback. A unilateral version of the constraint supplies a force of one sign only (Section SEC(sec-unilateral)).
  • Impressed. Apply a task force $F(x,\dot x)$, for example $F=-K_{P,x}\tilde x-K_{D,x}\dot x$ with $\tilde x=x-x_d$ the error from a constant task target $x_d$, as an additional torque $J^\top F$ inside $\tau_{\rm imp}$. Gauss’s principle with no task constraint returns the free motion under that torque, and the task acceleration changes, relative to its value without $F$, by $$\Delta\ddot x=JM^{-1}J^\top F=\Lambda^{-1}F .\tag{EQDEF(impressed)}$$ The task responds with its natural inertia $\Lambda$: in the absence of other effects, $\Lambda\ddot x+K_{D,x}\dot x+K_{P,x}\tilde x=F_{\rm ext}$, an impedance with the robot’s own inertia.

Passivity-based (impedance) control with preserved natural inertia, the family studied by Ott (2008), Ott et al. (2015), Dietrich & Ott (2020) and Garofalo & Ott (2020), is the impressed-force view. Operational-space control is the constraint view. The two coincide in what they achieve (convergence of the task), but they assign different forces to the task and inject different power, which is what matters for interaction and passivity.

SECNUM Argmin or certificate?

Which of the two is optimal in the sense of the infinitesimal-window rule, and what certifies it? Take the rule with an energy-type boundary term, the kinetic energy plus a designed (“shaped”) potential $U_s$ in place of the gravitational one. Differentiating along $M\ddot q+C\dot q+g=\tau$,

$$ W=\tfrac12\,v^\top M v+U_s(q)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot W=\dot q^\top\big(\tau-g+\nabla U_s\big), \tag{EQDEF(energyW)} $$

where the term $\tfrac12\dot q^\top(\dot M-2C)\dot q$ vanished because $\dot M-2C$ is skew-symmetric for the Christoffel-symbol choice of $C$: the Coriolis and centrifugal forces do no work on the kinetic energy (Spong et al., 2006). The torque enters $\dot W$ only through the power $\dot q^\top\tau$, and the velocity momentum of this $W$ is $\pi_v=-\nabla_vW=-Mv$, minus the mechanical momentum. Now take a stage cost centred on an impressed torque $\tau_{\rm imp}(q)$,

$$ \ell=\tfrac12\,(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})^\top R\,(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})+\ell_0(q,v), \qquad \tau^\ast=\arg\min_\tau\big[\ell+\dot W\big]=\tau_{\rm imp}-R^{-1}\dot q , \tag{EQDEF(centred)} $$

the minimizer following from the gradient $R(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})+\dot q=0$. With $\tau_{\rm imp}=g-\nabla U_s$ (gravity compensation plus the shaped potential, e.g. $\nabla U_s=J^\top K_{P,x}\tilde x$), EQ(centred) is the passivity-based law $\tau=g-J^\top K_{P,x}\tilde x-K_D\dot q$ with $K_D=R^{-1}$. With Gauss’s weight $R=T_0M^{-1}$ the correction is $\tau_c=-M\dot q/T_0=\pi_v/T_0$, which is EQ(gausstorque): damping proportional to the mechanical momentum, $K_D=M/T_0$. Substituting $\tau^\ast$ gives the descent defect of Section SEC(sec-so-hj),

$$ \mathcal D=\min_\tau\big[\ell+\dot W\big]=\ell_0(q,v)-\tfrac12\,v^\top K_D\,v\ \le\ 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_0\le\tfrac12\,v^\top K_D\,v . \tag{EQDEF(defectE)} $$
Argmin versus certificate. Both laws minimize a one-step problem; they differ in what certifies them.
  • The operational-space law minimizes the Gauss cost, centred on the free motion, subject to the task as a hard constraint (Section SEC(sec-gauss-task)). No boundary term enters; its stability is that of the imposed task dynamics $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$.
  • The passivity-based law with natural inertia minimizes the cost EQ(centred), centred on the shaped impressed torque, plus $\dot W$ for the energy-type $W$ of EQ(energyW), and the minimum value EQ(defectE) is the certificate EQ(cert2). The minimization contributes only the damping $-R^{-1}\dot q$; stiffness and gravity compensation enter through $\tau_{\rm imp}$, which must match the $U_s$ in $W$.
Gauss’s weight, which makes the damping proportional to the inertia, is one modelling choice, not a requirement.

SECNUM Why energy alone certifies only damping

Condition EQ(defectE) requires $\ell_0(q,0)\le0$: a non-negative $\ell_0$ must vanish at rest, so it cannot penalize the task error $\tilde x$ there, because $\dot W$ in EQ(energyW) contains no term that is negative when $v=0$ and $\tilde x\neq0$. This is the precise sense in which the mechanical energy is a weak Lyapunov function:

  • the infinitesimal-window rule with an energy-type $W$ produces pure damping around the impressed torque; any stiffness has to be placed in $\tau_{\rm imp}$ by hand;
  • convergence of $\tilde x$ then follows from LaSalle’s invariance principle, not from EQ(cert2) with a positive-definite $\ell$;
  • a strict certificate needs cross terms between position and velocity in $W$, as in the composite variable $s=\dot e+\Gamma e$ of Slotine & Li (1987), worked out in Section SEC(sec-robot).
Take-away. “Optimal torque = momentum” EQ(gausstorque) is true, but with an energy-type $W$ the momentum $-\nabla_vW=-Mv$ carries no position information. Position feedback enters either through an impressed torque on which the designer centres the cost, or through a $W$ with cross terms. Natural-inertia controllers take the first route; Section SEC(sec-robot) takes the second.

SECNUM The plant’s energy as the boundary term

There is published precedent for taking a plant energy as $W$. Kawai et al. (2005) apply receding-horizon control to a two-link direct-drive manipulator with an energy function as the terminal cost (our boundary term $W$) instead of a terminal constraint (our region $\Omega$). The energy serves as a control Lyapunov function, chosen by considering inverse optimality, and the paper reports experiments on the arm. Terminal costs without terminal constraints are the setting of Jadbabaie et al. (2001). Two cautions apply when reusing the idea on another plant:

  • The Lagrangian must match $W$. If the Lagrangian is constructed from the energy (inverse optimality), descent holds by design. Choosing $\ell$ independently and then reusing the energy as $W$ does not guarantee descent: check EQ(terminal), or its continuous form EQ(cert2), for your $\ell$. Section SEC(sec-certificate) showed one way the match fails (a position penalty against a weak $W$); Section SEC(sec-robot-condition) shows another (the Slotine–Li spring).
  • One paper is evidence of a workable construction, not a general theorem. Any specific plant still needs its own check.
