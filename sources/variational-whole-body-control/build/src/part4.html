SECNUM Inequalities: Reactions, Saturation and the Exact Boundary Term

Speed limits, torque limits and the terminal region are inequalities. Sections SEC(sec-core)–SEC(sec-hj) already allowed them (admissible pairs, the set $\mathcal V(q)$, the region $\Omega$); this section says what they change. Multipliers acquire signs and enter the momenta as reactions, the rule saturates, and the exact boundary term changes.

SECNUM One step with active constraints

Write the admissibility of the step and the terminal region as inequalities $\chi_i(q,q')\le0$ on the free next point $q'$. If the gradients of the active ones are linearly independent, the minimizer $q^+$ of the one-step problem EQ(onestep) satisfies the KKT conditions

$$ \begin{gathered} \partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)+\sum_i\mu_i\nabla_{q'}\chi_i(q,q^+)=0,\\ \mu_i\ge0,\qquad \chi_i(q,q^+)\le0,\qquad \mu_i\,\chi_i(q,q^+)=0, \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(kkt)} $$

the explicit form of the inclusion $0\in\partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)+\mathcal N_{\mathcal C}(q^+)$, with $\mathcal N_{\mathcal C}$ the normal cone of the feasible set $\mathcal C$ of next points (for a longer window, $W$ is replaced by $V_{N-1}$). Without active constraints EQ(kkt) is EQ(dstationarity). By EQ(dlegendre) the arrival momentum becomes $p^+=-\nabla W(q^+)-\sum_i\mu_i\nabla_{q'}\chi_i(q,q^+)$: minus the gradient of the boundary term, corrected by the reactions of the active constraints. Two cases recur below.

  • A bound on the step. Take the rectangle rule of Section SEC(sec-numeric), $L_d=\tfrac1{2h}(q'-q)^2+\tfrac h2q^2$, with $W=\tfrac12q^2$ and $h=0.1$, and add the speed limit $q-q'\le h\bar v$ with $\bar v=0.5$. From $q=1$ the unconstrained minimizer solves $(q^+-q)/h+q^+=0$, i.e. $q^+=1/(1+h)=0.909$, which violates the bound; so the bound is active, $q^+=0.95$, and EQ(kkt) with $\nabla_{q'}\chi=-1$ gives $\mu=(q^+-q)/h+q^+=0.45$. The arrival momentum is $p^+=(q^+-q)/h=-0.50$ instead of $-W'(q^+)=-0.95$.
  • The terminal region as a sublevel set, $W(q')\le\bar W$. Its gradient is $\nabla W$ itself, so $p^+=-(1+\mu)\nabla W(q^+)$: an active terminal constraint acts like a locally inflated boundary term $(1+\mu)W$, a scaled boundary term $cW$ with $c=1+\mu$ chosen by the optimizer (compare the inflation of Section SEC(sec-exactness)). Section SEC(sec-robot-limits) measures $\mu$ on the arm.

SECNUM Constrained velocities in the infinitesimal rule

If the admissible velocities form a closed convex set $\mathcal V(q)$, Sections SEC(sec-hjderiv)–SEC(sec-allwindows) hold as written, with the constrained Hamiltonian $H_{\mathcal V}$ of EQ(legendre):

$$ v^*(q)=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L+\nabla W\cdot v\big],\qquad \min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L+\dot W\big]\le0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H_{\mathcal V}\big(q,-\nabla W\big)\ge0 , \tag{EQDEF(ruleV)} $$

and EQ(wdot) holds with $H_{\mathcal V}$. Three things change.

  • The argmin is a projection. For $L$ quadratic in $v$ with weight $R$, $L+\nabla W\cdot v=\tfrac12(v-v_u)^\top R\,(v-v_u)+\text{const}$ with $v_u$ the unconstrained minimizer, so $v^*$ is the $R$-metric projection of $v_u$ onto $\mathcal V(q)$. For a box and a diagonal $R$ this is componentwise clipping, a saturated law; for a non-diagonal $R$ it is not (Section SEC(sec-robot-limits)).
  • The Legendre map saturates. Where the constraint is active, $v^*$ sits on the boundary of $\mathcal V(q)$ and no longer follows the momentum. There the problem behaves like the singular case of Section SEC(sec-momentum); an interior, momentum-determined velocity survives only where the constraint is inactive.
  • The certificate becomes stronger. Since $H_{\mathcal V}\le H$, condition EQ(ruleV) implies EQ(cert) but not conversely: a boundary term that certifies the unconstrained rule can fail once the constraint binds. Constraints shrink the certified region; they add no new mechanism.

SECNUM Worked example: the hilltop with a speed limit

The fixed-point statement of Section SEC(sec-exactness) needs care with an active constraint. With $W$ equal to the remaining action of the constrained problem, the principle of optimality still gives $V_1=W$. The Riccati weight equals it only where the unconstrained optimal path stays admissible; elsewhere it under-estimates the tail, exactly like $c<1$, and the certificate fails even when the rule’s argmin is optimal. The hilltop shows this in closed form. Add the bound $|v|\le\bar v$ to the example EQ(example), so $\mathcal V=[-\bar v,\bar v]$, and write $q_s=\bar v/\omega$. The supremum in EQ(legendre) is attained at $v=p/m$ if that is admissible and at $v=\bar v\,\mathrm{sign}(p)$ otherwise, so the constrained Hamiltonian is

$$ H_{\mathcal V}(q,p)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p^2}{2m}-\tfrac12\kappa q^2, & |p|\le m\bar v,\\[6pt] \bar v\,|p|-\tfrac12m\bar v^2-\tfrac12\kappa q^2, & |p|>m\bar v.\end{cases} \tag{EQDEF(Hconstr)} $$
  1. The rule saturates. With $W=V$ of EQ(hilltopV) the infinitesimal-window rule EQ(ruleV) is $v^*=\mathrm{clip}(-\omega q,\,-\bar v,\,\bar v)$: the unconstrained law, clipped.
  2. The unconstrained remaining action no longer certifies it. With $p=-V'=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q$, the condition $|p|\le m\bar v$ is $|q|\le q_s$. For $|q|\le q_s$ nothing changes, $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=0$. For $|q|>q_s$, $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=-\tfrac12{\big(\sqrt\kappa\,|q|-\sqrt m\,\bar v\big)^2}<0$: condition EQ(ruleV) fails, although the state converges. With $m=\kappa=1$ and $\bar v=0.5$, at $q=1$ the value is $-0.125$.
  3. The constrained remaining action does certify it. Solving $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=0$ on the saturated branch outside $q_s$ gives $V'(q)=\big(\tfrac12m\bar v^2+\tfrac12\kappa q^2\big)/\bar v$ there, hence $$V_{\mathcal V}(q)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q^2, & |q|\le q_s,\\[2pt] \tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q_s^2+\tfrac12m\bar v\,(|q|-q_s)+\dfrac{\kappa\,(|q|^3-q_s^3)}{6\bar v}, & |q|>q_s.\end{cases}\tag{EQDEF(VV)}$$ It is $C^2$ at $q_s$ (both one-sided second derivatives equal $\sqrt{m\kappa}$) but not $C^3$, and it exceeds $V$ outside $q_s$: $V_{\mathcal V}(1)=0.5417$ against $V(1)=0.5$. Its own rule is again the clipped law, which is therefore optimal: integrating $L$ along the clipped law from $q=1$ gives $0.5417$.
The controller was optimal all along; only the boundary term was wrong. The clipped law is the exact infinite-window optimum, yet the unconstrained remaining action does not certify it wherever the speed limit binds, because it under-estimates the tail there, like $W=cV_\infty$ with $c<1$ in Section SEC(sec-stationary). An active constraint changes which boundary term is exact. In general the constrained remaining action is only piecewise smooth, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation has to be read in a generalized (viscosity) sense (Liberzon, 2012, §5.3); here it holds classically.
What Part I has established, from the principle of optimality alone.
  1. With a boundary term that passes the one-step test EQ(terminal), the remaining action is a Lyapunov function of the receding window for every window length, EQ(descent).
  2. In the continuous limit the test is the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality EQ(cert) on the pair $(L,W)$; the infinitesimal window yields a controller (the argmin) and a certificate (the sign of the minimum), and the certificate at $\vartheta=0$ covers every window, EQ(ctsmonotone).
  3. Along optimal paths the momentum is $p=-\nabla V$, the paths are the extremals of the action, and the boundary term fixes the arrival momentum, EQ(hamilton).
  4. Where the terminal constraint is inactive, one step is exact if and only if $W$ is a fixed point of the Bellman operator of the chosen discrete Lagrangian. With $L_d^E$ that operator is the continuous problem over one step, so $V_\infty$ is a fixed point; for a quadratic Lagrangian the continuous-time Riccati weight is also a fixed point for the midpoint rule (whose step is a Cayley map, not the exact sample of the LQR flow), but not for the rectangle rule, whose fixed point is $\tfrac12q^\top P_dq$ with $P_d\neq P$.
  5. Constraints add reactions to the momenta, saturate the rule, strengthen the certificate and change the exact boundary term.
Part II · Mechanical plants: momenta and forces

SECNUM Second-Order Plants: Multipliers Are Momenta

Part I assumed that the velocity is the decision variable. A robot does not work that way: we choose a torque, the torque sets an acceleration, and position and velocity follow. This section redoes the construction of Part I for such Newtonian plants. The recursion and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation carry over with $q$ replaced by the phase-space point $z=(q,v)$; what needs new work is the momentum. The classical tool for it is a Lagrange multiplier attached to every equation of motion, and the multipliers turn out to be the momenta.

SECNUM Setting, and what carries over

The state is now the pair $z=(q,v)\in\mathbb R^{2n}$, and the decision variable is a control $u\in\mathbb R^{n_u}$ that acts on the acceleration. The plant and the action are

$$ \dot q=v,\qquad \dot v=f(q,v,u),\qquad S=\int_t^{t_f}\ell(q,v,u)\,dt'+W\big(z(t_f)\big). \tag{EQDEF(plant2)} $$

A typical $f$ is the manipulator equation $M(q)\dot v+b(q,v)=\tau$. Here $M(q)$ is the inertia matrix of the kinetic energy $\mathcal T=\tfrac12v^\top M(q)v$, and $b=C(q,v)v+g(q)$ collects the Coriolis, centrifugal and gravity terms, with $g=\nabla U$ the gradient of the potential energy $U(q)$. Taking the gravity-compensated torque $u=\tau-g(q)$ as the control gives $f=M(q)^{-1}\big(u-C(q,v)v\big)$. Along solutions $v=\dot q$; we write $v$ where the velocity is a state variable (in $\ell$, $W$, $f$, $b$ and their gradients) and keep $\dot q$ in equations of motion, powers and control laws. The stage cost $\ell$ takes over the role of $L$. The standing assumptions are the analogues of Section SEC(sec-problem): $f(0,0,0)=0$ (the rest point is an equilibrium under zero control, which the gravity compensation in $u$ provides), $\ell(0,0,0)=0$, $\ell\ge\alpha_\ell(|z|)$ with $\alpha_\ell\in\mathcal K_\infty$; $f$ and $\ell$ are continuously differentiable; $f$ is affine in $u$, $f=f_0(q,v)+f_u(q,v)\,u$ (for the manipulator above, $f_0=-M^{-1}Cv$ and $f_u=M^{-1}$); $\ell$ is strictly convex and superlinear in $u$, as $L$ is in $v$ (superlinearity is not needed where the admissible inputs $\mathcal U(z)$ of Section SEC(sec-so-hj) form a compact convex set); and $W$ is as in Section SEC(sec-problem). Then the bracket $\pi_v\cdot f-\ell$ is strictly concave in $u$ and tends to $-\infty$ as $|u|\to\infty$, so the supremum over $u$ in the Hamiltonian of Section SEC(sec-so-hj) is finite and attained at a single $u^*$. The remaining action $V(z)$ is the least value of $S$ from the phase-space point $z$.

What carries over without any work. Sections SEC(sec-core) and SEC(sec-proof) used only the transition cost $L_d(z,z')$, the least action of an admissible transition from $z$ to $z'$ (infinite if there is none). Replace $q$ by $z$, let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^{2n}$ be a phase-space region and $\varphi_f$ a reference flow on it (for instance a stabilizing state feedback), and EQ(bellman), EQ(terminal), EQ(descent) and EQ(strictdescent) hold verbatim. Assumption A3 now needs $L_d(z,z')\ge\alpha_L(|z|)$, i.e. a stage cost that penalizes position and velocity. If $\ell$ ignores $v$, positivity in $z$ fails and asymptotic stability needs a detectability (LaSalle-type) argument instead; Section SEC(sec-so-example) has an example.

What does not carry over directly is the momentum. Why not simply treat $z$ as the configuration and $\dot z$ as its velocity, and reuse Section SEC(sec-momentum)? Because half of $\dot z$ is not free. The first block, $\dot q$, is pinned to the state component $v$; only the second block, $\dot v$, can be influenced, and only through $u$. Written as a function of $(z,\dot z)$, the stage cost is

$$ L_z(z,\dot z)= \begin{cases} \inf_u\big\{\ell(q,v,u):\ \dot v=f(q,v,u)\big\}, & \dot q=v,\\[2pt] +\infty, & \dot q\neq v . \end{cases} \tag{EQDEF(Lz)} $$

This Lagrangian is finite only on an affine subspace of velocities. It has no derivative with respect to $\dot q$, so the momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot q$ of Section SEC(sec-momentum) is undefined, and the Legendre map cannot be inverted: no momentum can tell the plant to move $q$ at any rate other than $v$. The way out is to let the Hamilton–Jacobi equation define the momentum, as it did in Part I, and to enforce the dynamics with multipliers.

SECNUM The Hamilton–Jacobi equation in phase space

Repeat the derivation of Section SEC(sec-hjderiv) with $z$ in place of $q$ and the control $u$ held constant over a first step of length $h$. The step costs $h\,\ell(z,u)+O(h^2)$ and, because the velocity changes during it, arrives at $z+h\dot z+O(h^2)$ with $\dot z=(v,f(z,u))$; the same expansion and the limit $h\to0$ give $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta=\min_u\big[\ell+\nabla_zV_\vartheta\cdot\dot z\big]$. Write $\pi:=-\nabla_zV$, the momentum EQ(pdef) of this window, now with a position component $\pi_q$ and a velocity component $\pi_v$. The minimum is minus the supremum

$$ H(z,\pi)=\sup_u\Big[\,\pi_q\cdot v+\pi_v\cdot f(q,v,u)-\ell(q,v,u)\,\Big], \tag{EQDEF(H2)} $$

the Hamiltonian of a second-order plant, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation reads $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta=-H(z,-\nabla_zV_\vartheta)$, exactly as EQ(hj). In Part I the supremum ran over the velocity, which was chosen freely. Here $\dot q=v$ is pinned and only $\dot v=f$ moves, through $u$. So the supremum runs over $u$, the term $\pi_q\cdot v$ sits in $H$ untouched, and $H$ is linear in $\pi_q$. It is still the Legendre transform of the degenerate Lagrangian EQ(Lz): in $\sup_{\dot z}[\pi\cdot\dot z-L_z(z,\dot z)]$ the value $+\infty$ forces $\dot q=v$, and the infimum over $u$ inside $L_z$ becomes the supremum over $u$. The transform survives; what is lost is its inverse, since $\partial H/\partial\pi_q=v$ merely restates $\dot q=v$.

With $\pi=-\nabla_zW$, the infinitesimal-window rule of Section SEC(sec-infinitesimal) and the rate of change of $W$ along it are

$$ \begin{gathered} u^*(z)=\arg\max_u\big[\pi_v\cdot f(z,u)-\ell(z,u)\big]_{\pi=-\nabla W},\\ \dot W=\nabla_zW\cdot\dot z\big|_{u^*}=-\ell(z,u^*)-H\big(z,-\nabla_zW\big), \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(rule2)} $$

which is EQ(wdot) with $q\to z$. Only $\pi_v$ enters the choice of $u$: the control is set by the sensitivity of $W$ (when $W=V_\infty$, of the remaining action) to velocity, because velocity is what the control acts on. The rest of Section SEC(sec-hj) follows by the same lines:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{stability condition:} && \min_u\big[\ell+\dot W\big]\le0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H\big(z,-\nabla_z W(z)\big)\ \ge\ 0,\\ &\text{stationary HJ:} && H\big(z,-\nabla_zV_\infty\big)=0,\\ &\text{transversality:} && \pi(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(z(t_f)\big). \end{aligned} \tag{EQDEF(cert2)} $$

The receding-window identity EQ(rhcrate) and the propagation argument of Section SEC(sec-allwindows) carry over verbatim, so the first line certifies every window length. Along an optimal path $\dot V=-\ell$ (Section SEC(sec-characteristics)), and $H=0$ for the infinite window because $H$ is conserved along extremals of an autonomous problem and $H(0,0)=-\inf_u\ell(0,0,u)=0$. The value of the bracket at its minimizer, $\mathcal D(z)=\min_u[\ell+\dot W]=-H(z,-\nabla_zW)$, the descent defect, is the form in which the certificate is used from Section SEC(sec-gauss) on: it must be non-positive.

With input constraints $u\in\mathcal U(z)$, take the supremum in EQ(H2) over $\mathcal U(z)$ (written $H_{\mathcal U}$, with descent defect $\mathcal D_{\mathcal U}$), as in Section SEC(sec-constrained-v): EQ(rule2) and EQ(cert2) keep their form, and the maximum condition $u^*\in\arg\max_{u\in\mathcal U(z)}[\pi_v\cdot f-\ell]$ is Pontryagin’s form of the principle, which needs no differentiability in $u$. State constraints are harder: in continuous time their multipliers are measures, and the momenta can jump where the path meets the constraint (Hartl et al., 1995), the optimal-control counterpart of an impact. The discrete version of Section SEC(sec-so-discrete) keeps the bookkeeping elementary.

SECNUM The multipliers are the momenta

Section SEC(sec-characteristics) followed the Hamilton–Jacobi equation along the optimal path and found Hamilton’s equations for $p=-\nabla V$. The same three steps in $z$ (differentiate the equation, use $\dot z=\partial H/\partial\pi$ along the path, cancel the Hessian terms) give, for $\pi:=-\nabla_zV$ along an optimal path,

$$ \dot z=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\pi},\qquad\dot\pi=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial z},\qquad\pi(t_f)=-\nabla_zW\big(z(t_f)\big). \tag{EQDEF(hamilton2)} $$

By the envelope theorem the derivatives of EQ(H2) are those of its bracket at the maximizing $u^*$, so $\dot z=\partial H/\partial\pi$ is the plant EQ(plant2), and the other equations, written out, are

$$ \begin{aligned} \dot\pi_q&=\nabla_q\ell-f_q^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{from }-\partial H/\partial q),\\ \dot\pi_v&=\nabla_v\ell-\pi_q-f_v^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{from }-\partial H/\partial v),\\ 0&=\nabla_u\ell-f_u^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{maximizing }u), \end{aligned} \tag{EQDEF(costate)} $$

where $f_q,f_v,f_u$ are Jacobians. These are exactly the costate equations and the stationarity condition of Pontryagin’s principle (Pontryagin et al., 1962; Bryson & Ho, 1975; Liberzon, 2012). For an infinite window, apply the argument to a finite window with $W=V_\infty$ and no terminal constraint: by the principle of optimality its remaining action is $V_\infty$ for every length ($c=1$ in EQ(finitewindow)), so $\pi=-\nabla_zV_\infty$ along the finite-action path.

The same equations come out of a variational principle once the dynamics are made visible to it. Attach a multiplier to each equation of motion, writing the constraints as “velocity minus model” so that the signs of Part I survive, and call the multipliers $\pi_q$ and $\pi_v$:

$$ \tilde L(q,v,u,\pi;\dot q,\dot v)=\ell(q,v,u)+\pi_q\cdot(\dot q-v)+\pi_v\cdot\big(\dot v-f(q,v,u)\big). \tag{EQDEF(augmented)} $$

On every path that obeys the plant the two brackets vanish, so $\int\tilde L\,dt=\int\ell\,dt$: nothing has changed except that the dynamics are now visible to the variational calculus. $\tilde L$ depends on the velocities $\dot q,\dot v$ only linearly, through $\pi_q\cdot\dot q+\pi_v\cdot\dot v$, so the canonical momenta of $\tilde L$ are the multipliers themselves: $\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot q=\pi_q$, $\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot v=\pi_v$. Treat $q$, $v$, $u$, $\pi_q$, $\pi_v$ as independent functions of time and write the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\tilde L$ for each: the rate of change of the momentum conjugate to a variable equals the derivative of $\tilde L$ with respect to that variable. For $q$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\,\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot q=\partial\tilde L/\partial q$ reads $\dot\pi_q=\nabla_q\ell-f_q^\top\pi_v$, the first line of EQ(costate); that is why $\pi$ carries a dot. The variations of $v$ and $u$ give the other two lines (the last is algebraic because $\tilde L$ contains no $\dot u$), and the variations of the multipliers give back the plant. In the variation of the action the derivative arrives by integration by parts, $\int\pi_q\cdot\delta\dot q\,dt=\big[\pi_q\cdot\delta q\big]-\int\dot\pi_q\cdot\delta q\,dt$, and the boundary term, which must cancel $\nabla W\cdot\delta z(t_f)$, gives the transversality condition in EQ(hamilton2). Finally, the Legendre transform of $\tilde L$ is EQ(H2): the $\dot z$ terms cancel in ${\pi\cdot\dot z}-\tilde L={\pi_q\cdot v}+{\pi_v\cdot f}-\ell$, and the supremum over $u$, whose stationarity condition is the third line of EQ(costate), gives EQ(H2).

The two derivations meet: the Lagrange multipliers of the equations of motion, adjoined with this sign, are the momenta $\pi=-\nabla_zV$. The calculus-of-variations derivation of optimal control introduces the costates in exactly this way, as Lagrange multipliers of the dynamics adjoined to the cost (Bryson & Ho, 1975).

Sign convention. Along an optimal path the multipliers equal minus the gradient of the remaining action, $\pi=(\pi_q,\pi_v)=\bigl(-\nabla_qV,\,-\nabla_vV\bigr)$, the analogue of $p=-\nabla V$ in Part I; Section SEC(sec-so-discrete) derives the same sign from the dynamic-programming recursion. SECNUM Fully actuated plants: a higher-order Lagrangian and Ostrogradsky’s momenta

When the control can produce any acceleration, i.e. $f_u$ is invertible, the control can be eliminated: $u$ is a function of $(q,\dot q,\ddot q)$. The cleanest case is the double integrator $f=u$ (which is also what computed-torque control makes of a manipulator); for a manipulator one substitutes $\tau=M(q)\ddot q+b(q,\dot q)$ instead. The stage cost then becomes a Lagrangian that depends on the acceleration:

$$ \hat\ell(q,\dot q,\ddot q)=\ell(q,\dot q,u)\big|_{u=\ddot q},\qquad p_1=\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q},\qquad p_2=\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q}. \tag{EQDEF(ostro)} $$

Mechanics has had a Hamiltonian theory for such Lagrangians since Ostrogradsky (1850). Its canonical coordinates are $(q,\dot q)$ and the Ostrogradsky momenta $p_1,p_2$ of EQ(ostro). They are not a new construction; they are our multipliers. With $f=u$, EQ(costate) reads $0=\nabla_u\ell-\pi_v$, $\dot\pi_v=\nabla_v\ell-\pi_q$ and $\dot\pi_q=\nabla_q\ell$. The first equation gives $\pi_v=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q=p_2$; solving the second for $\pi_q$ gives $\pi_q=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\dot q-\tfrac{d}{dt}\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q=p_1$; and the third becomes the Euler–Poisson equation of a second-order Lagrangian (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963):

$$ \frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\dot q} +\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q}=0 . \tag{EQDEF(eulerpoisson)} $$

The Ostrogradsky Hamiltonian $p_1\cdot\dot q+p_2\cdot\ddot q-\hat\ell$, with $\ddot q$ eliminated through $p_2=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q$, is EQ(H2) with $f=u$:

$$ \begin{gathered} H(q,v,p_1,p_2)=p_1\cdot v+\sup_a\big[\,p_2\cdot a-\hat\ell(q,v,a)\,\big],\\ (p_1,p_2)=(\pi_q,\pi_v)=-\nabla_zV\quad\text{on optimal paths}. \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(ostroH)} $$

So for a fully actuated plant, the momentum structure of Part I holds word for word, with Ostrogradsky’s momenta in place of $p=\partial L/\partial v$. The non-degeneracy condition of Ostrogradsky’s theory, invertibility of $\partial^2\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q^2$, asks a little more than our strict convexity of $\ell$ in $u$. For $f=u$, and for the manipulator, where the substitution $\tau=M\ddot q+b$ makes $u=\tau-g$ affine in $\ddot q$ with $\partial u/\partial\ddot q=M$, strict convexity makes $p_2=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q$ one-to-one in $\ddot q$, so $\ddot q$ is eliminated uniquely through $p_2$, as in EQ(ostroH). By the chain rule the Hessian is $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2$ for $f=u$ and $M\,(\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2)\,M$ for the manipulator; since $\ell$ is convex in $u$, it is invertible exactly where $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$. Positive definiteness of $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2$ for all $u$ implies strict convexity, but strict convexity does not imply it: with $f=u$, the cost $\ell=\tfrac12|z|^2+\tfrac14|u|^4$ meets every assumption of Section SEC(sec-so-setting), yet $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2=|u|^2I+2uu^\top$ vanishes at $u=0$, and its Hamiltonian $H=\pi_q\cdot v+\tfrac34|\pi_v|^{4/3}-\tfrac12|z|^2$ is not twice differentiable where $\pi_v=0$. The quadratic costs used here have $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$ ($w_u>0$ for the double integrator of Section SEC(sec-so-example), $R\succ0$ for the manipulator of Section SEC(sec-so-manipulator)), and for a smooth $\ell$ this makes $H$ twice differentiable, as the linearization at the rest point below requires. (Outside the assumptions of Section SEC(sec-so-setting), if $f$ is nonlinear in $u$, the curvature of $f$ enters as well, $\partial^2\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q^2=f_u^{-\top}\,\partial_u^2\big[\ell-p_2\cdot f\big]\,f_u^{-1}$ with $p_2$ held fixed, and $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$ no longer suffices.)

Is the Ostrogradsky instability a problem here? Ostrogradsky’s theorem says that the Hamiltonian EQ(ostroH) of a non-degenerate higher-derivative Lagrangian is linear in $p_1$, hence unbounded below. In physics, where this $H$ is the conserved energy, there is then no lowest-energy state; in interacting theories this generically lets positive- and negative-energy degrees of freedom grow together without limit (Woodard, 2015). Three things are different here, and they should be kept apart:
  • $H$ is not our Lyapunov candidate. The Lyapunov function is $V$. It is non-negative because $\ell\ge0$, and $\dot V=-\ell$ by EQ(rule2)–EQ(cert2); neither fact needs $H$ to be bounded. Boundedness of $H$ would matter only if we wanted to use $H$ itself as an energy.
  • The extremal flow is unstable, but that is not specific to higher order. The linearized state–momentum flow at the rest point has its eigenvalues in pairs $\pm\sigma$, so under the usual stabilizability and detectability assumptions exactly half of them lie in the right half-plane. This is already so for the first-order hilltop of Section SEC(sec-scalar), whose $H$ is unbounded below as well. The boundary conditions select the extremal. On an infinite window, finite action selects the stable manifold, on which $H=0$ (Section SEC(sec-so-example)). On a finite window whose endpoint lies in the interior of $\Omega$, transversality $\pi(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(z(t_f)\big)$ puts the window’s path on that manifold exactly when $\nabla W=\nabla V_\infty$ at $z(t_f)$, in particular for $W=V_\infty$ (Sections SEC(sec-riccati) and SEC(sec-so-augmented)). Otherwise, $W=0$ included, the path lies off the manifold, and its conserved Hamiltonian keeps the terminal value $H\big(z(t_f),-\nabla W(z(t_f))\big)=-\mathcal D\big(z(t_f)\big)$, which is in general not zero: by EQ(H2) it is $-\inf_u\ell\big(z(t_f),u\big)<0$ for $W=0$, and by EQ(quadH) it is $\tfrac{c^2-1}{2}\,\kappa\,q(t_f)^2$ for the hilltop with $W=cV_\infty$. The receding loop applies only the start of that path and re-solves, so the departure from the manifold is never followed; and when $\nabla_zV_\vartheta\to\nabla_zV_\infty$ as the window lengthens, as for the hilltop by EQ(finitewindow), the start, whose momentum is $-\nabla_zV_\vartheta$, approaches the manifold.
  • Linearity in $p_1$ is the kinematic constraint. The canonical equation $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p_1=v$ is just $\dot q=v$. What Section SEC(sec-momentum) calls singular, linearity in the decision variable, would be a problem; linearity in a momentum is not.
So the accurate statement is: the closed loop is stable because the remaining action of a positive-definite cost decreases along the selected flow, not because any Hamiltonian is bounded. It is not a claim that the extremal (state–momentum) dynamics are stable: with eigenvalues in $\pm\sigma$ pairs they are never asymptotically stable.

SECNUM Worked example: the double integrator

This example checks EQ(cert2), the sign convention $\pi=-\nabla_zV$ and Ostrogradsky’s momenta EQ(ostro) on a linear, fully actuated plant with an infinite window. Take a unit mass driven by a force, $\dot q=v$, $\dot v=u$ (scalar), with

$$ \begin{gathered} \ell=\tfrac12\big(w_q\,q^2+w_v\,v^2+w_u\,u^2\big),\quad w_q,w_u>0,\ w_v\ge0,\\ H(z,\pi)=\pi_q v+\frac{\pi_v^2}{2w_u}-\tfrac12\big(w_qq^2+w_vv^2\big), \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(dint)} $$

where $H$ follows from EQ(H2): the supremum of $\pi_vu-\tfrac {w_u}2u^2$ is attained at $u^*=\pi_v/w_u$. Note the linearity in $\pi_q$. In matrix form $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, $Q=\mathrm{diag}(w_q,w_v)$, $R=w_u$.

  1. Riccati solution in closed form. Try $V=\tfrac12z^\top Pz$ for the remaining action of the infinite window. The stationary equation in EQ(cert2) becomes the algebraic Riccati equation $A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0$, which splits into three scalar equations, $P_{12}^2=w_u w_q$, $P_{11}=P_{12}P_{22}/w_u$, $P_{22}^2=w_u(w_v+2P_{12})$, whose positive solution is
    $$ \begin{gathered} P_{12}=\sqrt{w_u w_q},\qquad P_{22}=\sqrt{w_u\big(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q}\big)},\\ P_{11}=\sqrt{w_q\big(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q}\big)} . \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(dintP)} $$
  2. Momenta and control. $\pi=-Pz$, i.e. $\pi_q=-(P_{11}q+P_{12}v)$, $\pi_v=-(P_{12}q+P_{22}v)$, and $u^*=\pi_v/w_u=-w_u^{-1}B^\top Pz$, the LQR law. Only the velocity momentum $\pi_v$ sets the force.
  3. Hamilton–Jacobi. Substituting $\pi=-Pz$ into EQ(dint) gives $H(z,-\nabla V)\equiv0$, and $\dot V=z^\top P(A-BK)z=-\ell(z,u^*)$ with $K=w_u^{-1}B^\top P$ (both identities hold symbolically for all $w_q,w_v,w_u$).
  4. The Hamiltonian matrix. Hamilton’s equations EQ(hamilton2) of EQ(dint) are linear, $\tfrac{d}{dt}(z,\pi)=\Xi\,(z,\pi)$ with
    $$ \Xi=\begin{pmatrix}A&BR^{-1}B^\top\\ Q&-A^\top\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(\sigma I-\Xi)=\frac{w_u \sigma^4-w_v \sigma^2+w_q}{w_u}. \tag{EQDEF(dintXi)} $$
    The roots come in pairs $\pm\sigma$. The graph $\{\pi=-Pz\}$ is invariant, $\Xi\binom{I}{-P}=\binom{I}{-P}(A-BK)$, and it is the stable invariant subspace (Laub, 1979; Anderson & Moore, 1990): the closed loop has $\det(\sigma I-A+BK)=\sigma^2+\sqrt{(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q})/w_u}\,\sigma+\sqrt{w_q/w_u}$, whose roots are the left-half-plane roots of EQ(dintXi). For $A=0$, $B=I$ this reduces to the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}$ of Section SEC(sec-riccati).
  5. Ostrogradsky check. With $\hat\ell=\tfrac12(w_qq^2+w_v\dot q^2+w_u\ddot q^2)$, EQ(ostro) gives $p_2=w_u\ddot q$ and $p_1=w_v\dot q-w_u\,q^{(3)}$. Along the closed loop $\ddot q=-(P_{12}q+P_{22}v)/w_u$, so $p_2=\pi_v$ at once; differentiating once more and using $P_{22}^2/w_u=w_v+2P_{12}$ and $P_{11}=P_{12}P_{22}/w_u$ gives $p_1=-(P_{11}q+P_{12}v)=\pi_q$.
  6. Scaled candidates. For $W=c\,V$, $c>0$, using the Riccati equation, $H(z,-c\nabla V)=\tfrac{c-1}{2}\big[c\,w_u^{-1}(B^\top Pz)^2+z^\top Qz\big]$. So the stability condition in EQ(cert2) holds exactly when $c\ge1$ (for $Q\succ0$), as in the scaled candidates of Section SEC(sec-stationary). With $A=0$, $B=I$ the bracket collapses to $(c+1)\,z^\top Qz$ and that formula is recovered.

SECNUM The manipulator: the torque is the velocity momentum

For $M(q)\dot v+b(q,v)=\tau$ with stage cost $\ell=\ell_0(q,v)+\tfrac12\tau^\top R\,\tau$, $R\succ0$, the supremum in EQ(H2) is a concave quadratic in $\tau$. Setting its gradient $M^{-1}\pi_v-R\tau$ to zero ($M$ is symmetric) gives

$$ \tau^*=R^{-1}M(q)^{-1}\pi_v,\qquad H=\pi_q\cdot v-\pi_v\cdot M^{-1}b+\tfrac12\,\pi_v^\top M^{-1}R^{-1}M^{-1}\pi_v-\ell_0(q,v). \tag{EQDEF(manip)} $$

With the plain torque cost $R=I$, the optimal torque is $M^{-1}\pi_v$. In every case the conclusion of Section SEC(sec-so-hj) is visible directly: the torque is dictated by $\pi_v=-\nabla_vV$, how much the remaining action would drop if the joints moved slightly faster. A holding torque against gravity is included by penalizing $\tau-g(q)$ instead of $\tau$, which adds $g(q)$ to $\tau^*$; more generally, centring the cost on any given torque adds that torque. One weight makes the inertia disappear from the optimal torque in EQ(manip), $R\propto M^{-1}$: with $R=T_0M^{-1}$, $T_0>0$ a time constant, $\tau^*=\pi_v/T_0$. It is Gauss’s weight, and it is the bridge to whole-body control (Section SEC(sec-gauss)).

SECNUM Discrete version: multiple shooting and its multipliers

A digital controller holds the control constant over each step and integrates the plant over it. Call the result $z_{j+1}=\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)$ (an exact flow, or a Runge–Kutta step) and let $\ell_d(z_j,u_j)$ be the stage cost of the step. Multiple shooting keeps every node $z_j$ as a separate unknown and imposes the step equations as continuity constraints, the “defects” $z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)=0$ (Bock & Plitt, 1984; Rawlings et al., 2017, Ch. 8). Attaching a multiplier to each defect, with the sign of EQ(augmented), gives the discrete augmented action

$$ \tilde S_d=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\Big[\ell_d(z_j,u_j)+\pi_{j+1}\cdot\big(z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)\big)\Big]+W(z_N). \tag{EQDEF(ms)} $$

This is precisely the Lagrangian function of the multiple-shooting nonlinear program, and its stationarity conditions are the program’s KKT conditions. Differentiate with respect to the interior nodes, the last node and the controls:

$$ \begin{gathered} \pi_j=\partial_z\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}-\nabla_z\ell_d(z_j,u_j),\qquad \pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N),\\ \nabla_u\ell_d(z_j,u_j)=\partial_u\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}. \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(mskkt)} $$

The first equation is the discrete costate recursion, run backwards from the discrete transversality condition (the second, the phase-space form of $p_N=-\nabla W(q_N)$ in Section SEC(sec-stationarity)); the third is the discrete stationarity condition. Differentiating the dynamic-programming recursion $V_N(z)=\min_u\{\ell_d(z,u)+V_{N-1}(\Phi_h(z,u))\}$, $V_0=W$ on $\Omega$, with the envelope theorem, exactly as in Section SEC(sec-stationarity), gives the same recursion for $-\nabla V_N$. Hence $\pi_j=-\nabla V_{N-j}(z_j)$: the defect multipliers are the discrete momenta EQ(dmomentum), minus the gradient of the remaining action with $N-j$ steps to go. If the initial value is imposed as a constraint, with the same sign convention $\pi_0\cdot(z_0-\hat z)$, the node $z_0$ becomes an unknown and its stationarity condition is the first equation of EQ(mskkt) at $j=0$. The multiplier of the initial value is therefore the momentum at the current state, $\pi_0=-\nabla V_N(\hat z)$: the solver returns minus the gradient of the Lyapunov function at the current state for free.

How does this relate to the discrete Legendre transforms EQ(dlegendre)? Define the transition cost on state pairs, $L_d(z,z')=\min_u\{\ell_d(z,u):\Phi_h(z,u)=z'\}$. Where it is differentiable, the multiplier rule and EQ(mskkt) give

$$ \pi_j=-\partial_1L_d(z_j,z_{j+1}),\qquad \pi_{j+1}=\partial_2L_d(z_j,z_{j+1}), \tag{EQDEF(msLd)} $$

which is EQ(dlegendre) with $q\to z$, $p\to\pi$. The caveat is in “where it is differentiable”. With a constant control over the step, the set of reachable $z'$ has dimension $n_u\le n$ inside the $2n$-dimensional state space, so $L_d=+\infty$ off a thin set and $\partial_2L_d$ does not exist. The multipliers of EQ(ms) are what replaces it. With the exact discrete Lagrangian EQ(LdE), where the control may vary continuously within the step, $L_d$ is finite and smooth for a controllable plant and EQ(msLd) holds literally; it is, however, stiff (for the double integrator the inverse controllability Gramian over one step has entries of order $h^{-3}$), so an endpoint pair inconsistent with the plant is very expensive rather than forbidden.

Discrete mechanics has been used for optimal control before: the DMOC approach discretizes the variational description of a mechanical system directly, and the resulting program is equivalent to the one obtained by discretizing Hamilton’s equations of the mechanical system itself, in configuration and mechanical momentum, by a symplectic partitioned Runge–Kutta scheme (Ober-Blöbaum et al., 2011). Here the discrete momenta are instead those of the remaining action, EQ(dmomentum), read off the multipliers of a standard multiple-shooting program.

Inequalities. Input and state constraints $\chi(z_j,u_j)\le0$ and the terminal region $W(z_N)\le\bar W$ enter EQ(ms) with multipliers $\mu_j\ge0$, $\mu_N\ge0$ and complementarity, and EQ(mskkt) becomes

$$ \begin{gathered} \pi_j=\partial_z\Phi_h^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}-\nabla_z\ell_d-\nabla_z\chi^\top\mu_j,\qquad \nabla_u\ell_d+\nabla_u\chi^\top\mu_j=\partial_u\Phi_h^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1},\\ \pi_N=-(1+\mu_N)\,\nabla W(z_N). \end{gathered} \tag{EQDEF(msineq)} $$

Active state constraints change the momenta by their reactions; active input constraints change only the stationarity condition; an active terminal constraint scales the arrival momentum, as in Section SEC(sec-kkt): EQ(msineq) is the multiple-shooting form of EQ(kkt).

Reading momenta off a multiple-shooting solver
  1. Solve the window problem as an NLP in the nodes $z_1,\dots,z_N$ and controls $u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}$, with defect constraints $z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)=0$.
  2. Read the defect multipliers. If the solver forms its Lagrangian as cost $+\sum_j\psi_{j+1}^{\top}\big(z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)\big)$, then $\psi_{j+1}=\pi_{j+1}$. If it writes the defects as $\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)-z_{j+1}$, or subtracts the multiplier term, the sign flips: $\psi_{j+1}=-\pi_{j+1}=\nabla V_{N-j-1}(z_{j+1})$ is the minimum-principle costate $\eta$. Multipliers of inequalities carry the signs of EQ(msineq) in the same convention.
  3. Check the convention once at the last node: $\pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N)$.
  4. Use $-\pi_j$ as $\nabla V_{N-j}(z_j)$, e.g. to monitor the Hamilton–Jacobi residual or the stability condition EQ(cert2) along the predicted path.
The discrete version was checked on the double integrator of Section SEC(sec-so-example) with step $h=0.2$, $N=10$, zero-order hold and the rectangle-rule stage cost $h\,\ell$. The KKT multipliers of the multiple-shooting problem EQ(ms) satisfy $\pi_j=-P_{N-j}z_j$, with $P_N$ the weight of $V_N=\tfrac12z^\top P_Nz$ from the backward discrete Riccati recursion started at $P_0=\nabla^2W$, $\pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N)$ and EQ(mskkt) to machine precision, for $W$ equal to the discrete Riccati weight, to the continuous one, and to three times the former. With the exact discrete Lagrangian and $W$ the continuous Riccati weight, the one-step rule of Section SEC(sec-exactness) reproduces $z'=e^{(A-BK)h}z$ and $V_1=W$, now in phase space.
