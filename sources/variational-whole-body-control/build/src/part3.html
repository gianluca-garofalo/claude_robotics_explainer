SECNUM Extremals: Solving the Hamilton–Jacobi Equation

Section SEC(sec-hj) derived the Hamilton–Jacobi equation; this section solves it. The tool is the method of characteristics, which follows the equation along the optimal path. It returns Hamilton’s equations, so the optimal paths are the extremals of classical mechanics and the momentum EQ(pdef) is their canonical momentum.

SECNUM Characteristics: the momentum along the optimal path

Fix a window $\vartheta_0$ and start the optimal path from $q$ at $t=0$, so that $t_f=\vartheta_0$; along it the time to go is $\vartheta=t_f-t$. Its velocity is the minimizer in EQ(hjraw), which by the envelope theorem applied to EQ(legendre) is $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$ at $p=-\nabla V$. Assume $V$ twice continuously differentiable along the path.

  1. Differentiate EQ(hj) with respect to $q$: $\partial_\vartheta\nabla V=-H_q+\nabla^2V\,H_p$, with $H_q$ and $H_p$ evaluated at $(q,-\nabla V)$.
  2. Along the path the window shrinks, so $\tfrac{d}{dt}\nabla V=\nabla^2V\,\dot q-\partial_\vartheta\nabla V=\nabla^2V\,H_p+H_q-\nabla^2V\,H_p=H_q$.
  3. Hence $p(t)=-\nabla V\big(q(t),t_f-t\big)$ obeys Hamilton’s equations, and at the end of the window, where the time to go is zero and the remaining action is $V_0=W$, it takes the value fixed by the boundary term:
$$ \dot q=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad \dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\qquad p(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(q(t_f)\big). \tag{EQDEF(hamilton)} $$

The last condition is the transversality condition of a Bolza problem (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963, §§6, 14). Step 3 uses $V_0=W$ near $q(t_f)$, so the condition needs $q(t_f)$ in the interior of $\Omega$; at an endpoint on the boundary of $\Omega$ the arrival momentum also carries the reaction of the terminal constraint (see the discussion of the principal function below). Without velocity constraints, eliminating $p$ through $\partial L/\partial v=p$ turns the first two equations into the Euler–Lagrange equation $\tfrac{d}{dt}\,\partial L/\partial\dot q=\partial L/\partial q$: the optimal paths are the extremals of the action, and their canonical momentum is minus the gradient of the remaining action. Three consequences follow.

  • Descent along the optimal path. Along the path computed at one instant, $\tfrac{d}{dt}V=\nabla V\cdot\dot q-\partial_\vartheta V=-p\cdot\dot q+H=-L$, using $H=p\cdot\dot q-L$ at the maximizer. This holds for every window. It is not EQ(rhcrate): along the computed path the window shrinks, whereas the receding loop keeps $\vartheta$ fixed and re-solves, and its rate differs from $-L$ by $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta$, the monotonicity term that the certificate EQ(cert) controls (Section SEC(sec-allwindows)).
  • Why $H=0$ for the infinite window. $L$ has no explicit time dependence, so $H$ is conserved along EQ(hamilton): $\tfrac{d}{dt}H={H_q\cdot\dot q}+{H_p\cdot\dot p}=0$. On the path that reaches the rest point, $q\to0$ and $p\to0$, and $H(0,0)=-\min_vL(0,v)=0$. So $H=0$ all along, which is EQ(stationary) seen from the path.
  • One rule for every window. The momentum is minus the gradient of the remaining action of the problem at hand:

The link to textbook mechanics. Hamilton’s principal function $\mathcal S(q_i,t_i;q_f,t_f)$ is the integral of $L$ along the extremal that joins two endpoints, without the boundary term. Textbook mechanics reads it as a function of the final point, with $\partial\mathcal S/\partial q_{\text{final}}=p$ (Arnold, 1989, §46); its differential is $d\mathcal S=p_f\,dq_f-H_f\,dt_f-p_i\,dq_i+H_i\,dt_i$. The remaining action reads it as a function of the initial point, adds the boundary term and minimizes over the endpoint in $\Omega$:

$$ V_{t_f-t}(q)=\min_{q_f\in\Omega}\big[\mathcal S(q,t;q_f,t_f)+W(q_f)\big]. \tag{EQDEF(principal)} $$

In $V$ the final time is fixed and the endpoint is free in $\Omega$. At an optimal endpoint in the interior of $\Omega$ the transversality condition in EQ(hamilton) makes the $dq_f$ terms of $d\mathcal S$ and $dW$ cancel. At an optimal endpoint on the boundary of $\Omega=\{\chi\le0\}$ the first-order condition of EQ(principal) is instead $p_f=-\nabla W(q_f)-\mu\nabla\chi(q_f)$ with $\mu\ge0$: the arrival momentum carries the reaction of the terminal constraint ($p_f=-(1+\mu)\nabla W$ for a sublevel set of $W$, as in Section SEC(sec-kkt)). The $dq_f$ terms still drop out, because while the constraint stays active the optimal endpoint moves along the boundary, $\nabla\chi\cdot dq_f=0$. Either way this leaves $dV=-p_i\,dq_i+H_i\,dt_i$: $\nabla V=-p$ and, since the time to go is $\vartheta=t_f-t_i$, $\partial_\vartheta V=-H$ at the initial point, which are EQ(pdef) and EQ(hj) again. Every sign difference between the two literatures comes from that one switch, from the final to the initial point:

SECNUM Worked example: the hilltop

This example solves the stationary equation EQ(stationary) for the running example EQ(example), the ideal boundary term $V_\infty$ (written $V$ here), and checks the three predictions of Section SEC(sec-characteristics): $H=0$ on the finite-action extremal, $p=-V'$ along it, and, with $W=V$ in the rule EQ(rule), $\dot V=-L$ exactly. Each step is labelled with what it establishes. Write the Lagrangian with $\dot q$ for $v$, $L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2+\tfrac12\kappa q^2$.

  1. Read $L$ as $\mathcal T-U$. $\mathcal T=\tfrac12m\dot q^2$ and $U(q)=-\tfrac12\kappa \,q^2$. The minimizing paths obey Newton’s law for a particle on an inverted parabolic potential, a hilltop at the origin. This $L$ is the variational Lagrangian, the cost we chose; the hilltop belongs to the variational problem, not to any robot (Section SEC(sec-equilibria) returns to the difference).
  2. Hamilton’s equations: the candidate extremals. With $H=p^2/(2m)-\tfrac12\kappa q^2$ (Section SEC(sec-momentum)), EQ(hamilton) reads ${\dot q=p/m}$, ${\dot p=\kappa q}$, i.e. the Euler–Lagrange equation $m\ddot q=\kappa q$, so $q(t)\sim e^{\pm\omega t}$. The Hamiltonian matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1/m\\\kappa&0\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm\omega$: the origin is a saddle in the $(q,p)$ plane.
  3. Jacobi: a minimum between fixed endpoints, not just stationarity. The Jacobi equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation linearized along the extremal. A later point where a nonzero solution $y$ with $y(0)=0$ vanishes again is called conjugate; with $\partial^2L/\partial v^2>0$ and no conjugate point in $(0,t_f]$, the extremal is a weak minimum: no path with the same two endpoints that stays close to it in both position and velocity has a smaller action (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963, §28). Here the Jacobi equation is $\ddot y=\omega^2y$ along every extremal, and $y(0)=0$ gives $y\propto\sinh\omega t$, which never vanishes again: no extremal has a conjugate point, on any interval. Because this $L$ is jointly convex in $(q,v)$, the minimum is even global among paths with the same endpoints. For an extremal $q$ with momentum $p=\partial L/\partial v$ and any path $q+\delta q$, convexity and the Euler–Lagrange equation $\dot p=\partial L/\partial q$ give $L(q+\delta q,\dot q+\delta\dot q)-L(q,\dot q)\ge\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\delta q+p\cdot\delta\dot q=\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(p\cdot\delta q\big)$; integrated, the action of $q+\delta q$ exceeds that of $q$ by at least $\big[p\cdot\delta q\big]_0^{t_f}$, which is zero when $\delta q(0)=\delta q(t_f)=0$. So every extremal is the least-action path between its own endpoints, the caveat in the table of Section SEC(sec-characteristics). Both results hold for every extremal, the growing ones $\propto e^{+\omega t}$ included, so they do not select the path of the infinite window: finite action selects it (step 4), and the verification theorem of Section SEC(sec-stationary) makes its action the least (step 5). For an ordinary oscillator, $U=+\tfrac12\kappa q^2$, the Jacobi field is $\sin\omega t$, a conjugate point appears at $t=\pi/\omega$, and the action is only stationary on longer intervals.
  4. Finite action: it selects the $H=0$ extremal. Of all extremals only $q(t)=q_0e^{-\omega t}$ has finite action. Its momentum is $p=m\dot q=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q$ and its energy is $$H=\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac12\kappa q^2=0,$$ the separatrix, i.e. the stable manifold of the saddle (Fig. 3).
  5. The remaining action generates it: $p=-V'$. With $p=-V'$, the stationary equation EQ(stationary) reads $V'^2/(2m)=\tfrac12\kappa q^2$, and its solution with $V\ge0$, $V(0)=0$ is $$V(q)=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q^2,\qquad p=-V'(q)=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q,\tag{EQDEF(hilltopV)}$$ the momentum of step 4: the finite-action extremal is the graph of $p=-V'(q)$. It is the Riccati solution $P=\sqrt{m\kappa}$ of Section SEC(sec-stationary). Check against direct integration (along $q=q_0e^{-\omega t}$, both terms of $L$ are equal): $$\int_0^\infty L\,dt=\int_0^\infty \kappa \,q_0^2e^{-2\omega t}\,dt=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q_0^2\ \checkmark$$
  6. Velocity law: the rule EQ(rule) with $W=V$ gives $\dot V=-L$. The minimizer of $L+V'v$ solves $mv=-V'$, so $v^*=-\omega q$, which is $\dot q=p/m$ again. Along it $\dot V=V'v^*=-\kappa q^2=-L(q,v^*)$, as EQ(wdot) with $H=0$ predicts, and $q$ decays exponentially to the rest point.
Physical intuition: balancing on the hilltop. Picture a ball that must come to rest on top of an upside-down parabola. Its energy $H$ is conserved. Push it slightly too hard ($H>0$) and it rolls over the top and off the other side. Push it too softly ($H<0$) and it stops short and rolls back. Only with exactly $H=0$ does it creep up and arrive, as $t\to\infty$, with a speed $\omega|q|$ proportional to the distance still to go. That knife-edge set of initial momenta is the stable manifold. The finite-action requirement selects it, and the remaining action $V$ is its generating function.

Finite windows and scaled candidates. With a quadratic boundary term and no terminal constraint ($\Omega=\mathbb R$) the time-dependent equation EQ(hj) can be solved for every window. Insert $V_\vartheta=\tfrac12P_\vartheta\,q^2$: by EQ(quadH), $\tfrac12(\partial_\vartheta P_\vartheta)\,q^2=-\big(\tfrac{P_\vartheta^2}{2m}-\tfrac\kappa2\big)q^2$, the Riccati differential equation $\partial_\vartheta P_\vartheta=\kappa-P_\vartheta^2/m$, started at the weight $P_0$ of $W$. For the scaled candidate $W=cV$ of Section SEC(sec-stationary), $P_0=c\sqrt{m\kappa}$. Divided by $\sqrt{m\kappa}$, the equation reads $\partial_\vartheta\big(P_\vartheta/\sqrt{m\kappa}\big)=\omega\big(1-P_\vartheta^2/(m\kappa)\big)$, the differential equation of $\tanh$, and its solution from the value $c$ is

$$ V_\vartheta(q)=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\;\frac{c+\tanh\omega\vartheta}{1+c\tanh\omega\vartheta}\;q^2 . \tag{EQDEF(finitewindow)} $$
  • $c=0$ (no boundary term): $V_\vartheta=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,\tanh(\omega\vartheta)\,q^2$, which grows with $\vartheta$ and tends to EQ(hilltopV) as $\vartheta\to\infty$;
  • $c=1$: $V_\vartheta=V$ for every $\vartheta$: the boundary term has already supplied the infinite tail. With a terminal interval $\Omega=[-r,r]$ this holds only where the path $q\,e^{-\omega t}$ of the rule (step 6) ends in $\Omega$, i.e. for $|q|\le r\,e^{\omega\vartheta}$ (Section SEC(sec-stationary)); farther out the optimal endpoint is pushed to $\pm r$ and $V_\vartheta>V$. For $m=\kappa=1$, $r=\tfrac12$ and $\vartheta=0.1$, the action of the extremal from $q=1$ to $r$ in time $\vartheta$, plus $V(r)$, gives $V_\vartheta(1)=1.404$ against $V(1)=0.5$, and its initial velocity gives a first move $p/m=\dot q(0)=-5.04$ against the rule’s $-\omega q=-1$;
  • $c>1$: $V_\vartheta$ decreases with $\vartheta$ towards $V$. This is EQ(ctsmonotone), and by EQ(rhcrate) every window length descends at least at the rate $L$, as the certificate $H(q,-W')=\tfrac{c^2-1}{2}\,\kappa q^2>0$ promised;
  • $c<1$: $V_\vartheta$ increases with $\vartheta$ for every window: the failure of EQ(cert) at $\vartheta=0$ propagates, and the receding loop descends more slowly than $L$ although it converges. Along it $v^*_\vartheta=-P_\vartheta q/m$, so $\dot V_\vartheta=-P_\vartheta^2q^2/m=-\beta_\vartheta\,L(q,v^*_\vartheta)$ with $\beta_\vartheta=2P_\vartheta^2/(P_\vartheta^2+m\kappa)<1$. For $0<c<1$, $\beta_\vartheta\ge2c^2/(1+c^2)$ for every window (for $c=0$, $\beta_\vartheta>0$ once $\vartheta>0$), so $V_\vartheta$ remains a Lyapunov function of the loop.

SECNUM Quadratic Lagrangians: the stable manifold and the boundary term

The structure of the hilltop is general. For $L=\tfrac12q^\top Qq+\tfrac12v^\top Rv$, Hamilton’s equations EQ(hamilton) are linear, $\tfrac{d}{dt}(q,p)=\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}(q,p)$, with eigenvalues in pairs $\pm\sigma$. The Riccati solution $P$ ($PR^{-1}P=Q$) is such that the graph $p=-Pq$ is the stable invariant subspace of this Hamiltonian matrix: $\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{I}{-P}=\binom{I}{-P}(-R^{-1}P)$, and $-R^{-1}P$ is the stable closed loop. This is how the Riccati equation is solved numerically (Laub, 1979). The remaining action $\tfrac12q^\top Pq$ is the generating function of that Lagrangian subspace, and EQ(stationary) is the time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation at zero energy.

What the boundary term does. The transversality condition in EQ(hamilton) prescribes the momentum with which a finite path must arrive. In this picture $W$ tells the finite path how to join the infinite-time stable manifold. If $W=V_\infty$ exactly and no constraint is active along the path, the finite-window path lies on the manifold and the window length is irrelevant ($c=1$ in EQ(finitewindow)). The terminal region $\Omega$ restricts where the endpoint may lie.

SECNUM Back to Steps: Discretization and Exactness

Section SEC(sec-proof) proved descent for any discrete Lagrangian; Sections SEC(sec-hj) and SEC(sec-extremals) solved the continuous problem. This section brings the two together: which discrete Lagrangian reproduces the continuous problem, what momentum a computed path carries, and when one step is already exact.

SECNUM The exact discrete Lagrangian and discrete momenta

The continuous problem over one step defines a distinguished discrete Lagrangian, the exact discrete Lagrangian:

$$ L_d^{E}(q,q')=\min\Big\{\int_0^{h}L\big(q(t),\dot q(t)\big)\,dt\ :\ q(0)=q,\ q(h)=q'\Big\}. \tag{EQDEF(LdE)} $$

By Section SEC(sec-characteristics) the minimizer is an extremal (for an $L$ jointly convex in $(q,v)$, the extremal joining the two points), so $L_d^E(q,q')=\mathcal S(q,0;q',h)$, and its derivatives are read off $d\mathcal S$: $\partial_1L_d^E=-p$ at the start of the step and $\partial_2L_d^E=p$ at its end. For any other $L_d$ these relations define the discrete momenta, through the discrete Legendre transforms (Marsden & West, 2001, who write $D_1$, $D_2$ for $\partial_1$, $\partial_2$, the derivatives in the first and second argument):

$$ p_j=-\partial_1L_d(q_j,q_{j+1}),\qquad p_{j+1}=\partial_2L_d(q_j,q_{j+1}). \tag{EQDEF(dlegendre)} $$

For the example. The extremal joining $q$ to $q'$ in time $h$ is $q(t)=\big[q\sinh\omega(h-t)+q'\sinh\omega t\big]/\sinh\omega h$ (step 2 of Section SEC(sec-scalar)). Along any extremal of EQ(example), $L=\tfrac12\big(m\dot q^2+\kappa q^2\big)=\tfrac12\tfrac{d}{dt}(mq\dot q)$ because $m\ddot q=\kappa q$, so the action of the step is $\tfrac12\big[mq\dot q\big]_0^h$. Inserting the extremal and $m\omega=\sqrt{m\kappa}$,

$$ L_d^E(q,q')=\frac{\sqrt{m\kappa}}{2\sinh\omega h}\Big[(q^2+q'^2)\cosh\omega h-2qq'\Big]. \tag{EQDEF(LdEexample)} $$

The rectangle rule EQ(rect) is instead a one-point quadrature of the same integral.

Why the distinction matters. With $L_d^E$ the discrete problem is the continuous one, sampled exactly; any other $L_d$ (rectangle rule, midpoint rule, …) is an approximation whose error vanishes as $h\to0$ but is not zero for the step actually used. Section SEC(sec-exactness) shows that the “one step is exact” statement depends on this choice, though a nonzero error need not break it: with the continuous-time Riccati weight it holds for $L_d^E$ and for the midpoint rule, but not for the rectangle rule. Section SEC(sec-numeric) shows numerically what goes wrong with the rectangle rule.

SECNUM The momentum of a computed path

Differentiate the recursion EQ(bellman) at its minimizer $q'=q^+$, assuming no constraint is active there and the functions are differentiable. Stationarity in $q'$ gives, by EQ(dlegendre),

$$ \partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla V_{N-1}(q^+)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^+=-\nabla V_{N-1}(q^+), \tag{EQDEF(dstationarity)} $$

and the envelope theorem gives $\nabla V_N(q)=\partial_1L_d(q,q^+)=-p$ at the start. Applied at every node of a computed path, where node $j$ has $N-j$ steps to go, this is the discrete form of EQ(pdef):

$$ p_j=-\nabla V_{N-j}(q_j),\qquad j=0,\dots,N. \tag{EQDEF(dmomentum)} $$

Matching the two expressions for $p_j$ at an interior node, from the step before and the step after, is the discrete Euler–Lagrange equation $\partial_2L_d(q_{j-1},q_j)+\partial_1L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})=0$. The last node lies in $\Omega$, where $V_0=W$, so $p_N=-\nabla W(q_N)$: the final momentum is fixed by the slope of the boundary term, the discrete form of the transversality condition in EQ(hamilton). For the one-step window EQ(onestep) it reads $\partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)=0$. Section SEC(sec-kkt) adds active constraints, and Section SEC(sec-so-discrete) finds EQ(dmomentum) again as the multipliers of a multiple-shooting solver.

SECNUM Exactness is a fixed point

When does a short window already give the infinite-time optimum? By EQ(bellman), $V_N=\mathcal B^NV_0$. Without a terminal constraint ($\Omega=\mathbb R^n$, so $V_0=W$), $V_N=W$ for every $N$ if and only if $\mathcal BW=W$ (if: by induction; only if: take $N=1$). With a terminal region, $V_0\ge W$, so $\mathcal BW=W$ and the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$ give $V_N\ge W$, while a chain of $N$ minimizing steps of $\mathcal BW$ costs exactly $W(q)$, by telescoping. Hence $V_N=W$ exactly at the points from which such a chain ends in $\Omega$ (a path that attains $W(q)$ must be one, because each step $q_j\to q_{j+1}$ costs at least $W(q_j)-W(q_{j+1})$). If the minimizer keeps $\Omega$ invariant (for instance $\Omega$ a sublevel set of $W$, since $W(q^+)=W(q)-L_d(q,q^+)\le W(q)$), these points include $\Omega$ and form a set that grows with $N$. Elsewhere the constraint $q_N\in\Omega$ binds and $V_N>W$. For example, with $L_d^E$, $m=\kappa=1$, $h=0.1$, its fixed point $W=\tfrac12q^2$ (first case below) and $\Omega=[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$: $V_1(1)=1.404$, and $V_N(1)=W(1)=0.5$ only from $N=7$, the first $N$ with $e^{-Nh}\le\tfrac12$. Where the terminal constraint is inactive, one step is exact exactly when $W$ is a fixed point of the Bellman operator of the chosen $L_d$, and a fixed point for one discrete Lagrangian need not be one for another.

  • Exact discrete Lagrangian. With $L_d^E$, the operator $\mathcal B$ is the continuous problem over one step: by EQ(LdE), $\min_{q'}\{L_d^E(q,q')+W(q')\}$ minimizes $\int_0^hL\,dt+W\big(q(h)\big)$ over all paths from $q$. The principle of optimality in continuous time therefore makes $V_\infty$ a fixed point, and one step is exact with $W=V_\infty$. For a quadratic Lagrangian, with $W=\tfrac12q^\top Pq$, $P$ the Riccati solution of Section SEC(sec-stationary), and no constraint active: $V_1=W$, the step is $q^+=e^{-hR^{-1}P}q$, the exact sample of the flow of the LQR law $v=-R^{-1}Pq$, and EQ(terminal) holds with equality along that flow.
  • Midpoint rule. The midpoint rule $L_d(q,q')=h\,L\big(q_{1/2},\tfrac{q'-q}{h}\big)$, $q_{1/2}=\tfrac12(q+q')$, is only an approximation of $L_d^E$, yet for a quadratic Lagrangian $\tfrac12q^\top Pq$ is a fixed point of its Bellman operator too. Write $v=(q'-q)/h$. For a quadratic $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$ the increment over the step is exactly the slope at the midpoint times the step, $W(q')-W(q)=\tfrac12(q'-q)^\top X(q'+q)=h\,\nabla W(q_{1/2})\cdot v$, so the midpoint analogue of the expansion EQ(expansion) holds with no remainder: $$L_d(q,q')+W(q')=W(q)+h\big[L(q_{1/2},v)+\nabla W(q_{1/2})\cdot v\big].$$ By EQ(legendre) and EQ(quadH) the bracket is at least $-H(q_{1/2},-Xq_{1/2})$, with equality only at $v=-R^{-1}Xq_{1/2}$. For $X=P$ this bound is $0$ by EQ(stationary), whatever $q'$ is, and the step $q^+=\big(I+\tfrac h2R^{-1}P\big)^{-1}\big(I-\tfrac h2R^{-1}P\big)q$ attains it (the inverse exists: $R^{-1}P$ is similar to $R^{-1/2}PR^{-1/2}\succ0$, so its eigenvalues are positive). Hence, with no constraint active, $V_1=W$ for every $h$ and EQ(terminal) holds with equality along the step; but the step is this Cayley map, the implicit-midpoint step of the LQR flow, and not its exact sample $e^{-hR^{-1}P}q$.
  • Rectangle rule. The rectangle rule EQ(rect) for a quadratic Lagrangian, $L_d=\tfrac1{2h}(q'-q)^\top R\,(q'-q)+\tfrac h2q^\top Qq$, maps quadratics to quadratics. With $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$, the stationarity condition $\tfrac Rh(q^+-q)+Xq^+=0$ gives $q^+=\big(\tfrac Rh+X\big)^{-1}\tfrac Rh\,q$, and substituting back, $$\mathcal B\big(\tfrac12q^\top Xq\big)=\tfrac12q^\top \mathcal R_h(X)\,q,\qquad \mathcal R_h(X)=hQ+\tfrac Rh-\tfrac Rh\Big(\tfrac Rh+X\Big)^{-1}\tfrac Rh ,\tag{EQDEF(riccatimap)}$$ the one-step Riccati map. The rectangle rule is the discrete LQ problem $q'=q+hv$ with stage cost $\tfrac h2(q^\top Qq+v^\top Rv)$, so the fixed point of $\mathcal R_h$ is the solution $P_d$ of its discrete algebraic Riccati equation (Anderson & Moore, 1990), and $P_d\neq P$.

The threshold. By EQ(V1V0) and its converse, $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$ satisfies EQ(terminal) if and only if $\mathcal R_h(X)\preceq X$. The map $\mathcal R_h$ is monotone ($X_a\preceq X_b$ implies $\mathcal R_h(X_a)\preceq\mathcal R_h(X_b)$, the matrix form of the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$), so $\mathcal R_h(X)\preceq X$ gives $\mathcal R_h^{k+1}(X)\preceq\mathcal R_h^k(X)$ for every $k$. The sequence decreases and is bounded below by $hQ$, so it converges to a fixed point, and $P_d$ is the only positive-definite one. Hence $P_d=\lim_k\mathcal R_h^k(X)\preceq X$, and the scaled weight $W=\tfrac{c}{2}q^\top Pq$ can satisfy EQ(terminal) only if $cP\succeq P_d$, i.e. $c\ge\lambda_{\max}(P^{-1}P_d)$. This is necessary in general. For $\dot q=v$ with the rectangle rule it is also sufficient, in any dimension: in the coordinates $R^{1/2}q$ the matrices $P$, $P_d$ and $\mathcal R_h(cP)$ are all functions of $R^{-1/2}QR^{-1/2}$, so the condition splits into scalar ones. For general plants and discretizations only necessity holds. In the scalar case ($Q=\kappa$, $R=m$) $\mathcal R_h(X)=h\kappa+{mX/(m+hX)}$, whose fixed point solves $X^2-h\kappa X-m\kappa=0$:

$$ P_d=\tfrac12\Big(h\kappa+\sqrt{h^2\kappa^2+4m\kappa}\Big), \tag{EQDEF(Pd)} $$

that is $P_d=\tfrac12\big(h+\sqrt{h^2+4}\big)$ for $m=\kappa=1$, against $P=\sqrt{m\kappa}=1$. There $\mathcal R_h(X)-X$ decreases in $X$, so $c\ge P_d/P$ is also enough: for $m=\kappa=1$ the threshold $P_d/P$ is $1.05125$ at $h=0.1$ and $1.28078$ at $h=0.5$. (With an active constraint the fixed point is the remaining action of the constrained problem; Section SEC(sec-speedlimit).)

Practical consequence. Pairing a rectangle-rule $L_d$ with the continuous-time Riccati weight is a natural, and wrong, shortcut: the state still converges, but the Lyapunov certificate EQ(descent) fails, at every step of the example below.

SECNUM Window and tail

Section SEC(sec-onestep) said that the window length moves the cut between the computed part of the remaining action (the window) and the estimated part (the tail $W$), while the certificate stays the same. With an exact pair, $W=V_\infty$ for the discrete problem and no active constraint, the fixed-point property gives $V_N=V_\infty$ for every $N$: only the cut moves. With an inexact pair the total itself changes with $N$. If the pair passes the one-step test EQ(terminal), the total can only fall, by EQ(monotone). If it fails EQ(terminal), then $V_1>W$ somewhere on $\Omega$ (Section SEC(sec-terminal)), and EQ(monotone) fails already at $N=1$. The textbook weight of Section SEC(sec-numeric) is such a pair, with no terminal region: by EQ(riccatimap), $V_1=\tfrac12\mathcal R_h(1)\,q^2$ with $\mathcal R_h(1)=1+h^2/(1+h)>1$, so $V_1\ge V_0$ everywhere, and the induction of Section SEC(sec-monotone) with the inequality reversed gives $V_{N+1}=\mathcal BV_N\ge\mathcal BV_{N-1}=V_N$: the total rises with $N$, the discrete mirror of $c<1$ in EQ(finitewindow). A failed EQ(terminal) does not fix the direction in general, because $V_1-V_0$ need not have one sign: with a terminal region, $V_1<V_0=+\infty$ on $\mathcal X_1\setminus\Omega$, and in the matrix case of Section SEC(sec-exactness) a $c$ between the scalar thresholds of two eigendirections of $R^{-1/2}QR^{-1/2}$ makes $V_N$ fall in one of them and rise in the other.
