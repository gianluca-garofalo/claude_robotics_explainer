From the Remaining Action to the Forces on a Task

A variational-mechanics view of receding-window stabilization and hierarchical passivity-based whole-body control:
Lagrangians, momenta, Gauss’s principle and the multipliers of tasks
November 2026
Abstract. Model predictive control (MPC) stabilizes a system by repeatedly solving a finite-time optimal control problem and applying only its first move; the classical stability argument uses the optimal value function as a Lyapunov function, made to decrease by a terminal equality constraint (Keerthi & Gilbert, 1988) or, as here, by a terminal cost and a terminal set (Chen & Allgöwer, 1998; Mayne et al., 2000). Whole-body control shares one torque vector among prioritized tasks, one instant at a time. This document reads both in the language of analytical mechanics. It builds the receding-window results from one starting point, Bellman’s principle of optimality; the whole-body results add Gauss’s principle, with tasks as constraints, and a block factorization. The stage cost becomes a Lagrangian and the value function the “remaining action” (Hamilton’s principal function plus the boundary term). For a window of $N$ steps the principle of optimality is a monotone recursion, so a boundary term that passes a one-step test makes the remaining action fall, by at least the action just spent, for every window length. Letting the step shrink turns the recursion into the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, its minimization into a Legendre transform whose variable is the momentum $p=-\nabla V$, and the one-step test into the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality $H(q,-\nabla W)\ge0$; its characteristics are Hamilton’s equations. Discretizing again shows, with numbers, that exactness of one step is a fixed-point property of the pair of discrete Lagrangian and boundary term: the continuous-time Riccati boundary term makes one step exact with the exact discrete Lagrangian (Marsden & West, 2001) and also with the midpoint rule, but not with the rectangle rule, which needs its own discrete-Riccati boundary term. For mechanical plants the Lagrange multipliers carry the physics. The multipliers of the equations of motion are the momenta (Ostrogradsky’s momenta, the defect multipliers of multiple shooting); with Gauss’s weight the infinitesimal-window torque is the velocity momentum per unit time; and a task imposed as a constraint in Gauss’s principle has the operational-space force as its multiplier (Bruyninckx & Khatib, 2000). Three distinctions recur. Argmin versus certificate: a two-link arm whose infinitesimal-window rule, for a cost on the gravity-compensated torque $u=\tau-g$, is PD control with gravity compensation, but whose classical Slotine–Li gain matching fails the certificate, and the mechanical energy, which certifies only damping, show that a certificate belongs to the pair of Lagrangian and boundary term. Impressed versus constraint forces: passivity-based control with natural inertia versus operational-space control. Invariant versus attributed: the window length leaves the certificate unchanged and, for a given torque, the priority order leaves the total task power unchanged, while the split of the remaining action between window and tail, and of the power among priority levels, depends on the choice. For hierarchies, one block factorization of the stacked mobility, a Gram–Schmidt process in the $M^{-1}$ metric, yields the dynamically consistent extended Jacobians, the decoupled inertias and the dictionary $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ between decoupled task forces and stacked multipliers, with consequences for energy tanks at active-set switches. Inequalities (speed and torque limits, terminal regions, joint limits; contacts in outline) keep this structure for each active set, or contact mode without frictional sliding, and a feasible stack: multipliers acquire signs and active sets switch. Frictional sliding, impacts and friction-cone switching are not treated, and infeasible hierarchies only in outline (Section 14.4). An active terminal constraint acts as an inflated boundary term; the sign of a unilateral multiplier survives neither a change of basis nor a pairing with velocity; and an activation anywhere in a hierarchy, in the constraint view, re-attributes forces and power at and below its level while the torque and the total power are unchanged, whereas with prescribed (impressed) forces both jump in general unless the forces are re-mapped through $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ at the switch. Every number in the document is printed by a script; Section 19 lists them and says which are run by hand.
Part I · Receding windows as mechanics

1. Setting and Road Map

1.1 The idea in one picture

A receding-window scheme looks ahead over a window of $N$ steps, finds the cheapest admissible path that ends inside a small terminal region $\Omega$ around the rest point, pays a boundary term $W$ for wherever that path ends, and then follows only the first segment. At the next instant it does the same again from the new point (Fig. 1).

The first question this document answers is why this converges. The answer will be that the remaining action, the least action still to be paid from the current point, can only go down, and by at least the action of the step just taken, so that it is a Lyapunov function. The argument needs a single principle, and every quantity it produces on the way (momentum, Legendre transform, Hamilton–Jacobi equation) has a name in analytical mechanics. The second question is what the same quantities say about whole-body control, where one torque vector has to serve several tasks at once. There the Lagrange multipliers of the tasks will turn out to be the forces acting on them, and the priority order of a hierarchy a choice of basis for those forces.

1.2 The variational problem

Part I is written for a first-order (“kinematic”) plant, in which the velocity is the decision variable:

$$ \dot q=v,\qquad v\in\mathcal V(q),\qquad q\in\mathbb R^n . \tag{1} $$

The set of admissible velocities $\mathcal V(q)$ is closed; it encodes unilateral constraints such as a speed limit, and it is all of $\mathbb R^n$ when there are none. This keeps the mechanics visible with the least notation. A robot is a second-order plant, in which the decision variable is a torque that sets an acceleration; Part II redoes the construction for that case.

A path is scored by its action: the integral of a variational Lagrangian $L(q,v)$ plus a boundary term $W$ paid where the path ends, which must be inside the terminal region $\Omega$:

$$ S=\int_t^{t_f}L\big(q,\dot q\big)\,dt'+W\big(q(t_f)\big),\qquad q(t_f)\in\Omega . \tag{2} $$

In MPC terms $L$ is the stage cost, $S$ the cost over the horizon, $W$ the terminal cost and $\Omega$ the terminal set. The variational Lagrangian is a cost we choose; it is not the Lagrangian of the plant, and the two will be kept apart throughout. A function $\alpha\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is of class $\mathcal K$ if it is continuous, strictly increasing and $\alpha(0)=0$; it is of class $\mathcal K_\infty$ if, in addition, $\alpha(\rho)\to\infty$ as $\rho\to\infty$. These are the comparison functions of Lyapunov theory (Rawlings et al., 2017, App. B). The standing assumptions are:

Standing assumptions.
  • $L(0,0)=0$, $L(q,v)\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$ with $\alpha_L\in\mathcal K_\infty$; $L$ is continuously differentiable, strictly convex in $v$ and superlinear in $v$: $L(q,v)/|v|\to\infty$ as $|v|\to\infty$, uniformly for $q$ in compact sets (superlinearity is not needed where $\mathcal V(q)$ is compact);
  • $W$ is continuous, $W\ge0$ and $W(0)=0$; wherever $\nabla W$ is used (from Section 4.4 on), $W$ is continuously differentiable on a neighbourhood of $\Omega$;
  • $\Omega$ is closed and contains a neighbourhood of the rest point $q=0$.

Running example. The quadratic Lagrangian $L=\tfrac12q^\top Qq+\tfrac12v^\top Rv$, $Q,R\succ0$, meets these assumptions, and its scalar case

$$ L=\tfrac12m v^2+\tfrac12\kappa \,q^2,\qquad m,\kappa >0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\kappa /m}, \tag{3} $$

(that is, $Q=\kappa$, $R=m$) is the simplest one. It penalizes speed and distance from the rest point. Every result of Part I will be computed on it as soon as it is derived.

1.3 Steps of a digital controller

A digital controller works in steps of length $h$. Over one step the action is replaced by a discrete Lagrangian $L_d(q,q')$, the cost of moving from $q$ to $q'$ in one step (Marsden & West, 2001): a function of the two endpoints, finite on admissible pairs (those that a velocity in $\mathcal V$ can join) and $+\infty$ otherwise. The simplest choice evaluates $L$ once per step, the rectangle rule:

$$ L_d(q,q')=h\,L\Big(q,\frac{q'-q}{h}\Big),\qquad\text{for the example}\quad L_d=\frac{m}{2h}(q'-q)^2+\frac h2\,\kappa q^2 . \tag{4} $$

Section 6.1 will single out a distinguished choice, the exact discrete Lagrangian, which needs the solution of the continuous problem; nothing before it depends on which $L_d$ is used. Positivity carries over to steps: we assume $L_d(q,q')\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$ on admissible pairs, with a class-$\mathcal K_\infty$ function again written $\alpha_L$ (for (4) it is $h$ times the one of $L$).

2. The Remaining Action and the Principle of Optimality

2.1 The window problem

Consider a window of $N$ steps. The remaining action is the least action that has to be paid from the current point $q$:

$$ V_N(q)=\min_{q_1,\dots,q_N}\ \Big\{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})+W(q_N)\Big\}, \qquad q_0=q,\quad q_N\in\Omega, \tag{5} $$

over admissible paths. It is the optimal value function of MPC; in mechanics it is Hamilton’s principal function of the initial point plus the boundary term, minimized over the endpoint in $\Omega$ (Section 5.1 makes this precise). The set of points from which (5) has a solution is the solvable set $\mathcal X_N$; we write $V_N(q)=+\infty$ outside it and assume that the minima are attained (for instance, $L_d$ and $W$ continuous and $\Omega$ compact). The receding-window scheme re-solves (5) from the current point and follows only the first segment $q\mapsto q^+=q_1$ of the extremal path (Fig. 1).

2.2 The principle of optimality

Everything in Part I follows from one observation, Bellman’s principle of optimality: the tail of an optimal path is optimal for the problem that starts where the tail starts. Split the sum in (5) after the first step. For a fixed first point $q_1=q'$, the minimum over $q_2,\dots,q_N$ is the problem (5) with $N-1$ steps from $q'$, so

$$ V_N(q)=\min_{q'}\big\{L_d(q,q')+V_{N-1}(q')\big\},\qquad V_0(q)=\begin{cases}W(q),&q\in\Omega,\\ +\infty,&q\notin\Omega.\end{cases} \tag{6} $$

The recursion builds the remaining action one step at a time, from the boundary term outwards; the terminal constraint is carried by the value $+\infty$ of $V_0$. Write it as $V_N=\mathcal B V_{N-1}$ with the Bellman operator

$$ (\mathcal BV)(q)=\min_{q'}\big\{L_d(q,q')+V(q')\big\}. \tag{7} $$

The one property of $\mathcal B$ used below is immediate from (7): $\mathcal B$ is monotone, i.e. $V_a\le V_b$ pointwise implies $\mathcal BV_a\le\mathcal BV_b$. A cheaper arrival can only make the departure cheaper. (The dynamic-programming literature often writes this operator $T$; here the letter $\mathcal T$ is the kinetic energy.)

2.3 Terminal ingredients

The recursion starts from $V_0$, which is finite only on $\Omega$, so whether the scheme stabilizes depends on $W$ and $\Omega$. The classical requirement (Chen & Allgöwer, 1998; Mayne et al., 2000; Rawlings et al., 2017, Ch. 2; Grüne & Pannek, 2017, Ch. 5) is a reference flow $\varphi_f:\Omega\to\Omega$, $\varphi_f(0)=0$, with two properties:

Terminal ingredients.
  1. A1 (invariance). For all $q\in\Omega$ the step $(q,\varphi_f(q))$ is admissible and $\varphi_f(q)\in\Omega$.
  2. A2 (boundary-term descent). Along the reference flow, $W$ decreases at least as fast as the action is spent: $$W(\varphi_f(q))-W(q)\le -L_d\big(q,\varphi_f(q)\big)\qquad\forall q\in\Omega.\tag{8}$$

In the language of the recursion the two conditions say one thing. For $q\in\Omega$ the reference step is a candidate in (6) with $N=1$, so $V_1(q)\le L_d(q,\varphi_f(q))+W(\varphi_f(q))\le W(q)=V_0(q)$; for $q\notin\Omega$, $V_0(q)=+\infty$. Hence

$$ \text{A1 and A2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_1\le V_0\quad\text{everywhere.} \tag{9} $$

Conversely, if $V_1\le W$ on $\Omega$, then from each $q\in\Omega$ the minimizer of the one-step problem is an admissible successor in $\Omega$ for which (8) holds, and it can serve as the reference flow. So (8) says exactly that one step of the recursion does not raise the remaining action. It is a property of $W$, $\Omega$ and one step of $L_d$; it has to be verified before use, and it is the same for every window length.

The original argument of Keerthi & Gilbert (1988) used the terminal equality constraint $q_N=0$ instead of a boundary term: $\Omega=\{0\}$, $W=0$, $\varphi_f(0)=0$. A1 and A2 then ask only that the rest step $(0,0)$ be admissible with $L_d(0,0)=0$, and Sections 3.1–⁠3.2 apply unchanged (the candidate path is extended by a rest step). This $\Omega$ is excluded by the standing assumption that $\Omega$ contains a neighbourhood of $q=0$, on which the upper bound of Section 3.3 relies. With $\Omega=\{0\}$ that bound has to come from controllability instead: $V_N\le V_1$ by Section 3.1, and $V_1(q)=L_d(q,0)$ wherever the step $(q,0)$ is admissible, which for (4) gives $V_N(q)\le\big(\tfrac{m}{2h}+\tfrac{h\kappa}{2}\big)q^2$, the same bound that Section 3.3 obtains with $\mathcal V=\mathbb R$.

3. Descent for Every Window Length

This section derives the stability of the scheme from the recursion (6), the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$ and the one-step condition (9), for any $N\ge1$ and any discrete Lagrangian.

3.1 Monotonicity in the window length

Apply the monotone operator $\mathcal B$ to (9): $V_2=\mathcal BV_1\le\mathcal BV_0=V_1$, and by induction

$$ V_N\le V_{N-1}\le\dots\le V_1\le V_0 . \tag{10} $$

Two consequences follow at once. The solvable sets are nested, $\mathcal X_N\supseteq\mathcal X_{N-1}$, because a finite $V_{N-1}$ forces a finite $V_N$; and $V_N\le W$ on $\Omega$ for every $N$.

3.2 The descent inequality

Let $q\in\mathcal X_N$ and let $q^+$ be the first point of an optimal path (uniqueness is not needed). The recursion (6) holds with equality at its minimizer, and (10) at $q^+$ gives $V_{N-1}(q^+)\ge V_N(q^+)$: $$V_N(q)=L_d(q,q^+)+V_{N-1}(q^+)\ \ge\ L_d(q,q^+)+V_N(q^+).$$ This is the descent inequality:

$$ V_N(q^+)-V_N(q)\ \le\ -L_d(q,q^+). \tag{11} $$

In words: after one step, the action still to be paid has dropped by at least the action just spent. The same line gives recursive feasibility: $V_{N-1}(q^+)<\infty$, so $q^+\in\mathcal X_{N-1}\subseteq\mathcal X_N$ and the scheme is defined again at the next step. Nothing beyond (6), the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$ and (8) was used, and $N$ is arbitrary. The classical proof unpacks (10) into an explicit feasible candidate (Mayne et al., 2000, Sec. 3); written out, it also covers solvers that do not return the exact minimum.

The same proof with an explicit candidate: $V_N(q^+)-V_N(q)\le -L_d(q,q^+)$
  1. Optimal action at $q$. Let $(q_0,\dots,q_N)$, $q_0=q$, be an optimal path of (5). Then $V_N(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})+W(q_N)$, and the scheme moves to $q^+=q_1$.
  2. A feasible candidate from $q^+$. Shift the path and extend it by one reference step: $(q_1,\dots,q_N,\tilde q)$ with $\tilde q=\varphi_f(q_N)$. Since $q_N\in\Omega$, A1 makes the extra step admissible and puts $\tilde q$ in $\Omega$.
  3. Bound the optimum by the candidate. $V_N(q^+)\le\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})+L_d(q_N,\tilde q)+W(\tilde q)$.
  4. Use the boundary-term descent. By A2, $L_d(q_N,\tilde q)+W(\tilde q)\le W(q_N)$: the reference step cancels, and $V_N(q^+)\le\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})+W(q_N)$.
  5. Subtract step 1: $V_N(q^+)-V_N(q)\le-L_d(q,q^+)$. $\blacksquare$

For $N=1$ the shifted path is empty, the candidate is the reference step alone, and step 4 reads $V_1(q^+)\le W(q^+)$. Steps 3–⁠5 use only that the plan applied at the next step costs no more than the candidate. A solver that is warm-started with the candidate and returns it whenever it finds nothing better therefore keeps (11) for the cost it computes, whatever its optimality, as in suboptimal MPC; Section 11.5 uses this.

3.3 From descent to asymptotic stability

Strict decrease alone is not enough. A Lyapunov function must also be sandwiched between two comparison functions, $\alpha_1(|q|)\le V_N(q)\le\alpha_2(|q|)$ (Rawlings et al., 2017, App. B). Write the positivity assumption of Section 1.3 as A3: $L_d(q,q')\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$ on admissible pairs, with $W\ge0$, $W(0)=0$.

  • Lower bound. Every term of (5) is non-negative, so $V_N(q)\ge L_d(q,q_1)\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$.
  • Upper bound near the rest point. By (10), $V_N\le W$ on $\Omega$. Take $\varepsilon>0$ with $\{|q|\le\varepsilon\}\subseteq\Omega$ and set $\alpha_W(\rho)=\sup_{|q|\le \rho}W(q)+\rho$, which is of class $\mathcal K$ because $W$ is continuous with $W(0)=0$.
  • Upper bound away from it. If $V_N\le\bar V$ on $\mathcal X_N$, then for $|q|\ge \varepsilon$, $V_N(q)\le \bar V\le (\bar V/\varepsilon)|q|$. Since $V_N(q)\ge\alpha_L(|q|)$, such a $\bar V$ exists only if $\mathcal X_N$ is bounded. When $L_d$ is bounded on bounded sets of admissible pairs, as the example (4) is, a bounded $\mathcal X_N$ is also enough. Each point $q_j$ of an admissible $N$-step path ending in $\Omega$ lies in $\mathcal X_{N-j}\subseteq\mathcal X_N$, because its tail from $q_j$ is an admissible $(N-j)$-step path into $\Omega$ and the solvable sets are nested (Section 3.1). So $V_N\le N\sup L_d+\max_\Omega W$ on $\mathcal X_N$, with the supremum taken over admissible pairs in $\mathcal X_N$. Continuous $L_d$ and $W$ and a bounded $\Omega$ do not make $\mathcal X_N$ bounded, but bounded steps do: if $|q'-q|\le D$ on admissible pairs (a speed limit $|v|\le\bar v$ gives $D=h\bar v$), then $\mathcal X_N$ lies within distance $ND$ of $\Omega$. Without such a limit $\mathcal X_N$ need not be bounded. For (4) with $\mathcal V=\mathbb R$, every point reaches $\Omega$ in one step, so $\mathcal X_N=\mathbb R$, and on it $V_N(q)\ge\tfrac h2\kappa q^2$ is unbounded.

Hence $\alpha_2(\rho)=\max\{\alpha_W(\rho),\,\bar V \rho/\varepsilon\}$ is a class-$\mathcal K$ upper bound on all of $\mathcal X_N$, and with A3 the descent inequality (11) becomes

$$ V_N(q_{k+1})-V_N(q_k)\le-\alpha_L(|q_k|), \tag{12} $$

which yields asymptotic stability of $q=0$ with region of attraction $\mathcal X_N$. When no such $\bar V$ exists, a global bound may still hold. For (4) with $\mathcal V=\mathbb R$, (10) and the one-step candidate $q'=0\in\Omega$ give $V_N(q)\le V_1(q)\le L_d(q,0)+W(0)=\big(\tfrac m{2h}+\tfrac h2\kappa\big)q^2$ for every $q$. This is a class-$\mathcal K$ choice of $\alpha_2$ on all of $\mathcal X_N=\mathbb R$, so the region of attraction is all of $\mathbb R$. Otherwise, restrict the region of attraction to a sublevel set $\{V_N\le\bar V\}$ with $\bar V>0$. It is invariant by (11), and on it the upper bound holds with this $\bar V$.

3.4 What the window length does: the one-step window

The certificate (8) involves $W$, $\Omega$ and one step of $L_d$; it does not involve $N$. What a longer window does is read off (10): it enlarges the solvable set, $\mathcal X_N\supseteq\mathcal X_{N-1}$; it lowers the remaining action, $V_N\le V_{N-1}$ (appending a reference step never increases the action); and it leaves the certificate unchanged. It moves the cut between the part of the remaining action that is computed (the window) and the part that is estimated (the tail $W$); Section 6.5 shows this cut with numbers.

The shortest window, $N=1$, has no lookahead. The scheme becomes a local rule: from $q$, jump to the admissible $q'\in\Omega$ that minimizes

$$ V_1(q)=\min_{q'}\ \big\{L_d(q,q')+W(q')\big\} \quad\text{s.t. }(q,q')\ \text{admissible},\ q'\in\Omega, \tag{13} $$

which is (6) with $N=1$. All the stability-relevant information now sits in $W$, which must satisfy (8) on $\Omega$ and only there, because the constraint forces every successor into $\Omega$. Each step trades the descent of $W$ against the cost $L_d$ of moving, and nothing beyond the next point is examined (a “greedy” path). The solvable set is the one-step preimage of $\Omega$,

$$ \mathcal X_1=\{q:\ \exists\,q'\in\Omega,\ (q,q')\ \text{admissible}\}\ \supseteq\ \Omega , \tag{14} $$

larger than $\Omega$ and, when the steps are limited (a speed limit, or the torque limits of Section 11), typically still small (with no limit on the steps it is all of $\mathbb R^n$, Section 3.3); from points outside it no admissible one-step path exists, and the scheme is undefined there (Section 11.6 has an example). 4. The Continuous Limit: Hamilton–Jacobi, Momentum and the Certificate

Sections 2 and 3 worked with steps. What happens when the step itself shrinks to zero? The recursion (6) becomes a partial differential equation, its minimization a Legendre transform, and the one-step condition (9) a pointwise inequality. The limit is worth taking for two reasons: it exposes the mechanics, since momentum and Hamiltonian appear without being put in, and it is where controllers that re-plan at every tick of a fast loop live.

4.1 From steps to rates

Measure the window in time. Write $\vartheta=t_f-t$ for the time to go and $V_\vartheta(q)$ for the least action (2) over paths of duration $\vartheta$ from $q$; this is (5) with the sum replaced by the integral, and $V_0$ is the boundary term of (6). The remaining action is thus a function of two variables, the point and the time to go; when both change we write $V(q,\vartheta):=V_\vartheta(q)$. Apply the principle of optimality to a first step of length $h$, taken with a constant velocity $v$: its action is $h\,L(q,v)+O(h^2)$, the rectangle rule (4), and it arrives at $q+hv$ with $\vartheta-h$ still to go, so $$V_\vartheta(q)=\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big\{h\,L(q,v)+V_{\vartheta-h}(q+hv)\big\}+o(h).$$ The step changes both arguments of $V$. Along the line $t'\mapsto(q+t'v,\ \vartheta-t')$, $0\le t'\le h$, the chain rule gives the rate $\tfrac{d}{dt'}V(q+t'v,\vartheta-t')\big|_{t'=0}=\nabla V_\vartheta(q)\cdot v-\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta(q)$, so where $V$ is continuously differentiable in $(q,\vartheta)$, $$V_{\vartheta-h}(q+hv)=V_\vartheta(q)+h\,\big[\nabla V_\vartheta(q)\cdot v-\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta(q)\big]+o(h).$$ Moving to a new point changes the remaining action at the rate $\nabla V\cdot v$, and the shrinking window at the rate $-\partial_\vartheta V$. The term $V_\vartheta(q)$ cancels on both sides and $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta(q)$ does not depend on $v$; dividing by $h$ and letting $h\to0$ gives

$$ \partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta(q)=\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L(q,v)+\nabla V_\vartheta(q)\cdot v\big],\qquad V_0=W\ \text{on }\Omega . \tag{15} $$

This is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (in optimal control, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation), in time-to-go form: the rate at which the remaining action changes as the window opens. The bracket is a balance: $\nabla V\cdot v$ rewards moving downhill on what remains to be paid, while $L$ charges for moving at all. (Passing the minimizer to the limit uses remainders that are $o(h)$ uniformly over bounded sets of velocities, to which superlinearity of $L$ confines the near-minimizers, and a minimizer that exists, by superlinearity or a compact $\mathcal V(q)$, and is unique, by strict convexity of $L$ in $v$ on a convex $\mathcal V(q)$. Where $V$ is not differentiable, constraints being the usual cause, (15) holds in a generalized, viscosity, sense; Liberzon, 2012, §5.3.)

4.2 Momentum and the Legendre transform

Give a name to the vector that multiplies $v$ in the bracket. The momentum field is minus the gradient of the remaining action,

$$ p(q):=-\nabla V_\vartheta(q). \tag{16} $$

The bracket becomes $L-p\cdot v$, and its minimum over $v$ is minus a Legendre transform:

$$ \min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L(q,v)-p\cdot v\big]=-H_{\mathcal V}(q,p),\qquad H_{\mathcal V}(q,p):=\sup_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[\,p\cdot v-L(q,v)\,\big]. \tag{17} $$

Without constraints, $\mathcal V(q)=\mathbb R^n$, we write $H$: it is the Hamiltonian of $L$. By superlinearity $p\cdot v-L(q,v)\to-\infty$ as $|v|\to\infty$, so the supremum is finite and attained, at the velocity where $\partial L/\partial v=p$. So the selected velocity is the one whose momentum $\partial L/\partial v$ equals $-\nabla V$; strict convexity of $L$ in $v$ makes $v\mapsto\partial L/\partial v$ one-to-one and superlinearity makes it onto, so this relation can be inverted for every momentum. With (17), (15) reads

$$ \partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta=-H_{\mathcal V}\big(q,-\nabla V_\vartheta\big),\qquad V_0=W\ \text{on }\Omega, \tag{18} $$

or, in the absolute initial time $t=t_f-\vartheta$ with the final time $t_f$ fixed, $\partial_t\big[V_{t_f-t}(q)\big]=H_{\mathcal V}(q,-\nabla V)$. The minus sign in (16) comes from reading the remaining action as a function of the initial point of the path still to be travelled; textbook mechanics reads the action at the final point (Section 5.1 shows that the two are one rule). Everything in this section holds with or without constraints; Section 7 collects what changes when a constraint binds, and until then we drop the subscript.

For the example (3), the supremum of $pv-\tfrac12mv^2$ is attained at $v=p/m$, so $H(q,p)=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}-\tfrac12\kappa q^2$; for the quadratic Lagrangian, likewise, $H(q,p)=\tfrac12p^\top R^{-1}p-\tfrac12q^\top Qq$, attained at $v=R^{-1}p$.

The sign of the costate depends on convention. In Pontryagin’s maximum principle (normal case, cost multiplier $-1$) the costate equals the momentum $p$. In the minimum principle commonly used in MPC texts it equals $\nabla V=-p$, written $\eta$ here, and the control Hamiltonian $L+\eta\cdot v$ is minimized: $\min_v[L+\eta\cdot v]=-H(q,-\eta)$. This document fixes $p=\partial L/\partial v$ throughout.

Why $L$ cannot be dropped. Strict convexity of $L$ in $v$ is what lets the momentum determine the velocity. Set $L\equiv0$: the bracket becomes $\nabla V\cdot v$, which is linear in $v$. A Lagrangian linear in the velocities is singular: its Legendre map is not invertible, and momentum no longer determines velocity. If $\mathcal V$ is unbounded, the minimum does not exist (the speed grows without bound); if $\mathcal V$ is compact, the minimizer sits on its boundary, typically at a vertex (extreme-speed, bang-bang motion). A Lagrangian that is strictly convex and superlinear in $v$ regularizes the problem: it makes the momentum–velocity relation invertible and selects an interior velocity that balances the rate of descent against the cost of motion. For a robot the torque cost plays this role (Section 8.6). Both properties are needed. $L=\tfrac12\kappa q^2+\sqrt{1+v^2}-1$ is strictly convex in $v$ but grows only linearly: its momentum $\partial L/\partial v=v/\sqrt{1+v^2}$ stays in $(-1,1)$, $H(q,p)=+\infty$ for $|p|>1$, and where $|\nabla V|\ge1$ the bracket has no minimizer and the speed grows without bound, as for $L\equiv0$. In general, $H(q,\cdot)$ is finite for every momentum exactly when $L$ is superlinear in $v$.

4.3 The receding window in continuous time

The receding-window scheme applies the first move of the window problem and re-solves. In continuous time the first move is the minimizer $v^*_\vartheta(q)$ of the bracket in (15), and the closed loop is $\dot q=v^*_\vartheta(q)$ with the window length $\vartheta$ held fixed. Along it, by (15) evaluated at its own minimizer,

$$ \frac{d}{dt}V_\vartheta\big(q(t)\big)=\nabla V_\vartheta\cdot v^*_\vartheta=-L\big(q,v^*_\vartheta\big)+\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta(q). \tag{19} $$

So the continuous-time descent condition $\dot V_\vartheta\le-L$ holds exactly where $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\le0$, that is, where a longer window would not raise the remaining action. This is (10) and (11) in continuous time. Descent at the full rate $L$ is more decrease than a Lyapunov function needs: by (19), $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\le(1-\beta)\,L(q,v^*_\vartheta)$ for all $q$, with a constant $0<\beta\le1$, gives $\dot V_\vartheta\le-\beta L\le-\beta\,\alpha_L(|q|)$, the continuous counterpart of (12) with $\beta\,\alpha_L$ in place of $\alpha_L$. The remaining action can therefore decrease along the receding window although it increases with the window length; the scaled candidates $W=cV_\infty$ with $0<c<1$ do so at $\vartheta=0$ (Section 4.6) and, on the hilltop, for every window (Section 5.2). In steps the bookkeeping is the same: by (6) at its minimizer, $V_N(q^+)-V_N(q)=-L_d(q,q^+)+\big[V_N(q^+)-V_{N-1}(q^+)\big]$, so (11) holds exactly when $V_N(q^+)\le V_{N-1}(q^+)$, while decrease needs only a bracket below $L_d(q,q^+)$ (Section 6.4). The full rate is the one pursued here because it can be checked on the pair $(L,W)$ before any window is solved (Sections 4.4 and 4.5), whereas the weaker condition involves $V_\vartheta$ itself. What remains is to find boundary terms for which $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\le0$, and the place to look is the shortest window.

4.4 The infinitesimal window: a rule and a certificate

As $\vartheta\to0$, $V_\vartheta\to W$ on $\Omega$ (by superlinearity, a path that covers a fixed distance in a vanishing time has unbounded action, and $W$ is continuous): the boundary term itself plays the part of the remaining action, and the receding window applies the minimizer of the bracket with $V=W$,

$$ v^*(q)=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big\{L(q,v)+\nabla W(q)\cdot v\big\}. \tag{20} $$

The window problem has become a pointwise feedback law: one minimization at the current state, with no trajectory to optimize. A controller that re-plans at every tick of a fast loop, with a window as short as one tick, lives in this limit, and it is the form whole-body controllers take; for the arm of Section 11 it will be PD control with gravity compensation. By (17) with $p=-\nabla W$, or by (19) at $\vartheta=0$, the rate of change of $W$ along the rule is

$$ \dot W=\nabla W\cdot v^*=-L(q,v^*)-H\big(q,-\nabla W(q)\big). \tag{21} $$

Hence the continuous-time descent condition $\dot W\le -L$ holds if and only if

$$ \min_{v}\big[L(q,v)+\dot W(q,v)\big]\le0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\big(q,-\nabla W(q)\big)\ \ge\ 0 , \tag{22} $$

with $\dot W(q,v)=\nabla W(q)\cdot v$. This is the continuous form of (8): the rule (20) plays the part of the reference flow, and (22) says that along it $W$ decreases at least at the rate at which action is spent. By (18) it is also the continuous form of (9): $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\big|_{\vartheta=0}=-H(q,-\nabla W)\le0$, so a short window never needs more than $W$. Here $W$ is a candidate, and (22) is its certificate.

The same conclusions follow from the action of a short window directly. Over a window of length $\vartheta$ with constant velocity $v$, both terms of the action expand to first order in $\vartheta$:

$$ \begin{gathered} S_\vartheta(q,v)=\int_t^{t+\vartheta}L\,dt'+W(q+\vartheta v)\\ =W(q)+\vartheta\,\big[\,L(q,v)+\nabla W(q)\cdot v\,\big]+o(\vartheta). \end{gathered} \tag{23} $$

$W(q)$ does not depend on $v$. Subtracting it and dividing by $\vartheta>0$ do not change which $v$ minimizes, and both steps are needed: as $\vartheta\to0$, $S_\vartheta\to W(q)$ for every $v$, so the limit of $S_\vartheta$ itself cannot rank velocities, whose costs differ only by $O(\vartheta)$. The comparison of costs $V_1\le W$ of Section 3 degenerates to $W\le W$; what survives is the comparison of rates, the bracket, which is what a Lyapunov argument checks. With $v=v^*$, (23) bounds the remaining action of the infinitesimal window from above by $W-\vartheta\,H(q,-\nabla W)+o(\vartheta)$. Conversely, along any admissible path $W\big(q(\vartheta)\big)=W(q)+\int_0^\vartheta\nabla W\cdot\dot q\,dt$, so its action is $W(q)+\int_0^\vartheta\big[L+\nabla W\cdot\dot q\big]\,dt\ge W(q)-\int_0^\vartheta H\big(q(t),-\nabla W(q(t))\big)\,dt$, and superlinearity keeps the paths of bounded action within $o(1)$ of $q$. Hence, at interior points of $\Omega$, $V_\vartheta=W-\vartheta\,H(q,-\nabla W)+o(\vartheta)$, the first-order solution of (18).

Two outputs of one minimization. The bracket $L+\dot W$ is minimized once and yields two different objects: its argmin (20), the controller the rule selects, and its minimum value $-H(q,-\nabla W)$, whose sign is the certificate (22). They answer different questions. A convergent controller whose certificate fails is possible and common; the first examples follow in Sections 4.6 and 6.4. The certificate is a property of the pair $(L,W)$, not of $W$ alone and not of the closed loop.

4.5 One certificate for every window length

Section 4.3 reduced descent of the receding window to $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\le0$, and Section 4.4 gave that inequality at $\vartheta=0$. It propagates to every $\vartheta$ by the argument of Section 3.1:

  1. A short window never needs more than $W$. Suppose (22) holds on $\Omega$ and the rule (20) keeps $\Omega$ invariant (for instance $\Omega$ a sublevel set of $W$, which $\dot W\le-L\le0$ keeps invariant). Integrating $L+\dot W=-H(q,-\nabla W)\le0$ along the rule over a time $\vartheta'$ gives $\int_0^{\vartheta'} L\,dt+W\big(q(\vartheta')\big)\le W(q)$: a feasible path for the window $\vartheta'$ that costs no more than $W$. Hence $V_{\vartheta'}\le V_0$.
  2. Monotonicity in the boundary term. The principle of optimality, splitting the window $\vartheta+\vartheta'$ after its first $\vartheta$, writes $V_{\vartheta+\vartheta'}$ as the window-$\vartheta$ problem with boundary term $V_{\vartheta'}$. That problem is monotone in its boundary term, exactly as $\mathcal B$ is, so $V_{\vartheta'}\le V_0$ gives
$$ \begin{gathered} V_{\vartheta+\vartheta'}\le V_\vartheta\quad\text{for all }\vartheta,\vartheta'\ge0,\\ \text{hence}\quad\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta\le0 . \end{gathered} \tag{24} $$

By (19), the receding window then satisfies $\dot V_\vartheta\le-L$ for every window length: one condition on the pair $(L,W)$ certifies all of them, as (8) did in discrete time. In receding-horizon control this is the standard continuous-time requirement on a terminal cost that is a control Lyapunov function (Chen & Allgöwer, 1998; Jadbabaie et al., 2001), and the rule (20) is a pointwise controller built on that function, the kind of law that Primbs et al. (2000) generalize to receding horizons.

4.6 The exact boundary term: the stationary Hamilton–Jacobi equation

Equality in (22) for every $q$ makes the right-hand side of (18) vanish at $\vartheta=0$, and $W$ is a stationary solution of (18); whether it is the remaining action depends on the terminal region. By (17), along any admissible path $L+\dot W\ge\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L+\nabla W\cdot v\big]=-H(q,-\nabla W)=0$, with equality only at the minimizer (20). Integrated over the window, this says that every path from $q$ that ends in $\Omega$ costs at least $W(q)$, and that the path of the rule (20) attains $W(q)$ whenever it ends in $\Omega$. Without a terminal constraint ($\Omega=\mathbb R^n$, so $V_0=W$) this gives $V_\vartheta=W$ for all $\vartheta$, and the window length is irrelevant. With a terminal region, $V_\vartheta=W$ exactly where the path of the rule is in $\Omega$ at time $\vartheta$: if the rule keeps $\Omega$ invariant, on $\Omega$ and at every point that the rule brings into $\Omega$ within time $\vartheta$, a set that grows with $\vartheta$. Elsewhere the terminal constraint binds and $V_\vartheta>W$, and the window length still sets the value, the first move and, with bounded velocities, the solvable set (numbers in Section 5.2). The condition, equality in (22) for every $q$, is the stationary Hamilton–Jacobi equation,

$$ H\big(q,-\nabla V_\infty(q)\big)=0 . \tag{25} $$

Among continuously differentiable $W\ge0$ with $W(0)=0$ (and assuming every finite-action path converges to the rest point), its solution is exactly the infinite-time remaining action, $W=V_\infty$; this is the verification theorem of optimal control (Liberzon, 2012, Sec. 5.1). The boundary term is then exact. Without these qualifications the equation has other solutions (for the quadratic case below, $-V_\infty$).

Quadratic Lagrangians. For $L=\tfrac12q^\top Qq+\tfrac12v^\top Rv$, try $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$ with $X\succ0$. Then $p=-\nabla W=-Xq$, and the Hamiltonian of Section 4.2 gives

$$ H\big(q,-Xq\big)=\tfrac12\,q^\top\big(XR^{-1}X-Q\big)\,q,\qquad v^*=R^{-1}p=-R^{-1}Xq . \tag{26} $$

The stationary equation (25) holds if and only if $XR^{-1}X=Q$. Its positive-definite solution $P$, $PR^{-1}P=Q$, is the continuous-time Riccati solution for $\dot q=v$; with $W=\tfrac12q^\top Pq$ the rule (20) is the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) $v=-R^{-1}Pq$. For the example (3), $P=\sqrt{m\kappa}$.

Example: scaled candidates. Take $W=c\,V_\infty$, i.e. $X=cP$ with $c>0$. By (26) and $PR^{-1}P=Q$, $H(q,-\nabla W)=\tfrac{c^2-1}{2}\,q^\top Qq$:
  • $c=1$: $H=0$, and $\dot W=-L$ exactly;
  • $c>1$: $H>0$, so $\dot W<-L$: an over-estimate of the tail certifies descent;
  • $c<1$: $H<0$, so $\dot W>-L$. $W$ still decreases ($\dot W=-c^2q^\top Qq$), but (22) fails: the condition is sufficient for decrease, not necessary.
Under-estimating the tail breaks the certificate, not necessarily the convergence: the first instance of a good argmin with a failed certificate. Section 5.2 follows the three cases to finite windows, and Section 6.4 finds the discrete mirror image.

5. Extremals: Solving the Hamilton–Jacobi Equation

Section 4 derived the Hamilton–Jacobi equation; this section solves it. The tool is the method of characteristics, which follows the equation along the optimal path. It returns Hamilton’s equations, so the optimal paths are the extremals of classical mechanics and the momentum (16) is their canonical momentum.

5.1 Characteristics: the momentum along the optimal path

Fix a window $\vartheta_0$ and start the optimal path from $q$ at $t=0$, so that $t_f=\vartheta_0$; along it the time to go is $\vartheta=t_f-t$. Its velocity is the minimizer in (15), which by the envelope theorem applied to (17) is $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$ at $p=-\nabla V$. Assume $V$ twice continuously differentiable along the path.

  1. Differentiate (18) with respect to $q$: $\partial_\vartheta\nabla V=-H_q+\nabla^2V\,H_p$, with $H_q$ and $H_p$ evaluated at $(q,-\nabla V)$.
  2. Along the path the window shrinks, so $\tfrac{d}{dt}\nabla V=\nabla^2V\,\dot q-\partial_\vartheta\nabla V=\nabla^2V\,H_p+H_q-\nabla^2V\,H_p=H_q$.
  3. Hence $p(t)=-\nabla V\big(q(t),t_f-t\big)$ obeys Hamilton’s equations, and at the end of the window, where the time to go is zero and the remaining action is $V_0=W$, it takes the value fixed by the boundary term:
$$ \dot q=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad \dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\qquad p(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(q(t_f)\big). \tag{27} $$

The last condition is the transversality condition of a Bolza problem (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963, §§6, 14). Step 3 uses $V_0=W$ near $q(t_f)$, so the condition needs $q(t_f)$ in the interior of $\Omega$; at an endpoint on the boundary of $\Omega$ the arrival momentum also carries the reaction of the terminal constraint (see the discussion of the principal function below). Without velocity constraints, eliminating $p$ through $\partial L/\partial v=p$ turns the first two equations into the Euler–Lagrange equation $\tfrac{d}{dt}\,\partial L/\partial\dot q=\partial L/\partial q$: the optimal paths are the extremals of the action, and their canonical momentum is minus the gradient of the remaining action. Three consequences follow.

  • Descent along the optimal path. Along the path computed at one instant, $\tfrac{d}{dt}V=\nabla V\cdot\dot q-\partial_\vartheta V=-p\cdot\dot q+H=-L$, using $H=p\cdot\dot q-L$ at the maximizer. This holds for every window. It is not (19): along the computed path the window shrinks, whereas the receding loop keeps $\vartheta$ fixed and re-solves, and its rate differs from $-L$ by $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta$, the monotonicity term that the certificate (22) controls (Section 4.5).
  • Why $H=0$ for the infinite window. $L$ has no explicit time dependence, so $H$ is conserved along (27): $\tfrac{d}{dt}H={H_q\cdot\dot q}+{H_p\cdot\dot p}=0$. On the path that reaches the rest point, $q\to0$ and $p\to0$, and $H(0,0)=-\min_vL(0,v)=0$. So $H=0$ all along, which is (25) seen from the path.
  • One rule for every window. The momentum is minus the gradient of the remaining action of the problem at hand:

The link to textbook mechanics. Hamilton’s principal function $\mathcal S(q_i,t_i;q_f,t_f)$ is the integral of $L$ along the extremal that joins two endpoints, without the boundary term. Textbook mechanics reads it as a function of the final point, with $\partial\mathcal S/\partial q_{\text{final}}=p$ (Arnold, 1989, §46); its differential is $d\mathcal S=p_f\,dq_f-H_f\,dt_f-p_i\,dq_i+H_i\,dt_i$. The remaining action reads it as a function of the initial point, adds the boundary term and minimizes over the endpoint in $\Omega$:

$$ V_{t_f-t}(q)=\min_{q_f\in\Omega}\big[\mathcal S(q,t;q_f,t_f)+W(q_f)\big]. \tag{28} $$

In $V$ the final time is fixed and the endpoint is free in $\Omega$. At an optimal endpoint in the interior of $\Omega$ the transversality condition in (27) makes the $dq_f$ terms of $d\mathcal S$ and $dW$ cancel. At an optimal endpoint on the boundary of $\Omega=\{\chi\le0\}$ the first-order condition of (28) is instead $p_f=-\nabla W(q_f)-\mu\nabla\chi(q_f)$ with $\mu\ge0$: the arrival momentum carries the reaction of the terminal constraint ($p_f=-(1+\mu)\nabla W$ for a sublevel set of $W$, as in Section 7.1). The $dq_f$ terms still drop out, because while the constraint stays active the optimal endpoint moves along the boundary, $\nabla\chi\cdot dq_f=0$. Either way this leaves $dV=-p_i\,dq_i+H_i\,dt_i$: $\nabla V=-p$ and, since the time to go is $\vartheta=t_f-t_i$, $\partial_\vartheta V=-H$ at the initial point, which are (16) and (18) again. 5.2 Worked example: the hilltop

This example solves the stationary equation (25) for the running example (3), the ideal boundary term $V_\infty$ (written $V$ here), and checks the three predictions of Section 5.1: $H=0$ on the finite-action extremal, $p=-V'$ along it, and, with $W=V$ in the rule (20), $\dot V=-L$ exactly. Each step is labelled with what it establishes. Write the Lagrangian with $\dot q$ for $v$, $L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2+\tfrac12\kappa q^2$.

  1. Read $L$ as $\mathcal T-U$. $\mathcal T=\tfrac12m\dot q^2$ and $U(q)=-\tfrac12\kappa \,q^2$. The minimizing paths obey Newton’s law for a particle on an inverted parabolic potential, a hilltop at the origin. This $L$ is the variational Lagrangian, the cost we chose; the hilltop belongs to the variational problem, not to any robot (Section 12 returns to the difference).
  2. Hamilton’s equations: the candidate extremals. With $H=p^2/(2m)-\tfrac12\kappa q^2$ (Section 4.2), (27) reads ${\dot q=p/m}$, ${\dot p=\kappa q}$, i.e. the Euler–Lagrange equation $m\ddot q=\kappa q$, so $q(t)\sim e^{\pm\omega t}$. The Hamiltonian matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1/m\\\kappa&0\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm\omega$: the origin is a saddle in the $(q,p)$ plane.
  3. Jacobi: a minimum between fixed endpoints, not just stationarity. The Jacobi equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation linearized along the extremal. A later point where a nonzero solution $y$ with $y(0)=0$ vanishes again is called conjugate; with $\partial^2L/\partial v^2>0$ and no conjugate point in $(0,t_f]$, the extremal is a weak minimum: no path with the same two endpoints that stays close to it in both position and velocity has a smaller action (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963, §28). Here the Jacobi equation is $\ddot y=\omega^2y$ along every extremal, and $y(0)=0$ gives $y\propto\sinh\omega t$, which never vanishes again: no extremal has a conjugate point, on any interval. Because this $L$ is jointly convex in $(q,v)$, the minimum is even global among paths with the same endpoints. For an extremal $q$ with momentum $p=\partial L/\partial v$ and any path $q+\delta q$, convexity and the Euler–Lagrange equation $\dot p=\partial L/\partial q$ give $L(q+\delta q,\dot q+\delta\dot q)-L(q,\dot q)\ge\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\delta q+p\cdot\delta\dot q=\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(p\cdot\delta q\big)$; integrated, the action of $q+\delta q$ exceeds that of $q$ by at least $\big[p\cdot\delta q\big]_0^{t_f}$, which is zero when $\delta q(0)=\delta q(t_f)=0$. So every extremal is the least-action path between its own endpoints, the caveat in the table of Section 5.1. Both results hold for every extremal, the growing ones $\propto e^{+\omega t}$ included, so they do not select the path of the infinite window: finite action selects it (step 4), and the verification theorem of Section 4.6 makes its action the least (step 5). For an ordinary oscillator, $U=+\tfrac12\kappa q^2$, the Jacobi field is $\sin\omega t$, a conjugate point appears at $t=\pi/\omega$, and the action is only stationary on longer intervals.
  4. Finite action: it selects the $H=0$ extremal. Of all extremals only $q(t)=q_0e^{-\omega t}$ has finite action. Its momentum is $p=m\dot q=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q$ and its energy is $$H=\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac12\kappa q^2=0,$$ the separatrix, i.e. the stable manifold of the saddle (Fig. 3).
  5. The remaining action generates it: $p=-V'$. With $p=-V'$, the stationary equation (25) reads $V'^2/(2m)=\tfrac12\kappa q^2$, and its solution with $V\ge0$, $V(0)=0$ is $$V(q)=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q^2,\qquad p=-V'(q)=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q,\tag{29}$$ the momentum of step 4: the finite-action extremal is the graph of $p=-V'(q)$. It is the Riccati solution $P=\sqrt{m\kappa}$ of Section 4.6. Check against direct integration (along $q=q_0e^{-\omega t}$, both terms of $L$ are equal): $$\int_0^\infty L\,dt=\int_0^\infty \kappa \,q_0^2e^{-2\omega t}\,dt=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q_0^2\ \checkmark$$
  6. Velocity law: the rule (20) with $W=V$ gives $\dot V=-L$. The minimizer of $L+V'v$ solves $mv=-V'$, so $v^*=-\omega q$, which is $\dot q=p/m$ again. Along it $\dot V=V'v^*=-\kappa q^2=-L(q,v^*)$, as (21) with $H=0$ predicts, and $q$ decays exponentially to the rest point.
Physical intuition: balancing on the hilltop. Picture a ball that must come to rest on top of an upside-down parabola. Its energy $H$ is conserved. Push it slightly too hard ($H>0$) and it rolls over the top and off the other side. Push it too softly ($H<0$) and it stops short and rolls back. Only with exactly $H=0$ does it creep up and arrive, as $t\to\infty$, with a speed $\omega|q|$ proportional to the distance still to go. That knife-edge set of initial momenta is the stable manifold. The finite-action requirement selects it, and the remaining action $V$ is its generating function.

Finite windows and scaled candidates. With a quadratic boundary term and no terminal constraint ($\Omega=\mathbb R$) the time-dependent equation (18) can be solved for every window. Insert $V_\vartheta=\tfrac12P_\vartheta\,q^2$: by (26), $\tfrac12(\partial_\vartheta P_\vartheta)\,q^2=-\big(\tfrac{P_\vartheta^2}{2m}-\tfrac\kappa2\big)q^2$, the Riccati differential equation $\partial_\vartheta P_\vartheta=\kappa-P_\vartheta^2/m$, started at the weight $P_0$ of $W$. For the scaled candidate $W=cV$ of Section 4.6, $P_0=c\sqrt{m\kappa}$. Divided by $\sqrt{m\kappa}$, the equation reads $\partial_\vartheta\big(P_\vartheta/\sqrt{m\kappa}\big)=\omega\big(1-P_\vartheta^2/(m\kappa)\big)$, the differential equation of $\tanh$, and its solution from the value $c$ is

$$ V_\vartheta(q)=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\;\frac{c+\tanh\omega\vartheta}{1+c\tanh\omega\vartheta}\;q^2 . \tag{30} $$
  • $c=0$ (no boundary term): $V_\vartheta=\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,\tanh(\omega\vartheta)\,q^2$, which grows with $\vartheta$ and tends to (29) as $\vartheta\to\infty$;
  • $c=1$: $V_\vartheta=V$ for every $\vartheta$: the boundary term has already supplied the infinite tail. With a terminal interval $\Omega=[-r,r]$ this holds only where the path $q\,e^{-\omega t}$ of the rule (step 6) ends in $\Omega$, i.e. for $|q|\le r\,e^{\omega\vartheta}$ (Section 4.6); farther out the optimal endpoint is pushed to $\pm r$ and $V_\vartheta>V$. For $m=\kappa=1$, $r=\tfrac12$ and $\vartheta=0.1$, the action of the extremal from $q=1$ to $r$ in time $\vartheta$, plus $V(r)$, gives $V_\vartheta(1)=1.404$ against $V(1)=0.5$, and its initial velocity gives a first move $p/m=\dot q(0)=-5.04$ against the rule’s $-\omega q=-1$;
  • $c>1$: $V_\vartheta$ decreases with $\vartheta$ towards $V$. This is (24), and by (19) every window length descends at least at the rate $L$, as the certificate $H(q,-W')=\tfrac{c^2-1}{2}\,\kappa q^2>0$ promised;
  • $c<1$: $V_\vartheta$ increases with $\vartheta$ for every window: the failure of (22) at $\vartheta=0$ propagates, and the receding loop descends more slowly than $L$ although it converges. Along it $v^*_\vartheta=-P_\vartheta q/m$, so $\dot V_\vartheta=-P_\vartheta^2q^2/m=-\beta_\vartheta\,L(q,v^*_\vartheta)$ with $\beta_\vartheta=2P_\vartheta^2/(P_\vartheta^2+m\kappa)<1$. For $0<c<1$, $\beta_\vartheta\ge2c^2/(1+c^2)$ for every window (for $c=0$, $\beta_\vartheta>0$ once $\vartheta>0$), so $V_\vartheta$ remains a Lyapunov function of the loop.

5.3 Quadratic Lagrangians: the stable manifold and the boundary term

The structure of the hilltop is general. For $L=\tfrac12q^\top Qq+\tfrac12v^\top Rv$, Hamilton’s equations (27) are linear, $\tfrac{d}{dt}(q,p)=\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}(q,p)$, with eigenvalues in pairs $\pm\sigma$. The Riccati solution $P$ ($PR^{-1}P=Q$) is such that the graph $p=-Pq$ is the stable invariant subspace of this Hamiltonian matrix: $\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{I}{-P}=\binom{I}{-P}(-R^{-1}P)$, and $-R^{-1}P$ is the stable closed loop. This is how the Riccati equation is solved numerically (Laub, 1979). The remaining action $\tfrac12q^\top Pq$ is the generating function of that Lagrangian subspace, and (25) is the time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation at zero energy.

What the boundary term does. The transversality condition in (27) prescribes the momentum with which a finite path must arrive. In this picture $W$ tells the finite path how to join the infinite-time stable manifold. If $W=V_\infty$ exactly and no constraint is active along the path, the finite-window path lies on the manifold and the window length is irrelevant ($c=1$ in (30)). The terminal region $\Omega$ restricts where the endpoint may lie.

6. Back to Steps: Discretization and Exactness

Section 3 proved descent for any discrete Lagrangian; Sections 4 and 5 solved the continuous problem. This section brings the two together: which discrete Lagrangian reproduces the continuous problem, what momentum a computed path carries, and when one step is already exact.

6.1 The exact discrete Lagrangian and discrete momenta

The continuous problem over one step defines a distinguished discrete Lagrangian, the exact discrete Lagrangian:

$$ L_d^{E}(q,q')=\min\Big\{\int_0^{h}L\big(q(t),\dot q(t)\big)\,dt\ :\ q(0)=q,\ q(h)=q'\Big\}. \tag{31} $$

By Section 5.1 the minimizer is an extremal (for an $L$ jointly convex in $(q,v)$, the extremal joining the two points), so $L_d^E(q,q')=\mathcal S(q,0;q',h)$, and its derivatives are read off $d\mathcal S$: $\partial_1L_d^E=-p$ at the start of the step and $\partial_2L_d^E=p$ at its end. For any other $L_d$ these relations define the discrete momenta, through the discrete Legendre transforms (Marsden & West, 2001, who write $D_1$, $D_2$ for $\partial_1$, $\partial_2$, the derivatives in the first and second argument):

$$ p_j=-\partial_1L_d(q_j,q_{j+1}),\qquad p_{j+1}=\partial_2L_d(q_j,q_{j+1}). \tag{32} $$

For the example. The extremal joining $q$ to $q'$ in time $h$ is $q(t)=\big[q\sinh\omega(h-t)+q'\sinh\omega t\big]/\sinh\omega h$ (step 2 of Section 5.2). Along any extremal of (3), $L=\tfrac12\big(m\dot q^2+\kappa q^2\big)=\tfrac12\tfrac{d}{dt}(mq\dot q)$ because $m\ddot q=\kappa q$, so the action of the step is $\tfrac12\big[mq\dot q\big]_0^h$. Inserting the extremal and $m\omega=\sqrt{m\kappa}$,

$$ L_d^E(q,q')=\frac{\sqrt{m\kappa}}{2\sinh\omega h}\Big[(q^2+q'^2)\cosh\omega h-2qq'\Big]. \tag{33} $$

The rectangle rule (4) is instead a one-point quadrature of the same integral.

Why the distinction matters. With $L_d^E$ the discrete problem is the continuous one, sampled exactly; any other $L_d$ (rectangle rule, midpoint rule, …) is an approximation whose error vanishes as $h\to0$ but is not zero for the step actually used. Section 6.3 shows that the “one step is exact” statement depends on this choice, though a nonzero error need not break it: with the continuous-time Riccati weight it holds for $L_d^E$ and for the midpoint rule, but not for the rectangle rule. Section 6.4 shows numerically what goes wrong with the rectangle rule.

6.2 The momentum of a computed path

Differentiate the recursion (6) at its minimizer $q'=q^+$, assuming no constraint is active there and the functions are differentiable. Stationarity in $q'$ gives, by (32),

$$ \partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla V_{N-1}(q^+)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^+=-\nabla V_{N-1}(q^+), \tag{34} $$

and the envelope theorem gives $\nabla V_N(q)=\partial_1L_d(q,q^+)=-p$ at the start. Applied at every node of a computed path, where node $j$ has $N-j$ steps to go, this is the discrete form of (16):

$$ p_j=-\nabla V_{N-j}(q_j),\qquad j=0,\dots,N. \tag{35} $$

Matching the two expressions for $p_j$ at an interior node, from the step before and the step after, is the discrete Euler–Lagrange equation $\partial_2L_d(q_{j-1},q_j)+\partial_1L_d(q_j,q_{j+1})=0$. The last node lies in $\Omega$, where $V_0=W$, so $p_N=-\nabla W(q_N)$: the final momentum is fixed by the slope of the boundary term, the discrete form of the transversality condition in (27). For the one-step window (13) it reads $\partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)=0$. Section 7.1 adds active constraints, and Section 8.7 finds (35) again as the multipliers of a multiple-shooting solver.

6.3 Exactness is a fixed point

When does a short window already give the infinite-time optimum? By (6), $V_N=\mathcal B^NV_0$. Without a terminal constraint ($\Omega=\mathbb R^n$, so $V_0=W$), $V_N=W$ for every $N$ if and only if $\mathcal BW=W$ (if: by induction; only if: take $N=1$). With a terminal region, $V_0\ge W$, so $\mathcal BW=W$ and the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$ give $V_N\ge W$, while a chain of $N$ minimizing steps of $\mathcal BW$ costs exactly $W(q)$, by telescoping. Hence $V_N=W$ exactly at the points from which such a chain ends in $\Omega$ (a path that attains $W(q)$ must be one, because each step $q_j\to q_{j+1}$ costs at least $W(q_j)-W(q_{j+1})$). If the minimizer keeps $\Omega$ invariant (for instance $\Omega$ a sublevel set of $W$, since $W(q^+)=W(q)-L_d(q,q^+)\le W(q)$), these points include $\Omega$ and form a set that grows with $N$. Elsewhere the constraint $q_N\in\Omega$ binds and $V_N>W$. For example, with $L_d^E$, $m=\kappa=1$, $h=0.1$, its fixed point $W=\tfrac12q^2$ (first case below) and $\Omega=[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$: $V_1(1)=1.404$, and $V_N(1)=W(1)=0.5$ only from $N=7$, the first $N$ with $e^{-Nh}\le\tfrac12$. Where the terminal constraint is inactive, one step is exact exactly when $W$ is a fixed point of the Bellman operator of the chosen $L_d$, and a fixed point for one discrete Lagrangian need not be one for another.

  • Exact discrete Lagrangian. With $L_d^E$, the operator $\mathcal B$ is the continuous problem over one step: by (31), $\min_{q'}\{L_d^E(q,q')+W(q')\}$ minimizes $\int_0^hL\,dt+W\big(q(h)\big)$ over all paths from $q$. The principle of optimality in continuous time therefore makes $V_\infty$ a fixed point, and one step is exact with $W=V_\infty$. For a quadratic Lagrangian, with $W=\tfrac12q^\top Pq$, $P$ the Riccati solution of Section 4.6, and no constraint active: $V_1=W$, the step is $q^+=e^{-hR^{-1}P}q$, the exact sample of the flow of the LQR law $v=-R^{-1}Pq$, and (8) holds with equality along that flow.
  • Midpoint rule. The midpoint rule $L_d(q,q')=h\,L\big(q_{1/2},\tfrac{q'-q}{h}\big)$, $q_{1/2}=\tfrac12(q+q')$, is only an approximation of $L_d^E$, yet for a quadratic Lagrangian $\tfrac12q^\top Pq$ is a fixed point of its Bellman operator too. Write $v=(q'-q)/h$. For a quadratic $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$ the increment over the step is exactly the slope at the midpoint times the step, $W(q')-W(q)=\tfrac12(q'-q)^\top X(q'+q)=h\,\nabla W(q_{1/2})\cdot v$, so the midpoint analogue of the expansion (23) holds with no remainder: $$L_d(q,q')+W(q')=W(q)+h\big[L(q_{1/2},v)+\nabla W(q_{1/2})\cdot v\big].$$ By (17) and (26) the bracket is at least $-H(q_{1/2},-Xq_{1/2})$, with equality only at $v=-R^{-1}Xq_{1/2}$. For $X=P$ this bound is $0$ by (25), whatever $q'$ is, and the step $q^+=\big(I+\tfrac h2R^{-1}P\big)^{-1}\big(I-\tfrac h2R^{-1}P\big)q$ attains it (the inverse exists: $R^{-1}P$ is similar to $R^{-1/2}PR^{-1/2}\succ0$, so its eigenvalues are positive). Hence, with no constraint active, $V_1=W$ for every $h$ and (8) holds with equality along the step; but the step is this Cayley map, the implicit-midpoint step of the LQR flow, and not its exact sample $e^{-hR^{-1}P}q$.
  • Rectangle rule. The rectangle rule (4) for a quadratic Lagrangian, $L_d=\tfrac1{2h}(q'-q)^\top R\,(q'-q)+\tfrac h2q^\top Qq$, maps quadratics to quadratics. With $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$, the stationarity condition $\tfrac Rh(q^+-q)+Xq^+=0$ gives $q^+=\big(\tfrac Rh+X\big)^{-1}\tfrac Rh\,q$, and substituting back, $$\mathcal B\big(\tfrac12q^\top Xq\big)=\tfrac12q^\top \mathcal R_h(X)\,q,\qquad \mathcal R_h(X)=hQ+\tfrac Rh-\tfrac Rh\Big(\tfrac Rh+X\Big)^{-1}\tfrac Rh ,\tag{36}$$ the one-step Riccati map. The rectangle rule is the discrete LQ problem $q'=q+hv$ with stage cost $\tfrac h2(q^\top Qq+v^\top Rv)$, so the fixed point of $\mathcal R_h$ is the solution $P_d$ of its discrete algebraic Riccati equation (Anderson & Moore, 1990), and $P_d\neq P$.

The threshold. By (9) and its converse, $W=\tfrac12q^\top Xq$ satisfies (8) if and only if $\mathcal R_h(X)\preceq X$. The map $\mathcal R_h$ is monotone ($X_a\preceq X_b$ implies $\mathcal R_h(X_a)\preceq\mathcal R_h(X_b)$, the matrix form of the monotonicity of $\mathcal B$), so $\mathcal R_h(X)\preceq X$ gives $\mathcal R_h^{k+1}(X)\preceq\mathcal R_h^k(X)$ for every $k$. The sequence decreases and is bounded below by $hQ$, so it converges to a fixed point, and $P_d$ is the only positive-definite one. Hence $P_d=\lim_k\mathcal R_h^k(X)\preceq X$, and the scaled weight $W=\tfrac{c}{2}q^\top Pq$ can satisfy (8) only if $cP\succeq P_d$, i.e. $c\ge\lambda_{\max}(P^{-1}P_d)$. This is necessary in general. For $\dot q=v$ with the rectangle rule it is also sufficient, in any dimension: in the coordinates $R^{1/2}q$ the matrices $P$, $P_d$ and $\mathcal R_h(cP)$ are all functions of $R^{-1/2}QR^{-1/2}$, so the condition splits into scalar ones. For general plants and discretizations only necessity holds. In the scalar case ($Q=\kappa$, $R=m$) $\mathcal R_h(X)=h\kappa+{mX/(m+hX)}$, whose fixed point solves $X^2-h\kappa X-m\kappa=0$:

$$ P_d=\tfrac12\Big(h\kappa+\sqrt{h^2\kappa^2+4m\kappa}\Big), \tag{37} $$

that is $P_d=\tfrac12\big(h+\sqrt{h^2+4}\big)$ for $m=\kappa=1$, against $P=\sqrt{m\kappa}=1$. There $\mathcal R_h(X)-X$ decreases in $X$, so $c\ge P_d/P$ is also enough: for $m=\kappa=1$ the threshold $P_d/P$ is $1.05125$ at $h=0.1$ and $1.28078$ at $h=0.5$. (With an active constraint the fixed point is the remaining action of the constrained problem; Section 7.3.)

Practical consequence. Pairing a rectangle-rule $L_d$ with the continuous-time Riccati weight is a natural, and wrong, shortcut: the state still converges, but the Lyapunov certificate (11) fails, at every step of the example below.

6.5 Window and tail

Section 3.4 said that the window length moves the cut between the computed part of the remaining action (the window) and the estimated part (the tail $W$), while the certificate stays the same. With an exact pair, $W=V_\infty$ for the discrete problem and no active constraint, the fixed-point property gives $V_N=V_\infty$ for every $N$: only the cut moves. With an inexact pair the total itself changes with $N$. If the pair passes the one-step test (8), the total can only fall, by (10). If it fails (8), then $V_1>W$ somewhere on $\Omega$ (Section 2.3), and (10) fails already at $N=1$. The textbook weight of Section 6.4 is such a pair, with no terminal region: by (36), $V_1=\tfrac12\mathcal R_h(1)\,q^2$ with $\mathcal R_h(1)=1+h^2/(1+h)>1$, so $V_1\ge V_0$ everywhere, and the induction of Section 3.1 with the inequality reversed gives $V_{N+1}=\mathcal BV_N\ge\mathcal BV_{N-1}=V_N$: the total rises with $N$, the discrete mirror of $c<1$ in (30). 7. Inequalities: Reactions, Saturation and the Exact Boundary Term

Speed limits, torque limits and the terminal region are inequalities. Sections 2–⁠4 already allowed them (admissible pairs, the set $\mathcal V(q)$, the region $\Omega$); this section says what they change. Multipliers acquire signs and enter the momenta as reactions, the rule saturates, and the exact boundary term changes.

7.1 One step with active constraints

Write the admissibility of the step and the terminal region as inequalities $\chi_i(q,q')\le0$ on the free next point $q'$. If the gradients of the active ones are linearly independent, the minimizer $q^+$ of the one-step problem (13) satisfies the KKT conditions

$$ \begin{gathered} \partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)+\sum_i\mu_i\nabla_{q'}\chi_i(q,q^+)=0,\\ \mu_i\ge0,\qquad \chi_i(q,q^+)\le0,\qquad \mu_i\,\chi_i(q,q^+)=0, \end{gathered} \tag{38} $$

the explicit form of the inclusion $0\in\partial_2L_d(q,q^+)+\nabla W(q^+)+\mathcal N_{\mathcal C}(q^+)$, with $\mathcal N_{\mathcal C}$ the normal cone of the feasible set $\mathcal C$ of next points (for a longer window, $W$ is replaced by $V_{N-1}$). Without active constraints (38) is (34). By (32) the arrival momentum becomes $p^+=-\nabla W(q^+)-\sum_i\mu_i\nabla_{q'}\chi_i(q,q^+)$: minus the gradient of the boundary term, corrected by the reactions of the active constraints. Two cases recur below.

  • A bound on the step. Take the rectangle rule of Section 6.4, $L_d=\tfrac1{2h}(q'-q)^2+\tfrac h2q^2$, with $W=\tfrac12q^2$ and $h=0.1$, and add the speed limit $q-q'\le h\bar v$ with $\bar v=0.5$. From $q=1$ the unconstrained minimizer solves $(q^+-q)/h+q^+=0$, i.e. $q^+=1/(1+h)=0.909$, which violates the bound; so the bound is active, $q^+=0.95$, and (38) with $\nabla_{q'}\chi=-1$ gives $\mu=(q^+-q)/h+q^+=0.45$. The arrival momentum is $p^+=(q^+-q)/h=-0.50$ instead of $-W'(q^+)=-0.95$.
  • The terminal region as a sublevel set, $W(q')\le\bar W$. Its gradient is $\nabla W$ itself, so $p^+=-(1+\mu)\nabla W(q^+)$: an active terminal constraint acts like a locally inflated boundary term $(1+\mu)W$, a scaled boundary term $cW$ with $c=1+\mu$ chosen by the optimizer (compare the inflation of Section 6.3). Section 11.7 measures $\mu$ on the arm.

7.2 Constrained velocities in the infinitesimal rule

If the admissible velocities form a closed convex set $\mathcal V(q)$, Sections 4.1–⁠4.5 hold as written, with the constrained Hamiltonian $H_{\mathcal V}$ of (17):

$$ v^*(q)=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L+\nabla W\cdot v\big],\qquad \min_{v\in\mathcal V(q)}\big[L+\dot W\big]\le0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H_{\mathcal V}\big(q,-\nabla W\big)\ge0 , \tag{39} $$

and (21) holds with $H_{\mathcal V}$. Three things change.

  • The argmin is a projection. For $L$ quadratic in $v$ with weight $R$, $L+\nabla W\cdot v=\tfrac12(v-v_u)^\top R\,(v-v_u)+\text{const}$ with $v_u$ the unconstrained minimizer, so $v^*$ is the $R$-metric projection of $v_u$ onto $\mathcal V(q)$. For a box and a diagonal $R$ this is componentwise clipping, a saturated law; for a non-diagonal $R$ it is not (Section 11.7).
  • The Legendre map saturates. Where the constraint is active, $v^*$ sits on the boundary of $\mathcal V(q)$ and no longer follows the momentum. There the problem behaves like the singular case of Section 4.2; an interior, momentum-determined velocity survives only where the constraint is inactive.
  • The certificate becomes stronger. Since $H_{\mathcal V}\le H$, condition (39) implies (22) but not conversely: a boundary term that certifies the unconstrained rule can fail once the constraint binds. Constraints shrink the certified region; they add no new mechanism.

7.3 Worked example: the hilltop with a speed limit

The fixed-point statement of Section 6.3 needs care with an active constraint. With $W$ equal to the remaining action of the constrained problem, the principle of optimality still gives $V_1=W$. The Riccati weight equals it only where the unconstrained optimal path stays admissible; elsewhere it under-estimates the tail, exactly like $c<1$, and the certificate fails even when the rule’s argmin is optimal. The hilltop shows this in closed form. Add the bound $|v|\le\bar v$ to the example (3), so $\mathcal V=[-\bar v,\bar v]$, and write $q_s=\bar v/\omega$. The supremum in (17) is attained at $v=p/m$ if that is admissible and at $v=\bar v\,\mathrm{sign}(p)$ otherwise, so the constrained Hamiltonian is

$$ H_{\mathcal V}(q,p)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p^2}{2m}-\tfrac12\kappa q^2, & |p|\le m\bar v,\\[6pt] \bar v\,|p|-\tfrac12m\bar v^2-\tfrac12\kappa q^2, & |p|>m\bar v.\end{cases} \tag{40} $$
  1. The rule saturates. With $W=V$ of (29) the infinitesimal-window rule (39) is $v^*=\mathrm{clip}(-\omega q,\,-\bar v,\,\bar v)$: the unconstrained law, clipped.
  2. The unconstrained remaining action no longer certifies it. With $p=-V'=-\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q$, the condition $|p|\le m\bar v$ is $|q|\le q_s$. For $|q|\le q_s$ nothing changes, $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=0$. For $|q|>q_s$, $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=-\tfrac12{\big(\sqrt\kappa\,|q|-\sqrt m\,\bar v\big)^2}<0$: condition (39) fails, although the state converges. With $m=\kappa=1$ and $\bar v=0.5$, at $q=1$ the value is $-0.125$.
  3. The constrained remaining action does certify it. Solving $H_{\mathcal V}(q,-V')=0$ on the saturated branch outside $q_s$ gives $V'(q)=\big(\tfrac12m\bar v^2+\tfrac12\kappa q^2\big)/\bar v$ there, hence $$V_{\mathcal V}(q)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q^2, & |q|\le q_s,\\[2pt] \tfrac12\sqrt{m\kappa}\,q_s^2+\tfrac12m\bar v\,(|q|-q_s)+\dfrac{\kappa\,(|q|^3-q_s^3)}{6\bar v}, & |q|>q_s.\end{cases}\tag{41}$$ It is $C^2$ at $q_s$ (both one-sided second derivatives equal $\sqrt{m\kappa}$) but not $C^3$, and it exceeds $V$ outside $q_s$: $V_{\mathcal V}(1)=0.5417$ against $V(1)=0.5$. Its own rule is again the clipped law, which is therefore optimal: integrating $L$ along the clipped law from $q=1$ gives $0.5417$.
The controller was optimal all along; only the boundary term was wrong. The clipped law is the exact infinite-window optimum, yet the unconstrained remaining action does not certify it wherever the speed limit binds, because it under-estimates the tail there, like $W=cV_\infty$ with $c<1$ in Section 4.6. An active constraint changes which boundary term is exact. In general the constrained remaining action is only piecewise smooth, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation has to be read in a generalized (viscosity) sense (Liberzon, 2012, §5.3); here it holds classically.
What Part I has established, from the principle of optimality alone.
  1. With a boundary term that passes the one-step test (8), the remaining action is a Lyapunov function of the receding window for every window length, (11).
  2. In the continuous limit the test is the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality (22) on the pair $(L,W)$; the infinitesimal window yields a controller (the argmin) and a certificate (the sign of the minimum), and the certificate at $\vartheta=0$ covers every window, (24).
  3. Along optimal paths the momentum is $p=-\nabla V$, the paths are the extremals of the action, and the boundary term fixes the arrival momentum, (27).
  4. Where the terminal constraint is inactive, one step is exact if and only if $W$ is a fixed point of the Bellman operator of the chosen discrete Lagrangian. With $L_d^E$ that operator is the continuous problem over one step, so $V_\infty$ is a fixed point; for a quadratic Lagrangian the continuous-time Riccati weight is also a fixed point for the midpoint rule (whose step is a Cayley map, not the exact sample of the LQR flow), but not for the rectangle rule, whose fixed point is $\tfrac12q^\top P_dq$ with $P_d\neq P$.
  5. Constraints add reactions to the momenta, saturate the rule, strengthen the certificate and change the exact boundary term.
Part II · Mechanical plants: momenta and forces

8. Second-Order Plants: Multipliers Are Momenta

Part I assumed that the velocity is the decision variable. A robot does not work that way: we choose a torque, the torque sets an acceleration, and position and velocity follow. This section redoes the construction of Part I for such Newtonian plants. The recursion and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation carry over with $q$ replaced by the phase-space point $z=(q,v)$; what needs new work is the momentum. The classical tool for it is a Lagrange multiplier attached to every equation of motion, and the multipliers turn out to be the momenta.

8.1 Setting, and what carries over

The state is now the pair $z=(q,v)\in\mathbb R^{2n}$, and the decision variable is a control $u\in\mathbb R^{n_u}$ that acts on the acceleration. The plant and the action are

$$ \dot q=v,\qquad \dot v=f(q,v,u),\qquad S=\int_t^{t_f}\ell(q,v,u)\,dt'+W\big(z(t_f)\big). \tag{42} $$

A typical $f$ is the manipulator equation $M(q)\dot v+b(q,v)=\tau$. Here $M(q)$ is the inertia matrix of the kinetic energy $\mathcal T=\tfrac12v^\top M(q)v$, and $b=C(q,v)v+g(q)$ collects the Coriolis, centrifugal and gravity terms, with $g=\nabla U$ the gradient of the potential energy $U(q)$. Taking the gravity-compensated torque $u=\tau-g(q)$ as the control gives $f=M(q)^{-1}\big(u-C(q,v)v\big)$. Along solutions $v=\dot q$; we write $v$ where the velocity is a state variable (in $\ell$, $W$, $f$, $b$ and their gradients) and keep $\dot q$ in equations of motion, powers and control laws. The stage cost $\ell$ takes over the role of $L$. The standing assumptions are the analogues of Section 1.2: $f(0,0,0)=0$ (the rest point is an equilibrium under zero control, which the gravity compensation in $u$ provides), $\ell(0,0,0)=0$, $\ell\ge\alpha_\ell(|z|)$ with $\alpha_\ell\in\mathcal K_\infty$; $f$ and $\ell$ are continuously differentiable; $f$ is affine in $u$, $f=f_0(q,v)+f_u(q,v)\,u$ (for the manipulator above, $f_0=-M^{-1}Cv$ and $f_u=M^{-1}$); $\ell$ is strictly convex and superlinear in $u$, as $L$ is in $v$ (superlinearity is not needed where the admissible inputs $\mathcal U(z)$ of Section 8.2 form a compact convex set); and $W$ is as in Section 1.2. Then the bracket $\pi_v\cdot f-\ell$ is strictly concave in $u$ and tends to $-\infty$ as $|u|\to\infty$, so the supremum over $u$ in the Hamiltonian of Section 8.2 is finite and attained at a single $u^*$. The remaining action $V(z)$ is the least value of $S$ from the phase-space point $z$.

What carries over without any work. Sections 2 and 3 used only the transition cost $L_d(z,z')$, the least action of an admissible transition from $z$ to $z'$ (infinite if there is none). Replace $q$ by $z$, let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^{2n}$ be a phase-space region and $\varphi_f$ a reference flow on it (for instance a stabilizing state feedback), and (6), (8), (11) and (12) hold verbatim. Assumption A3 now needs $L_d(z,z')\ge\alpha_L(|z|)$, i.e. a stage cost that penalizes position and velocity. If $\ell$ ignores $v$, positivity in $z$ fails and asymptotic stability needs a detectability (LaSalle-type) argument instead; Section 8.5 has an example.

What does not carry over directly is the momentum. Why not simply treat $z$ as the configuration and $\dot z$ as its velocity, and reuse Section 4.2? Because half of $\dot z$ is not free. The first block, $\dot q$, is pinned to the state component $v$; only the second block, $\dot v$, can be influenced, and only through $u$. Written as a function of $(z,\dot z)$, the stage cost is

$$ L_z(z,\dot z)= \begin{cases} \inf_u\big\{\ell(q,v,u):\ \dot v=f(q,v,u)\big\}, & \dot q=v,\\[2pt] +\infty, & \dot q\neq v . \end{cases} \tag{43} $$

This Lagrangian is finite only on an affine subspace of velocities. It has no derivative with respect to $\dot q$, so the momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot q$ of Section 4.2 is undefined, and the Legendre map cannot be inverted: no momentum can tell the plant to move $q$ at any rate other than $v$. The way out is to let the Hamilton–Jacobi equation define the momentum, as it did in Part I, and to enforce the dynamics with multipliers.

8.2 The Hamilton–Jacobi equation in phase space

Repeat the derivation of Section 4.1 with $z$ in place of $q$ and the control $u$ held constant over a first step of length $h$. The step costs $h\,\ell(z,u)+O(h^2)$ and, because the velocity changes during it, arrives at $z+h\dot z+O(h^2)$ with $\dot z=(v,f(z,u))$; the same expansion and the limit $h\to0$ give $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta=\min_u\big[\ell+\nabla_zV_\vartheta\cdot\dot z\big]$. Write $\pi:=-\nabla_zV$, the momentum (16) of this window, now with a position component $\pi_q$ and a velocity component $\pi_v$. The minimum is minus the supremum

$$ H(z,\pi)=\sup_u\Big[\,\pi_q\cdot v+\pi_v\cdot f(q,v,u)-\ell(q,v,u)\,\Big], \tag{44} $$

the Hamiltonian of a second-order plant, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation reads $\partial_\vartheta V_\vartheta=-H(z,-\nabla_zV_\vartheta)$, exactly as (18). In Part I the supremum ran over the velocity, which was chosen freely. Here $\dot q=v$ is pinned and only $\dot v=f$ moves, through $u$. So the supremum runs over $u$, the term $\pi_q\cdot v$ sits in $H$ untouched, and $H$ is linear in $\pi_q$. It is still the Legendre transform of the degenerate Lagrangian (43): in $\sup_{\dot z}[\pi\cdot\dot z-L_z(z,\dot z)]$ the value $+\infty$ forces $\dot q=v$, and the infimum over $u$ inside $L_z$ becomes the supremum over $u$. The transform survives; what is lost is its inverse, since $\partial H/\partial\pi_q=v$ merely restates $\dot q=v$.

With $\pi=-\nabla_zW$, the infinitesimal-window rule of Section 4.4 and the rate of change of $W$ along it are

$$ \begin{gathered} u^*(z)=\arg\max_u\big[\pi_v\cdot f(z,u)-\ell(z,u)\big]_{\pi=-\nabla W},\\ \dot W=\nabla_zW\cdot\dot z\big|_{u^*}=-\ell(z,u^*)-H\big(z,-\nabla_zW\big), \end{gathered} \tag{45} $$

which is (21) with $q\to z$. Only $\pi_v$ enters the choice of $u$: the control is set by the sensitivity of $W$ (when $W=V_\infty$, of the remaining action) to velocity, because velocity is what the control acts on. The rest of Section 4 follows by the same lines:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{stability condition:} && \min_u\big[\ell+\dot W\big]\le0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H\big(z,-\nabla_z W(z)\big)\ \ge\ 0,\\ &\text{stationary HJ:} && H\big(z,-\nabla_zV_\infty\big)=0,\\ &\text{transversality:} && \pi(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(z(t_f)\big). \end{aligned} \tag{46} $$

The receding-window identity (19) and the propagation argument of Section 4.5 carry over verbatim, so the first line certifies every window length. Along an optimal path $\dot V=-\ell$ (Section 5.1), and $H=0$ for the infinite window because $H$ is conserved along extremals of an autonomous problem and $H(0,0)=-\inf_u\ell(0,0,u)=0$. The value of the bracket at its minimizer, $\mathcal D(z)=\min_u[\ell+\dot W]=-H(z,-\nabla_zW)$, the descent defect, is the form in which the certificate is used from Section 9 on: it must be non-positive.

With input constraints $u\in\mathcal U(z)$, take the supremum in (44) over $\mathcal U(z)$ (written $H_{\mathcal U}$, with descent defect $\mathcal D_{\mathcal U}$), as in Section 7.2: (45) and (46) keep their form, and the maximum condition $u^*\in\arg\max_{u\in\mathcal U(z)}[\pi_v\cdot f-\ell]$ is Pontryagin’s form of the principle, which needs no differentiability in $u$. State constraints are harder: in continuous time their multipliers are measures, and the momenta can jump where the path meets the constraint (Hartl et al., 1995), the optimal-control counterpart of an impact. The discrete version of Section 8.7 keeps the bookkeeping elementary.

8.3 The multipliers are the momenta

Section 5.1 followed the Hamilton–Jacobi equation along the optimal path and found Hamilton’s equations for $p=-\nabla V$. The same three steps in $z$ (differentiate the equation, use $\dot z=\partial H/\partial\pi$ along the path, cancel the Hessian terms) give, for $\pi:=-\nabla_zV$ along an optimal path,

$$ \dot z=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\pi},\qquad\dot\pi=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial z},\qquad\pi(t_f)=-\nabla_zW\big(z(t_f)\big). \tag{47} $$

By the envelope theorem the derivatives of (44) are those of its bracket at the maximizing $u^*$, so $\dot z=\partial H/\partial\pi$ is the plant (42), and the other equations, written out, are

$$ \begin{aligned} \dot\pi_q&=\nabla_q\ell-f_q^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{from }-\partial H/\partial q),\\ \dot\pi_v&=\nabla_v\ell-\pi_q-f_v^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{from }-\partial H/\partial v),\\ 0&=\nabla_u\ell-f_u^{\top}\pi_v &&(\text{maximizing }u), \end{aligned} \tag{48} $$

where $f_q,f_v,f_u$ are Jacobians. These are exactly the costate equations and the stationarity condition of Pontryagin’s principle (Pontryagin et al., 1962; Bryson & Ho, 1975; Liberzon, 2012). For an infinite window, apply the argument to a finite window with $W=V_\infty$ and no terminal constraint: by the principle of optimality its remaining action is $V_\infty$ for every length ($c=1$ in (30)), so $\pi=-\nabla_zV_\infty$ along the finite-action path.

The same equations come out of a variational principle once the dynamics are made visible to it. Attach a multiplier to each equation of motion, writing the constraints as “velocity minus model” so that the signs of Part I survive, and call the multipliers $\pi_q$ and $\pi_v$:

$$ \tilde L(q,v,u,\pi;\dot q,\dot v)=\ell(q,v,u)+\pi_q\cdot(\dot q-v)+\pi_v\cdot\big(\dot v-f(q,v,u)\big). \tag{49} $$

On every path that obeys the plant the two brackets vanish, so $\int\tilde L\,dt=\int\ell\,dt$: nothing has changed except that the dynamics are now visible to the variational calculus. $\tilde L$ depends on the velocities $\dot q,\dot v$ only linearly, through $\pi_q\cdot\dot q+\pi_v\cdot\dot v$, so the canonical momenta of $\tilde L$ are the multipliers themselves: $\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot q=\pi_q$, $\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot v=\pi_v$. Treat $q$, $v$, $u$, $\pi_q$, $\pi_v$ as independent functions of time and write the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\tilde L$ for each: the rate of change of the momentum conjugate to a variable equals the derivative of $\tilde L$ with respect to that variable. For $q$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\,\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot q=\partial\tilde L/\partial q$ reads $\dot\pi_q=\nabla_q\ell-f_q^\top\pi_v$, the first line of (48); that is why $\pi$ carries a dot. The variations of $v$ and $u$ give the other two lines (the last is algebraic because $\tilde L$ contains no $\dot u$), and the variations of the multipliers give back the plant. In the variation of the action the derivative arrives by integration by parts, $\int\pi_q\cdot\delta\dot q\,dt=\big[\pi_q\cdot\delta q\big]-\int\dot\pi_q\cdot\delta q\,dt$, and the boundary term, which must cancel $\nabla W\cdot\delta z(t_f)$, gives the transversality condition in (47). Finally, the Legendre transform of $\tilde L$ is (44): the $\dot z$ terms cancel in ${\pi\cdot\dot z}-\tilde L={\pi_q\cdot v}+{\pi_v\cdot f}-\ell$, and the supremum over $u$, whose stationarity condition is the third line of (48), gives (44).

The two derivations meet: the Lagrange multipliers of the equations of motion, adjoined with this sign, are the momenta $\pi=-\nabla_zV$. The calculus-of-variations derivation of optimal control introduces the costates in exactly this way, as Lagrange multipliers of the dynamics adjoined to the cost (Bryson & Ho, 1975).

Sign convention. Along an optimal path the multipliers equal minus the gradient of the remaining action, $\pi=(\pi_q,\pi_v)=\bigl(-\nabla_qV,\,-\nabla_vV\bigr)$, the analogue of $p=-\nabla V$ in Part I; Section 8.7 derives the same sign from the dynamic-programming recursion. 8.4 Fully actuated plants: a higher-order Lagrangian and Ostrogradsky’s momenta

When the control can produce any acceleration, i.e. $f_u$ is invertible, the control can be eliminated: $u$ is a function of $(q,\dot q,\ddot q)$. The cleanest case is the double integrator $f=u$ (which is also what computed-torque control makes of a manipulator); for a manipulator one substitutes $\tau=M(q)\ddot q+b(q,\dot q)$ instead. The stage cost then becomes a Lagrangian that depends on the acceleration:

$$ \hat\ell(q,\dot q,\ddot q)=\ell(q,\dot q,u)\big|_{u=\ddot q},\qquad p_1=\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q},\qquad p_2=\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q}. \tag{50} $$

Mechanics has had a Hamiltonian theory for such Lagrangians since Ostrogradsky (1850). Its canonical coordinates are $(q,\dot q)$ and the Ostrogradsky momenta $p_1,p_2$ of (50). They are not a new construction; they are our multipliers. With $f=u$, (48) reads $0=\nabla_u\ell-\pi_v$, $\dot\pi_v=\nabla_v\ell-\pi_q$ and $\dot\pi_q=\nabla_q\ell$. The first equation gives $\pi_v=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q=p_2$; solving the second for $\pi_q$ gives $\pi_q=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\dot q-\tfrac{d}{dt}\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q=p_1$; and the third becomes the Euler–Poisson equation of a second-order Lagrangian (Gelfand & Fomin, 1963):

$$ \frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\dot q} +\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\frac{\partial\hat\ell}{\partial\ddot q}=0 . \tag{51} $$

The Ostrogradsky Hamiltonian $p_1\cdot\dot q+p_2\cdot\ddot q-\hat\ell$, with $\ddot q$ eliminated through $p_2=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q$, is (44) with $f=u$:

$$ \begin{gathered} H(q,v,p_1,p_2)=p_1\cdot v+\sup_a\big[\,p_2\cdot a-\hat\ell(q,v,a)\,\big],\\ (p_1,p_2)=(\pi_q,\pi_v)=-\nabla_zV\quad\text{on optimal paths}. \end{gathered} \tag{52} $$

So for a fully actuated plant, the momentum structure of Part I holds word for word, with Ostrogradsky’s momenta in place of $p=\partial L/\partial v$. The non-degeneracy condition of Ostrogradsky’s theory, invertibility of $\partial^2\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q^2$, asks a little more than our strict convexity of $\ell$ in $u$. For $f=u$, and for the manipulator, where the substitution $\tau=M\ddot q+b$ makes $u=\tau-g$ affine in $\ddot q$ with $\partial u/\partial\ddot q=M$, strict convexity makes $p_2=\partial\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q$ one-to-one in $\ddot q$, so $\ddot q$ is eliminated uniquely through $p_2$, as in (52). By the chain rule the Hessian is $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2$ for $f=u$ and $M\,(\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2)\,M$ for the manipulator; since $\ell$ is convex in $u$, it is invertible exactly where $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$. Positive definiteness of $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2$ for all $u$ implies strict convexity, but strict convexity does not imply it: with $f=u$, the cost $\ell=\tfrac12|z|^2+\tfrac14|u|^4$ meets every assumption of Section 8.1, yet $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2=|u|^2I+2uu^\top$ vanishes at $u=0$, and its Hamiltonian $H=\pi_q\cdot v+\tfrac34|\pi_v|^{4/3}-\tfrac12|z|^2$ is not twice differentiable where $\pi_v=0$. The quadratic costs used here have $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$ ($w_u>0$ for the double integrator of Section 8.5, $R\succ0$ for the manipulator of Section 8.6), and for a smooth $\ell$ this makes $H$ twice differentiable, as the linearization at the rest point below requires. (Outside the assumptions of Section 8.1, if $f$ is nonlinear in $u$, the curvature of $f$ enters as well, $\partial^2\hat\ell/\partial\ddot q^2=f_u^{-\top}\,\partial_u^2\big[\ell-p_2\cdot f\big]\,f_u^{-1}$ with $p_2$ held fixed, and $\partial^2\ell/\partial u^2\succ0$ no longer suffices.)

Is the Ostrogradsky instability a problem here? Ostrogradsky’s theorem says that the Hamiltonian (52) of a non-degenerate higher-derivative Lagrangian is linear in $p_1$, hence unbounded below. In physics, where this $H$ is the conserved energy, there is then no lowest-energy state; in interacting theories this generically lets positive- and negative-energy degrees of freedom grow together without limit (Woodard, 2015). Three things are different here, and they should be kept apart:
  • $H$ is not our Lyapunov candidate. The Lyapunov function is $V$. It is non-negative because $\ell\ge0$, and $\dot V=-\ell$ by (45)–⁠(46); neither fact needs $H$ to be bounded. Boundedness of $H$ would matter only if we wanted to use $H$ itself as an energy.
  • The extremal flow is unstable, but that is not specific to higher order. The linearized state–momentum flow at the rest point has its eigenvalues in pairs $\pm\sigma$, so under the usual stabilizability and detectability assumptions exactly half of them lie in the right half-plane. This is already so for the first-order hilltop of Section 5.2, whose $H$ is unbounded below as well. The boundary conditions select the extremal. On an infinite window, finite action selects the stable manifold, on which $H=0$ (Section 8.5). On a finite window whose endpoint lies in the interior of $\Omega$, transversality $\pi(t_f)=-\nabla W\big(z(t_f)\big)$ puts the window’s path on that manifold exactly when $\nabla W=\nabla V_\infty$ at $z(t_f)$, in particular for $W=V_\infty$ (Sections 5.3 and 8.3). Otherwise, $W=0$ included, the path lies off the manifold, and its conserved Hamiltonian keeps the terminal value $H\big(z(t_f),-\nabla W(z(t_f))\big)=-\mathcal D\big(z(t_f)\big)$, which is in general not zero: by (44) it is $-\inf_u\ell\big(z(t_f),u\big)<0$ for $W=0$, and by (26) it is $\tfrac{c^2-1}{2}\,\kappa\,q(t_f)^2$ for the hilltop with $W=cV_\infty$. The receding loop applies only the start of that path and re-solves, so the departure from the manifold is never followed; and when $\nabla_zV_\vartheta\to\nabla_zV_\infty$ as the window lengthens, as for the hilltop by (30), the start, whose momentum is $-\nabla_zV_\vartheta$, approaches the manifold.
  • Linearity in $p_1$ is the kinematic constraint. The canonical equation $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p_1=v$ is just $\dot q=v$. What Section 4.2 calls singular, linearity in the decision variable, would be a problem; linearity in a momentum is not.
So the accurate statement is: the closed loop is stable because the remaining action of a positive-definite cost decreases along the selected flow, not because any Hamiltonian is bounded. It is not a claim that the extremal (state–momentum) dynamics are stable: with eigenvalues in $\pm\sigma$ pairs they are never asymptotically stable.

8.5 Worked example: the double integrator

This example checks (46), the sign convention $\pi=-\nabla_zV$ and Ostrogradsky’s momenta (50) on a linear, fully actuated plant with an infinite window. Take a unit mass driven by a force, $\dot q=v$, $\dot v=u$ (scalar), with

$$ \begin{gathered} \ell=\tfrac12\big(w_q\,q^2+w_v\,v^2+w_u\,u^2\big),\quad w_q,w_u>0,\ w_v\ge0,\\ H(z,\pi)=\pi_q v+\frac{\pi_v^2}{2w_u}-\tfrac12\big(w_qq^2+w_vv^2\big), \end{gathered} \tag{53} $$

where $H$ follows from (44): the supremum of $\pi_vu-\tfrac {w_u}2u^2$ is attained at $u^*=\pi_v/w_u$. Note the linearity in $\pi_q$. In matrix form $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, $Q=\mathrm{diag}(w_q,w_v)$, $R=w_u$.

  1. Riccati solution in closed form. Try $V=\tfrac12z^\top Pz$ for the remaining action of the infinite window. The stationary equation in (46) becomes the algebraic Riccati equation $A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0$, which splits into three scalar equations, $P_{12}^2=w_u w_q$, $P_{11}=P_{12}P_{22}/w_u$, $P_{22}^2=w_u(w_v+2P_{12})$, whose positive solution is
    $$ \begin{gathered} P_{12}=\sqrt{w_u w_q},\qquad P_{22}=\sqrt{w_u\big(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q}\big)},\\ P_{11}=\sqrt{w_q\big(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q}\big)} . \end{gathered} \tag{54} $$
  2. Momenta and control. $\pi=-Pz$, i.e. $\pi_q=-(P_{11}q+P_{12}v)$, $\pi_v=-(P_{12}q+P_{22}v)$, and $u^*=\pi_v/w_u=-w_u^{-1}B^\top Pz$, the LQR law. Only the velocity momentum $\pi_v$ sets the force.
  3. Hamilton–Jacobi. Substituting $\pi=-Pz$ into (53) gives $H(z,-\nabla V)\equiv0$, and $\dot V=z^\top P(A-BK)z=-\ell(z,u^*)$ with $K=w_u^{-1}B^\top P$ (both identities hold symbolically for all $w_q,w_v,w_u$).
  4. The Hamiltonian matrix. Hamilton’s equations (47) of (53) are linear, $\tfrac{d}{dt}(z,\pi)=\Xi\,(z,\pi)$ with
    $$ \Xi=\begin{pmatrix}A&BR^{-1}B^\top\\ Q&-A^\top\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(\sigma I-\Xi)=\frac{w_u \sigma^4-w_v \sigma^2+w_q}{w_u}. \tag{55} $$
    The roots come in pairs $\pm\sigma$. The graph $\{\pi=-Pz\}$ is invariant, $\Xi\binom{I}{-P}=\binom{I}{-P}(A-BK)$, and it is the stable invariant subspace (Laub, 1979; Anderson & Moore, 1990): the closed loop has $\det(\sigma I-A+BK)=\sigma^2+\sqrt{(w_v+2\sqrt{w_u w_q})/w_u}\,\sigma+\sqrt{w_q/w_u}$, whose roots are the left-half-plane roots of (55). For $A=0$, $B=I$ this reduces to the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&R^{-1}\\Q&0\end{pmatrix}$ of Section 5.3.
  5. Ostrogradsky check. With $\hat\ell=\tfrac12(w_qq^2+w_v\dot q^2+w_u\ddot q^2)$, (50) gives $p_2=w_u\ddot q$ and $p_1=w_v\dot q-w_u\,q^{(3)}$. Along the closed loop $\ddot q=-(P_{12}q+P_{22}v)/w_u$, so $p_2=\pi_v$ at once; differentiating once more and using $P_{22}^2/w_u=w_v+2P_{12}$ and $P_{11}=P_{12}P_{22}/w_u$ gives $p_1=-(P_{11}q+P_{12}v)=\pi_q$.
  6. Scaled candidates. For $W=c\,V$, $c>0$, using the Riccati equation, $H(z,-c\nabla V)=\tfrac{c-1}{2}\big[c\,w_u^{-1}(B^\top Pz)^2+z^\top Qz\big]$. So the stability condition in (46) holds exactly when $c\ge1$ (for $Q\succ0$), as in the scaled candidates of Section 4.6. With $A=0$, $B=I$ the bracket collapses to $(c+1)\,z^\top Qz$ and that formula is recovered.

8.6 The manipulator: the torque is the velocity momentum

For $M(q)\dot v+b(q,v)=\tau$ with stage cost $\ell=\ell_0(q,v)+\tfrac12\tau^\top R\,\tau$, $R\succ0$, the supremum in (44) is a concave quadratic in $\tau$. Setting its gradient $M^{-1}\pi_v-R\tau$ to zero ($M$ is symmetric) gives

$$ \tau^*=R^{-1}M(q)^{-1}\pi_v,\qquad H=\pi_q\cdot v-\pi_v\cdot M^{-1}b+\tfrac12\,\pi_v^\top M^{-1}R^{-1}M^{-1}\pi_v-\ell_0(q,v). \tag{56} $$

With the plain torque cost $R=I$, the optimal torque is $M^{-1}\pi_v$. In every case the conclusion of Section 8.2 is visible directly: the torque is dictated by $\pi_v=-\nabla_vV$, how much the remaining action would drop if the joints moved slightly faster. A holding torque against gravity is included by penalizing $\tau-g(q)$ instead of $\tau$, which adds $g(q)$ to $\tau^*$; more generally, centring the cost on any given torque adds that torque. One weight makes the inertia disappear from the optimal torque in (56), $R\propto M^{-1}$: with $R=T_0M^{-1}$, $T_0>0$ a time constant, $\tau^*=\pi_v/T_0$. It is Gauss’s weight, and it is the bridge to whole-body control (Section 9).

8.7 Discrete version: multiple shooting and its multipliers

A digital controller holds the control constant over each step and integrates the plant over it. Call the result $z_{j+1}=\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)$ (an exact flow, or a Runge–Kutta step) and let $\ell_d(z_j,u_j)$ be the stage cost of the step. Multiple shooting keeps every node $z_j$ as a separate unknown and imposes the step equations as continuity constraints, the “defects” $z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)=0$ (Bock & Plitt, 1984; Rawlings et al., 2017, Ch. 8). Attaching a multiplier to each defect, with the sign of (49), gives the discrete augmented action

$$ \tilde S_d=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\Big[\ell_d(z_j,u_j)+\pi_{j+1}\cdot\big(z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)\big)\Big]+W(z_N). \tag{57} $$

This is precisely the Lagrangian function of the multiple-shooting nonlinear program, and its stationarity conditions are the program’s KKT conditions. Differentiate with respect to the interior nodes, the last node and the controls:

$$ \begin{gathered} \pi_j=\partial_z\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}-\nabla_z\ell_d(z_j,u_j),\qquad \pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N),\\ \nabla_u\ell_d(z_j,u_j)=\partial_u\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}. \end{gathered} \tag{58} $$

The first equation is the discrete costate recursion, run backwards from the discrete transversality condition (the second, the phase-space form of $p_N=-\nabla W(q_N)$ in Section 6.2); the third is the discrete stationarity condition. Differentiating the dynamic-programming recursion $V_N(z)=\min_u\{\ell_d(z,u)+V_{N-1}(\Phi_h(z,u))\}$, $V_0=W$ on $\Omega$, with the envelope theorem, exactly as in Section 6.2, gives the same recursion for $-\nabla V_N$. Hence $\pi_j=-\nabla V_{N-j}(z_j)$: the defect multipliers are the discrete momenta (35), minus the gradient of the remaining action with $N-j$ steps to go. If the initial value is imposed as a constraint, with the same sign convention $\pi_0\cdot(z_0-\hat z)$, the node $z_0$ becomes an unknown and its stationarity condition is the first equation of (58) at $j=0$. The multiplier of the initial value is therefore the momentum at the current state, $\pi_0=-\nabla V_N(\hat z)$: the solver returns minus the gradient of the Lyapunov function at the current state for free.

How does this relate to the discrete Legendre transforms (32)? Define the transition cost on state pairs, $L_d(z,z')=\min_u\{\ell_d(z,u):\Phi_h(z,u)=z'\}$. Where it is differentiable, the multiplier rule and (58) give

$$ \pi_j=-\partial_1L_d(z_j,z_{j+1}),\qquad \pi_{j+1}=\partial_2L_d(z_j,z_{j+1}), \tag{59} $$

which is (32) with $q\to z$, $p\to\pi$. The caveat is in “where it is differentiable”. With a constant control over the step, the set of reachable $z'$ has dimension $n_u\le n$ inside the $2n$-dimensional state space, so $L_d=+\infty$ off a thin set and $\partial_2L_d$ does not exist. The multipliers of (57) are what replaces it. With the exact discrete Lagrangian (31), where the control may vary continuously within the step, $L_d$ is finite and smooth for a controllable plant and (59) holds literally; it is, however, stiff (for the double integrator the inverse controllability Gramian over one step has entries of order $h^{-3}$), so an endpoint pair inconsistent with the plant is very expensive rather than forbidden.

Discrete mechanics has been used for optimal control before: the DMOC approach discretizes the variational description of a mechanical system directly, and the resulting program is equivalent to the one obtained by discretizing Hamilton’s equations of the mechanical system itself, in configuration and mechanical momentum, by a symplectic partitioned Runge–Kutta scheme (Ober-Blöbaum et al., 2011). Here the discrete momenta are instead those of the remaining action, (35), read off the multipliers of a standard multiple-shooting program.

Inequalities. Input and state constraints $\chi(z_j,u_j)\le0$ and the terminal region $W(z_N)\le\bar W$ enter (57) with multipliers $\mu_j\ge0$, $\mu_N\ge0$ and complementarity, and (58) becomes

$$ \begin{gathered} \pi_j=\partial_z\Phi_h^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1}-\nabla_z\ell_d-\nabla_z\chi^\top\mu_j,\qquad \nabla_u\ell_d+\nabla_u\chi^\top\mu_j=\partial_u\Phi_h^{\!\top}\pi_{j+1},\\ \pi_N=-(1+\mu_N)\,\nabla W(z_N). \end{gathered} \tag{60} $$

Active state constraints change the momenta by their reactions; active input constraints change only the stationarity condition; an active terminal constraint scales the arrival momentum, as in Section 7.1: (60) is the multiple-shooting form of (38).

  1. Solve the window problem as an NLP in the nodes $z_1,\dots,z_N$ and controls $u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}$, with defect constraints $z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)=0$.
  2. Read the defect multipliers. If the solver forms its Lagrangian as cost $+\sum_j\psi_{j+1}^{\top}\big(z_{j+1}-\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)\big)$, then $\psi_{j+1}=\pi_{j+1}$. If it writes the defects as $\Phi_h(z_j,u_j)-z_{j+1}$, or subtracts the multiplier term, the sign flips: $\psi_{j+1}=-\pi_{j+1}=\nabla V_{N-j-1}(z_{j+1})$ is the minimum-principle costate $\eta$. Multipliers of inequalities carry the signs of (60) in the same convention.
  3. Check the convention once at the last node: $\pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N)$.

The discrete version was checked on the double integrator of Section 8.5 with step $h=0.2$, $N=10$, zero-order hold and the rectangle-rule stage cost $h\,\ell$. The KKT multipliers of the multiple-shooting problem (57) satisfy $\pi_j=-P_{N-j}z_j$, with $P_N$ the weight of $V_N=\tfrac12z^\top P_Nz$ from the backward discrete Riccati recursion started at $P_0=\nabla^2W$, $\pi_N=-\nabla W(z_N)$ and (58) to machine precision, for $W$ equal to the discrete Riccati weight, to the continuous one, and to three times the former. With the exact discrete Lagrangian and $W$ the continuous Riccati weight, the one-step rule of Section 6.3 reproduces $z'=e^{(A-BK)h}z$ and $V_1=W$, now in phase space.

9. Gauss’s Principle: Multipliers Are Forces

Section 8 ended with a torque set by a momentum. This section shows that the infinitesimal-window rule with Gauss’s weight is Gauss’s principle of least constraint, and that a task imposed as a constraint in it has a force as its multiplier. This is where whole-body control enters.

9.1 Gauss’s principle as the infinitesimal-window rule

Gauss’s principle of least constraint says that, among all accelerations compatible with the constraints, a mechanical system realizes the one closest to its free acceleration, measured in the kinetic-energy metric (Udwadia & Kalaba, 1996):

$$ \begin{gathered} \ddot q=\arg\min_{\ddot q}\ \tfrac12\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})^\top M\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free}) \quad\text{s.t. the constraints},\\ \ddot q_{\rm free}=M^{-1}(\tau_{\rm imp}-b). \end{gathered} \tag{61} $$

The free acceleration is what the impressed torque $\tau_{\rm imp}$, a given function of the state (gravity compensation, springs, dampers), would produce on its own. Write the actual acceleration as the free one plus a correction produced by an extra torque, $M{(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})}=\tau_c$, so that $\tau=\tau_{\rm imp}+\tau_c$. Then the Gauss function is

$$ \tfrac12\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})^\top M\,(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=\tfrac12\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c , \tag{62} $$

which is the torque cost of Section 8.6 centred on $\tau_{\rm imp}$, with weight $M^{-1}$. To be added to $\dot W$, a power, the cost needs a time constant: $\ell=\tfrac{T_0}2\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c$, i.e. the weight $R=T_0M^{-1}$, which we call Gauss’s weight. So Gauss’s principle is a one-step variational rule: over an infinitesimal step, choose the correction torque of least Gauss effort that achieves what is required. What is required can be said in two ways.

  • By a boundary term. Minimizing $\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c+\dot W$ over $\tau_c$ gives, by (56) with $R=T_0M^{-1}$, $$\tau_c=\frac{\pi_v}{T_0},\qquad \pi_v=-\nabla_vW:\tag{63}$$ the inertia cancels, and the correction torque is the velocity momentum of $W$ per unit time. The multiplier of the dynamics is the force, up to $T_0$.
  • By a constraint. Prescribe what a task must do; then the multiplier of the task is the force (Section 9.2).

In discrete time. Identity (63) is exact in continuous time. In a multiple-shooting transcription with the stage cost $h\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_j^\top M^{-1}\tau_j$, the stationarity condition of (58) reads $hT_0M^{-1}\tau_j=\partial_\tau\Phi_h^\top\pi_{j+1}$, with $\pi_{j+1}=-\nabla V_{N-j-1}(z_{j+1})$, so, written with the momenta at the end of the step, the position momentum enters as soon as the torque moves the position within the step:

  • Zero-order hold (exact if $M$ and $b$ are both constant, as for the free mass of the example below; to leading order in $h$ otherwise): $\partial_\tau\Phi_h=\big(\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1},\,hM^{-1}\big)$, so $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+\tfrac h2\,\pi_{q,j+1}$. A constant $M$ alone is not enough: it leaves $b=g(q)$, and a change of torque moves $q$ within the step and with it $g$. Differentiating the flow of $\dot q=v$, $\dot v=M^{-1}(\tau-b)$ with respect to the held torque and expanding in $h$ gives in general the position and velocity blocks $\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1}+O(h^3)$ and $hM^{-1}-\tfrac{h^2}2M^{-1}(\dot M+\partial_vb)M^{-1}+O(h^3)$, with $M$, $\dot M$ and $\partial_vb$ at $z_j$, so that $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+\tfrac h2\big[\pi_{q,j+1}-(\dot M+\partial_vb)^{\!\top}M^{-1}\pi_{v,j+1}\big]+O(h^2)$. For constant $M$ and $b=g(q)$ the bracket is $\pi_{q,j+1}$ and the identity is off only at $O(h^2)$; for a configuration-dependent $M$ in motion, or a damper, the second term of the bracket is of the same order as the first, and the position momentum is not the only $O(h)$ correction.
  • Symplectic Euler, $v_{j+1}=v_j+hM^{-1}(\tau_j-b)$, $q_{j+1}=q_j+h\,v_{j+1}$, written as an explicit map: $\partial_\tau\Phi_h=\big(h^2M^{-1},\,hM^{-1}\big)$, so $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$. Transcribed instead with $v_{j+1}$ as a node variable in the position defect, $q_{j+1}-q_j-hv_{j+1}=0$, the torque enters only the velocity defect, and $T_0\tau_j$ equals that defect’s multiplier exactly; the two transcriptions differ by a change of the defect coordinates, under which that multiplier is $\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$.

The costate recursion (58) carries these end-of-step momenta back to the node where the torque acts. Under symplectic Euler, $\partial v_{j+1}/\partial v_j=I-hM^{-1}b_v$ and $\partial q_{j+1}/\partial v_j=h\,(I-hM^{-1}b_v)$, with $b_v=\partial b/\partial v$ and $M$ evaluated at $z_j$, so the velocity part of (58) and the stationarity condition give, exactly, $$\pi_{v,j}=\big(I-h\,b_v^\top M^{-1}\big)\big(\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}\big)-\nabla_v\ell_d =\big(I-h\,b_v^\top M^{-1}\big)\,T_0\tau_j-\nabla_v\ell_d(z_j,\tau_j).$$ When neither $b$ nor the stage cost depends on $v$ (for instance Gauss’s weight alone and $b=g(q)$, with no Coriolis, centrifugal or damping terms), this is $T_0\tau_j=\pi_{v,j}=-\nabla_vV_{N-j}(z_j)$, exactly, for $j=0,\dots,N-1$; for $j=0$, $\pi_0$ is the multiplier of the initial value (Section 8.7). The torque is the velocity momentum at the node where it is applied: the discrete counterpart of $\tau^*=\pi_v/T_0$, (56) with Gauss’s weight. This holds in either transcription, because it involves only the remaining action, which does not depend on how the defects are written; in the implicit one, the velocity-defect multiplier of step $j$ is $\pi_{v,j}$. Coriolis terms, damping or a velocity cost change it by $O(h)$. Under zero-order hold with constant $M$ and $b$, $\partial_z\Phi_h=\begin{pmatrix}I&hI\\0&I\end{pmatrix}$, and with Gauss’s weight alone $\nabla_z\ell_d=0$; so (58) gives $\pi_{v,j}=\pi_{v,j+1}+h\,\pi_{q,j+1}$, and the first bullet becomes $T_0\tau_j=\tfrac12(\pi_{v,j}+\pi_{v,j+1})$: the held torque is the mean of the velocity momenta at the two ends of its step.

Take one degree of freedom, a free mass ($b=0$) with $M=2$ kg, the boundary term $W=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12K_eq^2$ with spring $K_e=30$ N/m, $T_0=0.1$ s and a window of 10 ms, starting at rest; since $M$ is constant and $b=0$, both exact identities hold. The end-of-step position-momentum term, $\tfrac h2\pi_{q,1}$ under zero-order hold and $h\pi_{q,1}$ under symplectic Euler, is then 110%, 35.5% and 5.5% of $T_0\tau_0$ under zero-order hold for $(h,N)=(10\ \text{ms},1)$, $(5\ \text{ms},2)$ and $(1\ \text{ms},10)$, and 54% and 10.5% under symplectic Euler for the last two. These numbers measure where the momentum is read, not a part of the torque supplied by the position momentum: the term is the difference $\pi_{v,0}-\pi_{v,1}=h\,\pi_{q,1}$ between the velocity momenta at the two ends of the first step (half of it under zero-order hold), and it is large only because a window started from rest, where $\nabla_vW=0$, produces a first torque of the order of the window length, so the ratio is of order $1/N$. “Torque equals velocity momentum” is exact in continuous time. In discrete time it is exact at the node where the torque is applied under symplectic Euler, and exact for the mean over the step under zero-order hold, under the conditions just stated; otherwise it holds to $O(h)$.

9.2 A task as a constraint: operational-space control

The second answer imposes the task acceleration: $J\ddot q=a_x$, where $J=\partial x/\partial q$ is the Jacobian of a task coordinate $x(q)$ and $a_x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}-\dot J\dot q$, with $\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$ a commanded task acceleration (for instance a PD law plus feedforward): since $\dot x=J\dot q$ gives $\ddot x=J\ddot q+\dot J\dot q$, the constraint says $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$. With multiplier $\lambda$ for this constraint, the KKT conditions of (61) are $M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J^\top\lambda$ and $J\ddot q=a_x$. Eliminating $\ddot q$:

$$ \lambda=\Lambda\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big),\qquad \tau_c=J^\top\lambda,\qquad \Lambda=(JM^{-1}J^\top)^{-1}. \tag{64} $$

This is Khatib’s operational-space control (Khatib, 1987): $a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}$ is the task acceleration that is missing from the free motion, and the task inertia $\Lambda$ converts it into the force that supplies it. The value of the Gauss function at the optimum is

$$ \min\ \tfrac12\,\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c=\tfrac12\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big)^\top\Lambda\,\big(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free}\big), \tag{65} $$

so $\Lambda$ is the Gauss metric pushed forward to task space (a Schur complement). The connection between Gauss’s principle and operational-space control was made explicit by Bruyninckx & Khatib (2000), building on the constrained-motion formulation of Udwadia & Kalaba (1996); the same family of control laws arises as minimizers of $\tfrac12\tau^\top R\,\tau$ subject to a task constraint, with $R=M^{-1}$ giving the operational-space law (Udwadia, 2003; Peters et al., 2008).

9.3 The dual is the task force

Reading. In the constraint view, the force acting on the task is the Lagrange multiplier of the task constraint: $\lambda$ in (64) is a generalized force in task coordinates, and its power is $\lambda^\top\dot x$. This is the whole-body counterpart of Section 8.3: there the multipliers of the dynamics were momenta; here the multipliers of the tasks are forces. Both are duals, and both are read off a KKT system.

Units make the parallel exact. In the units of Section 9.1, where the stage cost $\tfrac{T_0}2\tau_c^\top M^{-1}\tau_c$ is a power, the task-constrained problem has the multiplier $T_0\lambda=\Lambda\,T_0(a_x-J\ddot q_{\rm free})$: the task inertia times the velocity that the missing acceleration would build up over $T_0$, an impulse. Its torque is $J^\top(T_0\lambda)/T_0$, exactly as the torque of (63) is $\pi_v/T_0$. Written with a cost in units of power, both multipliers are momenta, and dividing by $T_0$ turns both into forces.

Contacts. The analogy with contact is exact: a bilateral contact is a task whose acceleration is prescribed so that the contact point does not move ($\ddot x_c=0$, i.e. $a_x=-\dot J_c\dot q$), and its multiplier is the contact force. A unilateral contact turns the equality into a complementarity condition, the subject of the next subsection.

9.4 Unilateral constraints: forces that can only push

A contact that may open, a joint limit or a distance to an obstacle is a unilateral constraint on a task coordinate, $x_i(q)\ge\underline x_i$. On the boundary it is imposed at the acceleration level, $J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q\ge a_{\mathcal I}$ (with $\dot J\dot q$ absorbed), next to equality rows $J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E}$. Gauss’s principle (61) with such rows has the KKT conditions

$$ M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J_{\mathcal E}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal E}+J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I},\qquad J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E},\qquad 0\le\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ \perp\ J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q-a_{\mathcal I}\ge0 . \tag{66} $$

The constraint force can only push ($\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ge0$), and only where the bound is reached (the complementarity $\perp$: each component of $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ or of the margin vanishes). Eliminating $\ddot q$ leaves $\lambda_{\mathcal E}$ and $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ coupled through the mobility of all rows (the Delassus matrix of contact mechanics), whose blocks are $J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$, $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$ and $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top$. The equality rows can be eliminated as well. Together with the second, $J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q=a_{\mathcal E}$, the first condition of (66) is the KKT system of Section 9.2 for the equality rows, with the free acceleration $\ddot q_{\rm free}+M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I}$; so, for $J_{\mathcal E}$ of full row rank, (64) gives $\lambda_{\mathcal E}=\Lambda_{\mathcal E}\big(a_{\mathcal E}-J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q_{\rm free}-J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal I}\big)$, with $\Lambda_{\mathcal E}=(J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top)^{-1}$ the task inertia of the equality rows. What remains is a linear complementarity problem for $\lambda_{\mathcal I}$ alone, $$0\le\lambda_{\mathcal I}\ \perp\ \big(J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top-J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top\Lambda_{\mathcal E}J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top\big)\lambda_{\mathcal I}+J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q_{\mathcal E}-a_{\mathcal I}\ \ge0 ,$$ where $\ddot q_{\mathcal E}=\ddot q_{\rm free}+M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top\Lambda_{\mathcal E}\big(a_{\mathcal E}-J_{\mathcal E}\ddot q_{\rm free}\big)$ is Gauss’s acceleration with the equality rows alone, so that the offset is the margin at $\lambda_{\mathcal I}=0$. Its matrix is the mobility of the unilateral rows while the equality rows are held: the Schur complement of $J_{\mathcal E}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top$ in the mobility of all rows, which is also the pivot of the unilateral rows when the stack $[J_{\mathcal E};J_{\mathcal I}]$ is factored block by block as in Section 13.2. It is positive definite when this stack has full row rank, and the complementarity problem then has a unique solution. It reduces to the unilateral block $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal I}^\top$ exactly when $J_{\mathcal I}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal E}^\top=0$, in particular when there are no equality rows. Optimality in the convex problem (61) can also be written as Fourier’s inequality, the unilateral form of d’Alembert’s principle: the gradient of the Gauss function, $M{(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})}=\tau_c$, makes a non-negative product with every admissible direction,

$$ \tau_c^\top\big(\ddot q'-\ddot q\big)\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{for every admissible acceleration }\ddot q' . \tag{67} $$

In a fixed-seed example ($n=7$, a two-row equality task, three unilateral rows) two rows end up active with $\lambda=1.770$ and $1.609$, the third is inactive with $\lambda=0$ and a margin of $0.375$, and (67) holds on 1407 sampled admissible accelerations.

At which level is a unilateral force workless? Complementarity can be written at three levels, and only one of them speaks about power.
  • Position (Signorini): gap $x_c\ge0$, force $f_n\ge0$, $f_nx_c=0$. The force acts only at contact.
  • Velocity (Moreau): on a closed contact, $\dot x_c\ge0$, $f_n\ge0$, $f_n\dot x_c=0$. The power $f_n\dot x_c$ vanishes identically; at an impact the velocity jumps and, with Moreau’s impact law and restitution coefficients up to one, energy is lost, not gained (Moreau, 1988; Brogliato, 2016).
  • Acceleration (Gauss, (66)): the complementarity pairs force with acceleration. A contact force is workless here only because $\dot x_c=0$ on a persistent contact.
For an inequality task whose acceleration bound $a_i(x_i,\dot x_i)$ is a feedback law, nothing ties $\dot x_i$ to zero while the row is active, and $\lambda_i\ge0$ does not make $\lambda_i\dot x_i\le0$. In the example above, at a velocity for which one active row stays on its bound ($\dot x=0$) and the other recedes from it ($\dot x=0.5$), the powers are $0$ and $+0.805$: the second force pushes in the allowed direction and injects energy. The power is non-positive only if a row can be active solely while its coordinate does not recede ($\dot x_i\le0$ whenever $\lambda_i>0$).

Passivity. Contacts with complementarity at the velocity level are passive constraints: no work while smooth, dissipation at impacts. Feedback-defined inequality tasks are not passive by construction; their power belongs in the energy bookkeeping like that of any task (Section 15.2). A sign on a force is not a sign on its power.

10. Impressed versus Constraint Forces

Section 9 gave two ways to say what a task requires: through a boundary term (Section 9.1) and through a constraint (Section 9.2). This section compares them as impressed and constraint forces, and asks what certifies each.

10.1 Two ways to act on a task

Analytical mechanics distinguishes impressed forces, which are given functions of the state, from constraint forces, which take whatever value the constraint requires. A controller can play either role; in the notation of Section 9.1, it acts through $\tau_{\rm imp}$ or through $\tau_c$.

  • Constraint (Section 9.2). Impose $J\ddot q=a_x$. The controller supplies whatever force $\lambda$ that takes. The closed-loop task dynamics are $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$: the task behaves as a unit mass, i.e. the controller shapes the inertia. An unmeasured external force $J^\top F_{\rm ext}$, which the controller does not see in $\ddot q_{\rm free}$, still acts through the natural inertia: the plant obeys $M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J^\top(\lambda+F_{\rm ext})$ with $\lambda$ from (64), so $J\ddot q=a_x+\Lambda^{-1}F_{\rm ext}$, i.e. $\Lambda(\ddot x-\ddot x_{\rm cmd})=F_{\rm ext}$, and shaping the apparent inertia against $F_{\rm ext}$ requires force feedback. A unilateral version of the constraint supplies a force of one sign only (Section 9.4).
  • Impressed. Apply a task force $F(x,\dot x)$, for example $F=-K_{P,x}\tilde x-K_{D,x}\dot x$ with $\tilde x=x-x_d$ the error from a constant task target $x_d$, as an additional torque $J^\top F$ inside $\tau_{\rm imp}$. Gauss’s principle with no task constraint returns the free motion under that torque, and the task acceleration changes, relative to its value without $F$, by $$\Delta\ddot x=JM^{-1}J^\top F=\Lambda^{-1}F .\tag{68}$$ The task responds with its natural inertia $\Lambda$: in the absence of other effects, $\Lambda\ddot x+K_{D,x}\dot x+K_{P,x}\tilde x=F_{\rm ext}$, an impedance with the robot’s own inertia.

Passivity-based (impedance) control with preserved natural inertia, the family studied by Ott (2008), Ott et al. (2015), Dietrich & Ott (2020) and Garofalo & Ott (2020), is the impressed-force view. Operational-space control is the constraint view. The two coincide in what they achieve (convergence of the task), but they assign different forces to the task and inject different power, which is what matters for interaction and passivity.

10.2 Argmin or certificate?

Which of the two is optimal in the sense of the infinitesimal-window rule, and what certifies it? Take the rule with an energy-type boundary term, the kinetic energy plus a designed (“shaped”) potential $U_s$ in place of the gravitational one. Differentiating along $M\ddot q+C\dot q+g=\tau$,

$$ W=\tfrac12\,v^\top M v+U_s(q)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot W=\dot q^\top\big(\tau-g+\nabla U_s\big), \tag{69} $$

where the term $\tfrac12\dot q^\top(\dot M-2C)\dot q$ vanished because $\dot M-2C$ is skew-symmetric for the Christoffel-symbol choice of $C$: the Coriolis and centrifugal forces do no work on the kinetic energy (Spong et al., 2006). The torque enters $\dot W$ only through the power $\dot q^\top\tau$, and the velocity momentum of this $W$ is $\pi_v=-\nabla_vW=-Mv$, minus the mechanical momentum. Now take a stage cost centred on an impressed torque $\tau_{\rm imp}(q)$,

$$ \ell=\tfrac12\,(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})^\top R\,(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})+\ell_0(q,v), \qquad \tau^\ast=\arg\min_\tau\big[\ell+\dot W\big]=\tau_{\rm imp}-R^{-1}\dot q , \tag{70} $$

the minimizer following from the gradient $R(\tau-\tau_{\rm imp})+\dot q=0$. With $\tau_{\rm imp}=g-\nabla U_s$ (gravity compensation plus the shaped potential, e.g. $\nabla U_s=J^\top K_{P,x}\tilde x$), (70) is the passivity-based law $\tau=g-J^\top K_{P,x}\tilde x-K_D\dot q$ with $K_D=R^{-1}$. With Gauss’s weight $R=T_0M^{-1}$ the correction is $\tau_c=-M\dot q/T_0=\pi_v/T_0$, which is (63): damping proportional to the mechanical momentum, $K_D=M/T_0$. Substituting $\tau^\ast$ gives the descent defect of Section 8.2,

$$ \mathcal D=\min_\tau\big[\ell+\dot W\big]=\ell_0(q,v)-\tfrac12\,v^\top K_D\,v\ \le\ 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_0\le\tfrac12\,v^\top K_D\,v . \tag{71} $$
Argmin versus certificate. Both laws minimize a one-step problem; they differ in what certifies them.
  • The operational-space law minimizes the Gauss cost, centred on the free motion, subject to the task as a hard constraint (Section 9.2). No boundary term enters; its stability is that of the imposed task dynamics $\ddot x=\ddot x_{\rm cmd}$.
  • The passivity-based law with natural inertia minimizes the cost (70), centred on the shaped impressed torque, plus $\dot W$ for the energy-type $W$ of (69), and the minimum value (71) is the certificate (46). The minimization contributes only the damping $-R^{-1}\dot q$; stiffness and gravity compensation enter through $\tau_{\rm imp}$, which must match the $U_s$ in $W$.
Gauss’s weight, which makes the damping proportional to the inertia, is one modelling choice, not a requirement.

10.3 Why energy alone certifies only damping

Condition (71) requires $\ell_0(q,0)\le0$: a non-negative $\ell_0$ must vanish at rest, so it cannot penalize the task error $\tilde x$ there, because $\dot W$ in (69) contains no term that is negative when $v=0$ and $\tilde x\neq0$. This is the precise sense in which the mechanical energy is a weak Lyapunov function:

  • the infinitesimal-window rule with an energy-type $W$ produces pure damping around the impressed torque; any stiffness has to be placed in $\tau_{\rm imp}$ by hand;
  • convergence of $\tilde x$ then follows from LaSalle’s invariance principle, not from (46) with a positive-definite $\ell$;
  • a strict certificate needs cross terms between position and velocity in $W$, as in the composite variable $s=\dot e+\Gamma e$ of Slotine & Li (1987), worked out in Section 11.
Take-away. “Optimal torque = momentum” (63) is true, but with an energy-type $W$ the momentum $-\nabla_vW=-Mv$ carries no position information. Position feedback enters either through an impressed torque on which the designer centres the cost, or through a $W$ with cross terms. Natural-inertia controllers take the first route; Section 11 takes the second.

10.4 The plant’s energy as the boundary term

There is published precedent for taking a plant energy as $W$. Kawai et al. (2005) apply receding-horizon control to a two-link direct-drive manipulator with an energy function as the terminal cost (our boundary term $W$) instead of a terminal constraint (our region $\Omega$). The energy serves as a control Lyapunov function, chosen by considering inverse optimality, and the paper reports experiments on the arm. Terminal costs without terminal constraints are the setting of Jadbabaie et al. (2001). Two cautions apply when reusing the idea on another plant:

  • The Lagrangian must match $W$. If the Lagrangian is constructed from the energy (inverse optimality), descent holds by design. Choosing $\ell$ independently and then reusing the energy as $W$ does not guarantee descent: check (8), or its continuous form (46), for your $\ell$. Section 10.2 showed one way the match fails (a position penalty against a weak $W$); Section 11.4 shows another (the Slotine–Li spring).
  • One paper is evidence of a workable construction, not a general theorem. Any specific plant still needs its own check.

11. Worked Example: A Two-Link Manipulator

The hilltop of Section 5.2 is a first-order, one-dimensional problem in which everything can be solved in closed form. This section works through a genuinely robotic case: a two-link arm moving in a vertical plane under gravity, regulated to a fixed posture. The plant is second order, its inertia depends on the configuration, and its torques are bounded. The boundary term is the composite-variable energy of Slotine & Li (1987), the strict alternative to the mechanical energy of Section 10.3. The example has three goals: to show what the infinitesimal-window rule produces for this $W$ (a familiar controller), to check the certificate (46) honestly, including choices for which it fails, and to run the receding-window scheme and confirm the descent inequality (11) step by step.

11.1 The plant

Two rigid, massless links of lengths $l_1=0.5$ m and $l_2=0.4$ m carry point masses $m_1=2.0$ kg and $m_2=1.5$ kg at their tips. Joint angle $q_1$ is measured from the horizontal, $q_2$ is the relative angle of the second link, and gravity $g_0=9.81$ m/s$^2$ acts along $-y$. The equations of motion have the standard form (Spong et al., 2006)

$$ M(q)\,\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q+g(q)=\tau , \tag{72} $$

with $\tau$ the joint torques. Writing $c_2=\cos q_2$, $c_1=\cos q_1$ and $c_{12}=\cos(q_1+q_2)$, the three ingredients are

$$ \begin{aligned} M(q)&=\begin{pmatrix} m_1l_1^2+m_2\big(l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2c_2\big) & m_2\big(l_2^2+l_1l_2c_2\big)\\[2pt] m_2\big(l_2^2+l_1l_2c_2\big) & m_2l_2^2\end{pmatrix},\\[4pt] C(q,\dot q)&=m_2l_1l_2\,\sin q_2\begin{pmatrix}-\dot q_2 & -(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\\ \dot q_1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \\[4pt] g(q)&=g_0\begin{pmatrix}(m_1+m_2)\,l_1c_1+m_2l_2c_{12}\\ m_2l_2c_{12}\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \tag{73} $$

$M$ is the mass matrix of the kinetic energy $\mathcal T=\tfrac12v^\top Mv$, $g=\nabla U$ is the gradient of the potential $U=g_0\,(m_1y_1+m_2y_2)$, with $y_1,y_2$ the heights of the two masses, and $C$ is built from the Christoffel symbols of $M$. With that choice of $C$ the matrix $\dot M-2C$ is skew-symmetric, as used in Section 10.2; here

$$ \dot M-2C=m_2l_1l_2\,\sin q_2\,(2\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \xi^\top(\dot M-2C)\,\xi=0\ \ \forall \xi . \tag{74} $$

This is the one structural fact the derivation below needs. It says that the Coriolis and centrifugal forces do no work on the kinetic energy (Spong et al., 2006).

The target is the posture $q_d=(\pi/4,\,-\pi/4)$, which places the second link horizontally. We use the error $e=q-q_d$, the velocity $v=\dot q=\dot e$ and the state $z=(e,v)$; the gradient $\nabla_z$ with respect to $(e,v)$ is the same as with respect to $(q,v)$. The input is the gravity-compensated torque

$$ u=\tau-g(q),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \dot q=v,\qquad \dot v=f(z,u)=M(q)^{-1}\big(u-C(q,v)\,v\big). \tag{75} $$

The reason for this change of input is simple: holding the arm still at $q_d$ needs the torque $g(q_d)\ne0$, so a cost on $\tau$ itself would not vanish at the target. In the language of Section 10.2, the cost is centred on the impressed torque $\tau_{\rm imp}=g(q)$. In terms of $u$ the rest point is $z=0$, $u=0$. The torque limits $|\tau_1|\le50$ N m, $|\tau_2|\le30$ N m stay limits on $\tau=u+g(q)$.

At the target, $M(q_d)=\begin{pmatrix}1.539&0.452\\0.452&0.240\end{pmatrix}$ kg m$^2$ with eigenvalues 0.098 and 1.681, a ratio of about 17. The small inertia direction produces a fast closed-loop mode, which matters for the step size in Section 11.5.

11.2 Lagrangian and boundary term

The variational Lagrangian (stage cost) penalizes position error, velocity and the compensated torque:

$$ \ell(z,u)=\tfrac12\,e^\top Q_e\,e+\tfrac12\,v^\top Q_v\,v+\tfrac12\,u^\top R\,u , \qquad Q_e,Q_v,R\succ0 . \tag{76} $$

For the boundary term we take the energy-like function of Slotine and Li. It is built from the composite velocity $s$, which blends the velocity with the position error,

$$ s=\dot e+\Gamma e,\qquad W(z)=\tfrac12\,s^\top M(q)\,s+\tfrac12\,e^\top K_e\,e,\qquad \Gamma\succ0,\ K_e\succ0 . \tag{77} $$

$W$ is positive definite: it is a kinetic energy evaluated at the “wrong” velocity $s$ plus a spring. To use it as a boundary term we need its rate of change along the plant, as a function of the input. Differentiate, use $M\dot s=M\ddot q+M\Gamma\dot e=u-Cv+M\Gamma\dot e$ from (75), and replace $\tfrac12s^\top\dot Ms$ by $s^\top Cs$, which is allowed by (74). The two Coriolis terms combine, $s^\top C(s-v)=s^\top C\Gamma e$, and what remains is

$$ \dot W(z,u)=s^\top u+s^\top a(z)+e^\top K_e\,\dot e, \qquad a(z)=C(q,v)\,\Gamma e+M(q)\,\Gamma\dot e . \tag{78} $$

Two features matter. First, $\dot W$ is affine in $u$, and the input enters only through $s^\top u$: the inertia has cancelled. Second, $-a(z)$ is exactly the (gravity-compensated) torque that would be needed to follow the “reference velocity” $\dot q_r=-\Gamma e$, since $M\ddot q_r+C\dot q_r=-a$.

The classical identity. Slotine and Li cancel $a$ and add damping on $s$, $u_{\text{SL}}=-a(z)-K_Ds$, with $K_D\succ0$ and $\Gamma K_D$ symmetric (e.g. both diagonal). Then $\dot W=-s^\top K_Ds+e^\top K_e\dot e$. Expanding $s^\top K_Ds$ produces a cross term $-2e^\top\Gamma K_D\dot e$, which the spring cancels exactly if $K_e=2\Gamma K_D$: $$\dot W=-\dot e^\top K_D\,\dot e-e^\top\Gamma K_D\Gamma\,e\qquad(K_e=2\Gamma K_D,\ u=u_{\text{SL}}).\tag{79}$$ This is the Lyapunov function of the Slotine–Li controller (Slotine & Li, 1987; Slotine & Li, 1991, Ch. 9). It requires the feed-forward $a(z)$, i.e. a model of $M$ and $C$.

11.3 The infinitesimal-window rule is PD control with gravity compensation

Now apply the rule of Section 8.2 to this pair $(\ell,W)$: over an infinitesimal window, choose the input that minimizes the action rate plus the rate of change of the boundary term, (45). By (78) the bracket is $\ell(z,u)+s^\top u+(\text{terms without }u)$, a strictly convex quadratic in $u$. Setting its gradient $Ru+s$ to zero gives

$$ \begin{gathered} u^*(z)=\arg\min_u\big[\ell(z,u)+\dot W(z,u)\big]=-R^{-1}s,\\ \tau^*=g(q)-K_D\,\dot e-K_P\,e,\quad K_D=R^{-1},\ K_P=R^{-1}\Gamma . \end{gathered} \tag{80} $$
A familiar controller falls out. The infinitesimal window does not produce the Slotine–Li law. It produces PD control with gravity compensation, the controller of Takegaki & Arimoto (1981), with damping gain $K_D=R^{-1}$ and stiffness $K_P=R^{-1}\Gamma$ (symmetric when $R$ and $\Gamma$ commute, e.g. both diagonal, as here). Three observations explain why.
  • The input enters $\dot W$ only through $s^\top u$, so the minimizer sees only $s$. The feed-forward $a(z)$ does not depend on $u$ and therefore cannot lower $\ell+\dot W$; the rule has no reason to cancel it.
  • In momentum language, the velocity component of $\pi=-\nabla_zW$ is $\pi_v=-M(q)\,s$, and the input acts through $M^{-1}$. The maximizer of $\pi_v\cdot M^{-1}u-\tfrac12u^\top Ru$ is $R^{-1}M^{-1}\pi_v=-R^{-1}s$, as in (56): the configuration-dependent inertia cancels, which is why constant gains come out.
  • Compare Section 10.2: with the mechanical energy the velocity momentum was $-M\dot q$, and the same rule produced only damping. The composite velocity puts the position error into the velocity momentum, $\pi_v=-M(\dot e+\Gamma e)$, and the rule now produces stiffness $K_P=R^{-1}\Gamma$ as well. This is the weak/strict distinction of Section 10.3 seen from the controller side.
The only model knowledge left is $g(q)$, which was put in by the choice $u=\tau-g$. The price is paid in the certificate: $a(z)$ stays in $\dot W$, and Section 11.4 shows that the gains must dominate it.

11.4 Checking the certificate

Whether $W$ certifies descent is decided by the value of the bracket at its minimizer, the descent defect of Section 8.2:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal D(z)&=\min_u\big[\ell(z,u)+\dot W(z,u)\big]\\ &=\tfrac12e^\top Q_ee+\tfrac12v^\top Q_vv-\tfrac12s^\top R^{-1}s+s^\top a(z)+e^\top K_e\dot e\\ &=-H\big(z,-\nabla_zW\big), \end{aligned} \tag{81} $$

where $H(z,\pi)=\sup_u[\pi_q\cdot v+\pi_v\cdot f(z,u)-\ell]$ is the second-order Hamiltonian (44). The last equality holds because, for $\pi=-\nabla_zW$, $\pi_q\cdot v+\pi_v\cdot f(z,u)=-\nabla_zW\cdot\dot z=-\dot W(z,u)$. Along $u^*$ we have $\dot W=-\ell+\mathcal D$, so the requirement $\dot W\le-\ell$ is $\mathcal D(z)\le0$, i.e. $H(z,-\nabla_zW)\ge0$: the certificate (46). It has to hold on a terminal region $\Omega$, here a sublevel set $\{W\le \bar W\}$.

Near the target: matching the Lagrangian to $W$

Close to $z=0$ the sign of $\mathcal D$ is decided by its quadratic part. The Coriolis part of $a(z)$ is quadratic in $z$, so it contributes only cubic terms. Freezing $M$ at $M_0=M(q_d)$ in the other part gives $\mathcal D(z)=\tfrac12z^\top\nabla^2\mathcal D(0)\,z+O(|z|^3)$ with

$$ \nabla^2\mathcal D(0)=\begin{pmatrix} Q_e-\Gamma K_D\Gamma & K_e-\Gamma K_D+\Gamma M_0\Gamma\\[2pt] \big(K_e-\Gamma K_D+\Gamma M_0\Gamma\big)^\top & Q_v-K_D+M_0\Gamma+\Gamma M_0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_D=R^{-1}, \tag{82} $$

for diagonal $\Gamma$ and $R$. The off-diagonal block of (82) is the coupling between position error and velocity. It vanishes for one spring only, and then the diagonal blocks give explicit bounds on the Lagrangian:

$$ K_e=\Gamma K_D-\Gamma M_0\Gamma,\qquad Q_e\preceq\Gamma K_D\Gamma,\qquad Q_v\preceq K_D-(M_0\Gamma+\Gamma M_0). \tag{83} $$

With strict inequalities, $\mathcal D\le-\alpha_0|z|^2$ near the target for some $\alpha_0\gt0$. With equalities, $\nabla^2\mathcal D(0)=0$ and $W$ matches $\ell$ to second order, the analogue of $H=0$; the cubic terms then decide, and they are odd in $z$. With $\Gamma=\gamma I$, a positive-definite spring needs $K_D\succ\gamma M_0$, and a positive-definite $Q_v$ needs $K_D\succ2\gamma M_0$: the damping must dominate the inertia seen through $\Gamma$.

The Slotine–Li spring is the wrong spring here. Give the Slotine–Li law the same damping as the rule, $K_D=R^{-1}$ as in (80); its matching spring (79) is then $K_e=2\Gamma R^{-1}$. With $\Gamma=\gamma I$ and $K_e=2\gamma K_D$, the off-diagonal block is $\gamma K_D+\gamma^2M_0$. For $Q_e=Q_v=0$ the Schur complement of the (negative-definite) upper-left block of $\nabla^2\mathcal D(0)$ is $4\gamma M_0+\gamma^2M_0K_D^{-1}M_0\succ0$, so $\nabla^2\mathcal D(0)$ has positive eigenvalues; adding $Q_e,Q_v\succeq0$ only raises them. Hence no stage cost of the form (76) is certified by the Slotine–Li gain matching. The reason is a factor of two: $\min_u[\tfrac12u^\top Ru+s^\top u]=-\tfrac12s^\top R^{-1}s$ supplies only half of the damping $s^\top K_Ds$ that (79) uses, and $u^*$ does not cancel $a(z)$. This is a concrete instance of the caution of Section 10.4: the Lagrangian must match $W$. The closed loop is still stable (for set-point regulation, PD with exact gravity compensation and without torque limits is globally asymptotically stable for any symmetric positive-definite gains; Takegaki & Arimoto, 1981); what fails is the certificate, exactly as in Case B of Section 6.4.

Chosen parameters

We take $\Gamma=\gamma I$ with $\gamma=2$ s$^{-1}$ and $R=\operatorname{diag}(0.05,\,0.1)$, so $K_D=\operatorname{diag}(20,\,10)$ and $K_P=\operatorname{diag}(40,\,20)$. The matched spring (83) is $K_e=\begin{pmatrix}33.84&-1.81\\-1.81&19.04\end{pmatrix}$, positive definite. The bounds in (83) are $\Gamma K_D\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}(80,40)$ and $K_D-2\gamma M_0$ with eigenvalues 8.44 and 14.45; we use half the first, $Q_e=\operatorname{diag}(40,\,20)$, and a round value below half the second, $Q_v=4I$ (the smallest eigenvalue of $\tfrac12(K_D-2\gamma M_0)$ is 4.22, so $\tfrac12(K_D-2\gamma M_0)-4I\succ0$).

How far does the certificate hold?

The largest admissible level is $\bar W^*=\inf\{W(z):\mathcal D(z)\gt0\}$. We estimated it numerically: along $2\times10^5$ random rays in the four-dimensional state space we located the first point with $\mathcal D\gt0$ (out to $|z|=30$) by bisection, took the smallest $W$ found, and refined it by a local minimization of $W$ subject to $\mathcal D\ge0$. 11.5 The receding-window scheme

A digital controller needs a step map. We use the semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler step with $h=0.005$ s,

$$ \begin{gathered} v_{k+1}=v_k+h\,M(q_k)^{-1}\big(u_k-C(q_k,v_k)v_k\big),\qquad e_{k+1}=e_k+h\,v_{k+1},\\ z_{k+1}=\Phi_h(z_k,u_k), \end{gathered} \tag{84} $$

the rectangle-rule stage cost $h\,\ell(z_k,u_k)$ and the terminal cost $c\,W$ on the terminal region $\Omega=\{W\le \bar W\}$, with a scale $c$ as in Section 6.3. The reference flow of Section 2.3 is the PD law (80) itself, $u_{\text{ref}}=-R^{-1}s$, and the discrete condition (8) reads

$$ c\,\big[W(\Phi_h(z,u_{\text{ref}}))-W(z)\big]\ \le\ -h\,\ell(z,u_{\text{ref}})\qquad\forall z\in\Omega, \tag{85} $$

together with the torque limits for $\tau=g(q)+u_{\text{ref}}$ on $\Omega$. Since $W$ then decreases under $u_{\text{ref}}$, the sublevel set $\Omega$ is invariant (A1). Three numbers fix the terminal ingredients.

  • Torque limits. The reference torque stays inside the limits on $\{W\le \bar W\}$ for $\bar W\le\bar W_\tau\approx0.310$. The first violation is $\tau_2=30$ N m, almost at the target posture, $e\approx(0.0006,\,0.013)$ rad, at a fast joint-2 velocity, $v\approx(0.71,\,-2.44)$ rad/s. At $e=0$ the bound can be read off: there $s=v$, and (80) gives $\tau_2=g_2(q_d)-(K_D)_{22}v_2$ with $g_2(q_d)=g_0m_2l_2=5.89$ N m, so the limit is reached at $v_2=-(30-g_2(q_d))/(K_D)_{22}=-2.41$ rad/s. The least $W=\tfrac12v^\top M_0v$ with this $v_2$, attained at $v_1=-(M_0)_{12}v_2/(M_0)_{11}=0.71$ rad/s, is $\tfrac12\big[(M_0)_{22}-(M_0)_{12}^2/(M_0)_{11}\big]v_2^2=0.312$, an upper bound on $\bar W_\tau$. The level itself is the least $W$ on the faces $\tau_i=\pm\tau_{\max,i}$ of the box. On such a face (80) fixes $s_i=\big(g_i(q)\mp\tau_{\max,i}\big)/(K_D)_{ii}$ as a function of $e$, while the other component $s_j$ is left free (this minimizes over the plane of the face, a lower bound for the face itself); minimizing (77) over $s_j$ leaves $\tfrac12\big(M_{ii}-M_{ij}^2/M_{jj}\big)(q)\,s_i^2+\tfrac12e^\top K_ee$, a function of $e$ alone, and since $W\ge\tfrac12e^\top K_ee\ge9.4\,|e|^2$ it needs to be minimized only over a bounded disk of $e$. This gives 0.310 on the face $\tau_2=+30$ N m; the other three faces are reached only at $W\ge0.68$. We take $\bar W=0.3$, about 3% below $\bar W_\tau$.
  • Discretization. The smallest admissible scale is $c\ge\sup_\Omega h\ell/(W-W\circ \Phi_h)$. Near the target this is a generalized eigenvalue problem of the linearization; on $\Omega$ we sampled $4\times10^5$ points and refined the worst ones. For $h=0.005$ s the supremum is 0.965 near the target and 0.994 on $\Omega$, so $c=1$ suffices: the unscaled $W$ is a valid terminal cost.
  • Why not $h=0.01$ s? Then the required scale is 1.69 near the target and 2.27 on $\Omega$. The culprit is the fast mode of the closed loop (eigenvalue $\sigma=-109.5$ s$^{-1}$, so $h|\sigma|\approx1.1$), for which the rectangle rule is a poor approximation of the action. Inflating $W$ would work, as in Section 6.3, but to first order in $h$ it also multiplies the gain of the one-step rule by $c$ (the rule for $cW$ is $u=-cR^{-1}s$), so we halve the step instead.
At $h=0.005$ s the margin is small ($c=1$ against a sampled requirement of 0.994). It exists only because the weights leave slack in (83): $Q_e$ sits at half its bound and $Q_v=4I$ below half of its bound. The slack is barely enough: with $Q_v$ at exactly half its bound, $\tfrac12(K_D-2\gamma M_0)$, the requirement near the target would already be 1.035, and $c=1$ would fail. A design at the knife edge would violate (85) with $c=1$ for small $h$, because its continuous-time limit $\mathcal D\le0$ already fails.
One cycle of the scheme for the arm

  2. 11.7 Torque limits and the terminal constraint as inequalities

The arm’s torque limits and its terminal region are inequalities, and every number above respects them. The theory of Sections 7.1, 7.2 and 8.7 says three things about them.

  • The infinitesimal rule with the torque box is saturated PD control. The box is a constraint $u\in\mathcal U(z)$ on the gravity-compensated input, and by (39), in its phase-space form, the rule minimizes the quadratic $\ell+\dot W$ over it. For the diagonal $R$ used here the minimizer is the componentwise clip of (80), $$\tau^*=\mathrm{clip}\big(g(q)-K_D\dot e-K_Pe,\ -\tau_{\max},\ \tau_{\max}\big),\tag{86}$$ which is how the PD$+g$ runs of Section 11.6 were implemented (their torques never reached the limits). A QP solver confirms the clip on 2000 random states, 1515 of them saturated, to $3\times10^{-14}$. With Gauss’s weight $R=T_0M^{-1}$ the minimizer is instead the $M^{-1}$-metric projection of the unconstrained torque, which is not a clip: at the most extreme of the same states ($T_0=0.05$ s) the unconstrained torque $(214.1,\,37.3)$ N m projects to $(50.0,\,-10.2)$ N m, whereas clipping gives $(50.0,\,30.0)$ N m. Saturating the shoulder reverses the best elbow torque, because the kinetic metric couples the joints.
  • The certificate survives saturation far beyond the torque-limit level. Since the minimum over the box is no smaller than the unconstrained one, $\mathcal D_{\mathcal U}(z)=\min_{u\in\mathcal U(z)}[\ell+\dot W]\ge\mathcal D(z)$. The level $\bar W_\tau\approx0.310$ of Section 11.5 is the level below which the box never binds for the reference law, so there $\mathcal D_{\mathcal U}=\mathcal D$. Beyond it, the saturated law still certifies descent in continuous time on $\{W\le\bar W\}$ for $\bar W$ up to about 5.9 (sampled ray search with local refinement, as in Section 11.4), against about $10^3$ without the box. In discrete time ($h=0.005$ s, saturated reference law, which is admissible by construction) the smallest admissible scale in (85), $\sup h\ell/(W-W\circ\Phi_h)$ over the set, is 1.048 on $\{W\le1\}$ and 1.137 on $\{W\le4\}$, so $c=1.05$ and $c=1.14$ suffice (sampled, with the worst samples refined by local maximization as in Section 11.5). In both sets the worst state lies on the boundary $W=\bar W$, exactly where the unclipped $\tau_2$ in (86) reaches its 30 N m limit. Letting the reference law saturate would enlarge the terminal region more than tenfold, at the price of a slightly inflated $W$.
  • The active terminal constraint inflates the boundary term. At the first step of the $N=10$ run from $(1.0,-0.95)$, six of the twenty planned torques sit at a bound, all with multipliers of the correct sign, and the terminal constraint $W(z_N)\le0.3$ is active with $\mu=39.2$. By (60) the window plans as if its boundary term were $(1+\mu)W\approx40\,W$, which is why its first move is so aggressive (Section 11.6). Along the run $\mu$ falls to 25.9, 17.8, 12.4, 8.4, 5.5, 3.3, 1.7 and 0.59, and the constraint releases at the tenth step, in agreement with the nine active steps reported there.

What the arm teaches.
  1. For the composite energy $W=\tfrac12s^\top Ms+\tfrac12e^\top K_ee$ and a quadratic Lagrangian in the gravity-compensated input, the infinitesimal-window rule is PD control with gravity compensation, $K_D=R^{-1}$, $K_P=R^{-1}\Gamma$ (80). The inertia cancels; the feed-forward of Slotine and Li is not selected.
  2. The certificate is $\mathcal D=-H(z,-\nabla_zW)\le0$. Near the target it reduces to the explicit matching conditions (83). The Slotine–Li spring $K_e=2\Gamma K_D$, correct for its own controller, violates them for every admissible $Q_e$, $Q_v$: a good argmin with a failed certificate.
  3. For this arm the binding limits on the terminal region are the actuators and the step size, not the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality. With $h=0.005$ s the plain $W$ is a valid discrete terminal cost; with $h=0.01$ s it must be inflated by more than 2.
  4. The window length behaves as Section 3.4 predicts: it does not change the certificate, it enlarges the solvable set (${N=1}$ and ${N=5}$ cannot start where ${N=10}$ can) and it lowers $V_N$. It does not automatically lower the action paid: with a conservative $W$ and a hard terminal constraint, short windows are more aggressive than the PD law they generalize.
  5. Torque limits and the terminal region are inequalities. With the torque box the infinitesimal rule is saturated PD$+g$ (86), the certificate survives saturation on a set far larger than the torque-limit level, and an active terminal constraint plans with the inflated boundary term $(1+\mu)W$ ($\mu=39.2$ at the first step of the $N=10$ run).

12. Two Lagrangians and the Stability of Equilibria

It is tempting to ask: if a positive-definite Lagrangian yields stability in Part I, what does the physical Lagrangian $\mathcal T-U$ say about the stability of the plant? The two questions look as if they should be linked, and the signs seem to clash: the hilltop of Section 5.2 is a positive-definite variational Lagrangian whose mechanical reading is an unstable equilibrium. There is no clash: it is a sign difference between two unrelated Lagrangians (the table of Section 8.3). The section ends by placing the impressed and constraint forces of Section 10 on the two sides of this divide.

12.1 The variational Lagrangian

A positive-definite $L$ is a necessary ingredient of the stability argument, not the argument itself. Stability follows because the remaining action $V$ is a Lyapunov function: $V\ge\alpha_1(|q|)$ and $V^+-V\le-L_d$ (Section 3), or $\dot V\le-L$ in continuous time (Section 4; for the infinitesimal window, $V=W$).

12.2 The physical Lagrangian

For a natural system, $L_{\text{phys}}=\mathcal T-U$ is positive definite only if $U$ is negative definite, which puts the origin at a potential maximum. What is known about equilibria of natural systems, with Lagrangian $\mathcal T-U$ and positive-definite kinetic energy $\mathcal T=\tfrac12\dot q^\top M(q)\dot q$, is summarized below.

12.3 Resolution

In Section 5.2 the variational problem is an unstable hilltop, and that is exactly why the optimal path is the stable manifold of a saddle: the extremal rides the separatrix. The physical stability of the plant is a separate matter, governed by the plant’s actual energy $E=\mathcal T+U$, not by the variational Lagrangian.

12.4 Where the two meet: energy shaping

The method of controlled Lagrangians modifies the plant’s Lagrangian so that the closed loop is Lagrangian by construction, which lets energy methods select stabilizing gains.

  • Part I (Bloch et al., 2000) uses kinetic shaping and stabilizes the system modulo a symmetry group.
  • Part II (Bloch et al., 2001) adds potential shaping and feedback dissipation, giving asymptotic stabilization of the full state.

The shaped energy has a minimum at the target. Lagrange–Dirichlet then gives Lyapunov stability, and injected dissipation (with LaSalle’s invariance principle) gives asymptotic stability. This is the physical analogue of Section 3, with the shaped energy playing the role of $V$.

Damping cuts both ways. Here damping helps, because the shaped energy has a minimum. In the gyroscopic row of Section 12.2 it destabilizes, because there the equilibrium is a maximum held only by gyroscopic coupling.

12.5 Impressed and constraint forces on the two sides

The two ways of acting on a task in Section 10.1 fall on opposite sides of the divide between the two Lagrangians.

  • Impressed forces shape the physical Lagrangian. A natural-inertia controller (one task, or several tasks without null-space projections) leaves the kinetic energy alone and reshapes only the potential, $\mathcal T-U\mapsto\mathcal T-U_s$ (gravity compensated, task springs added), then injects damping. Its closed loop is a mechanical system again, and its stability argument is the physical one of Section 12.4: Lagrange–Dirichlet for the shaped energy and LaSalle for the damping. In the language of Part I this energy is a weak boundary term (Section 10.3). Under strict priority the projected task forces are no longer the gradient of one potential, and the argument has to be made level by level (Section 13.5).
  • Constraint forces come from a variational problem. An operational-space controller does not modify the physical Lagrangian; it solves Gauss’s problem with the task as a constraint at every instant and applies the multiplier. The task then behaves as a unit mass. In a Lagrangian picture that would be a reshaping of the kinetic energy (compare the kinetic shaping of Bloch et al., 2000), but here it is produced by a constraint force computed from the model, not by a new closed-loop Lagrangian, and unmeasured external forces still meet the natural inertia (Section 10.1).
  • The receding window sits on the variational side, with a positive-definite Lagrangian and a certificate of Hamilton–Jacobi type. Section 11 shows that its infinitesimal limit can land on a physical-side controller (PD with gravity compensation, the classical potential-shaping law) while the certificate stays variational.
Part III · Hierarchies, power and windows

13. Hierarchy as Gram–Schmidt in the $M^{-1}$ Metric

With several tasks the question “what is the force on task $i$?” acquires a second layer: the same torque can be split among tasks in many ways. Strict priorities pick one split. This section shows that the split is a change of basis, and that one factorization produces all the objects of hierarchical, inertially decoupled control. It starts from the reason a change of basis matters at all.

13.1 Forces are covectors

A multiplier pairs with a velocity to give a power, and only that pairing is independent of the coordinates. Change the coordinates of the velocity, and the multiplier changes by the transposed map. The arm of Section 11 gives a small example: replace the velocity $v$ by the composite velocity $s=v+\Gamma e$ of (77), with $e=q-q_d$ the position error from the constant target (so ${\dot e=v}$). Writing $W(e,v)=\tilde W(e,s)$ with $v=s-\Gamma e$, the chain rule gives $\partial\tilde W/\partial s=\partial W/\partial v$ and $\partial\tilde W/\partial e=\partial W/\partial e-\Gamma^\top\partial W/\partial v$, so in the coordinates $(e,s)$ the momenta are $$\tilde\pi_s=\pi_v,\qquad\tilde\pi_e=\pi_q-\Gamma^\top\pi_v .$$ The velocity momentum, which sets the torque, is unchanged; the position momentum is not, because $\partial W/\partial e$ at fixed $v$ is not $\partial W/\partial e$ at fixed $s$. The pairing $\tilde\pi_e\cdot\dot e+\tilde\pi_s\cdot\dot s=\pi_q\cdot\dot e+\pi_v\cdot\dot v=-\dot W$ is the same in both (checked numerically for the arm, Section 19). The same mechanism appears in task space: the “force on a task” depends on which other task coordinates are held fixed, and a priority order is that choice.

13.2 One factorization

Let task $i$ have coordinates $x_i(q)$ of dimension $n_i$ and Jacobian $J_i=\partial x_i/\partial q$, with $r$ priority levels, $i=1$ the highest. Stack the task Jacobians in priority order, $J=[J_1;\dots;J_r]$, so that the task inertia of Section 9.2 is $\Lambda=(JM^{-1}J^\top)^{-1}$, and that of task $i$ alone $\Lambda_i=(J_iM^{-1}J_i^\top)^{-1}$. Throughout, the stack $J$ has full row rank (no algorithmic singularities; every set of task accelerations is then compatible), except where rank deficiency is discussed explicitly, and the terms $\dot J_i\dot q$ are absorbed in the task accelerations. Factor the stacked mobility (the inverse of the stacked task inertia) as

$$ JM^{-1}J^\top=\Theta\,\bar\Lambda^{-1}\,\Theta^\top,\qquad \Theta=\begin{pmatrix}I&&\\ \Theta_{21}&I&\\ \vdots&&\ddots\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bar\Lambda=\mathrm{blockdiag}(\bar\Lambda_1,\dots,\bar\Lambda_r). \tag{87} $$

This is a block $LDL^\top$ factorization, with $\Theta$ in the role of $L$ (the letter $L$ stays reserved for Lagrangians) and the pivots written as inverse inertias. It is also the block form of Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization of the rows of $J$ in the inner product $\langle a,b\rangle=aM^{-1}b^\top$: each block row is made $M^{-1}$-orthogonal to all rows above it. Define

$$ \bar J=\Theta^{-1}J,\qquad \zeta=\bar J\dot q,\qquad \dot x=J\dot q=\Theta\,\zeta . \tag{88} $$

13.3 What it reproduces

  • Dynamically consistent extended Jacobians. Since $\Theta$ is unit block lower triangular, block row $i$ of $\bar J$ is $J_i$ minus its $M^{-1}$-orthogonal projection on the rows above it, $$\bar J_i=J_i\big(I-J_{<i}^{M+}J_{<i}\big),\qquad J_{<i}^{M+}=M^{-1}J_{<i}^\top\big(J_{<i}M^{-1}J_{<i}^\top\big)^{-1},\tag{89}$$ with $J_{<i}$ the stack of higher-priority tasks. That is $J_i$ times the dynamically consistent null-space projector of the higher tasks: the projected (null-space-restricted) task Jacobian of hierarchical control (Dietrich et al., 2015; Ott et al., 2015). We call the stack $\bar J$ the extended Jacobian, following that literature; it is unrelated to Baillieul’s extended-Jacobian method. Checked numerically to $2.1\times10^{-15}$.
  • Dynamic consistency. By construction $J_{<i}M^{-1}\bar J_i^\top=0$: a torque $\bar J_i^\top F_i$ produces no acceleration of any higher-priority task.
  • Decoupled inertias. $\bar JM^{-1}\bar J^\top=\Theta^{-1}JM^{-1}J^\top\Theta^{-\top}=\bar\Lambda^{-1}$ is block diagonal, so $\bar\Lambda_i$ is the inertia seen by level $i$. If the stack is square ($\sum_i n_i=n$, completing it with a null-space task if needed), the kinetic energy splits: $$\tfrac12\,\dot q^\top M\dot q=\sum_i\tfrac12\,\zeta_i^\top\bar\Lambda_i\,\zeta_i .\tag{90}$$ This is the inertially decoupled structure exploited by hierarchical passivity-based controllers (Ott et al., 2015; Dietrich & Ott, 2020; Garofalo & Ott, 2020; Garofalo, 2024).
  • Conflicts and singularities. If a level is (partly) in conflict with higher levels, its pivot $\bar J_iM^{-1}\bar J_i^\top$ is singular. Using pseudo-inverse pivots in (87) still reproduces (89) with pseudo-inverses, and $\bar J$ stays unique; $\Theta$ is then not unique, neither are $\lambda$ and $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ (Section 13.4), and $\bar\Lambda_i$ exists only as a pseudo-inverse. Rank deficiency of the stack is a property of the Jacobians; whether the task targets are then compatible is a separate question, which decides whether the lower task is met or only satisfied in a least-squares sense.
Inertial decoupling is not dynamic decoupling. For a square stack and $\tau=g+\bar J^\top F$, the dynamics in the decoupled velocities read $\bar\Lambda\dot\zeta+\bar C\,\zeta=F$, with $\bar C$ the Coriolis matrix in these coordinates and $\dot{\bar\Lambda}-2\bar C$ skew-symmetric. Since $\bar\Lambda$ is block diagonal, the off-diagonal blocks satisfy $\bar C_{ij}=-\bar C_{ji}^\top$, and $$\frac{d}{dt}\,\tfrac12\,\zeta_i^\top\bar\Lambda_i\zeta_i=\zeta_i^\top F_i-\sum_{j\ne i}\zeta_i^\top\bar C_{ij}\zeta_j .$$ The Coriolis couplings exchange kinetic energy between levels without creating any (the coupling terms cancel in the sum over $i$). Hierarchical controllers compensate them or carry them through the proof (Ott et al., 2015; Dietrich & Ott, 2020). For Section 15 this means that $F_i^\top\zeta_i$ is the power injected into level $i$, not the rate of change of its kinetic energy.
The factorization is the computational content; the result is not new. Successive dynamically consistent projection is the standard construction, and sparse factorizations of the joint-space inertia matrix make the operational-space inertia and the dynamically consistent projectors cheap to compute (Featherstone, 2010); in prioritized whole-body control the decoupled inertias $\bar\Lambda_i$ are the prioritized task inertias (Sentis & Khatib, 2005). The dual statement of the next subsection, the change of basis $F=\Theta^\top\lambda$ between decoupled forces and stacked multipliers, follows directly from the velocity relation (88); we make it explicit because it organizes the rest of Part III.

13.4 Forces on a task depend on the basis

Write $\tau_x$ for the torque produced through the task channel. In the constraint view it is the constraint torque $\tau_c$ of Section 9.2, with the stacked multipliers $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r)$ of the stacked constraints; in the impressed view it is the task part of $\tau_{\rm imp}$ ($J^\top F$ of Section 10.1, and $\bar J^\top F$ level by level, with decoupled task forces $F=(F_1,\dots,F_r)$; a single task is the case $r=1$, $\bar J=J$). In both views it can be written in two ways: through the stacked Jacobian with stacked multipliers, or through the extended Jacobians with decoupled forces. Since $J=\Theta\bar J$, the transpose of the velocity relation (88) gives

$$ \tau_x=J^\top\lambda=\bar J^\top F \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F=\Theta^\top\lambda,\qquad F_i=\lambda_i+\sum_{j>i}\Theta_{ji}^\top\lambda_j . \tag{91} $$

In the constraint view, $\lambda$ are the multipliers of the stacked Gauss problem (64); in the impressed view, $F$ are the forces the controller designs per level. Equation (91) is the dictionary between them. It is Newton’s law written in two coordinate systems: multiplying $\tau_x=J^\top\lambda$ and $\tau_x=\bar J^\top F$ by $JM^{-1}$ and $\bar JM^{-1}$ and using (87),

$$ \lambda=\Lambda\,\big(JM^{-1}\tau_x\big),\qquad F_i=\bar\Lambda_i\,\big(\bar J_iM^{-1}\tau_x\big): \tag{92} $$

each is an inertia times the acceleration that $\tau_x$ produces, $\lambda$ in the stacked task coordinates with their coupled inertia $\Lambda$, and $F_i$ in the decoupled coordinate of level $i$ with its decoupled inertia $\bar\Lambda_i$. This is the covector mechanism of Section 13.1: a component of a force depends on which other coordinates are held fixed. Three consequences:

  • The effort separates by level (in the constraint view, the Gauss function): $\tfrac12\tau_x^\top M^{-1}\tau_x=\tfrac12F^\top\bar JM^{-1}\bar J^\top F=\sum_i\tfrac12F_i^\top\bar\Lambda_i^{-1}F_i$.
  • Only the last level agrees: $F_r=\lambda_r$. Every higher level carries, in addition, the multipliers of the lower levels mapped through $\Theta$.
  • The priority order is a choice of basis. With the same torque and the order reversed, the force attributed to a task in general changes; it cannot for a task with $J_iM^{-1}J_k^\top=0$ for all $k\neq i$ (see the end of this subsection). In a fixed-seed example (random symmetric positive-definite $M$, Jacobians and stacked multipliers $\lambda$, $n=7$, task dimensions 2, 3, 2, and a random joint velocity $\dot q$ for the powers of Section 15.1), task 1 carries $F_1=(-0.079,\,-1.197)$ at first priority and $(0.260,\,-0.858)$ at last priority, which equals its stacked multiplier.
What “the force acting on a task” means. There is no basis-free answer. The multiplier $\lambda_i$ is the force on task $i$ as seen in the stacked task coordinates; $F_i$ is the force on task $i$ in the decoupled coordinates of the hierarchy. They coincide, in general, only for the lowest-priority task. In the constraint view with a feasible stack, all orders produce the same torque and the same acceleration; the order then matters for the motion only under conflict or singularity (including conflicts that active inequalities create, Section 14), but it matters, in general, for the attribution of forces. By (92), task $i$ carries $F_i=\Lambda_iJ_iM^{-1}\tau_x=\lambda_i+\Lambda_i\sum_{k\neq i}J_iM^{-1}J_k^\top\lambda_k$ at first priority (where $\bar J_i=J_i$ and $\bar\Lambda_i=\Lambda_i$) and $F_i=\lambda_i$ at last priority. For generic multipliers the two differ unless $J_iM^{-1}J_k^\top=0$ for all $k\neq i$; in that case (89) gives $\bar J_i=J_i$, hence $F_i=\lambda_i$, in every order. In the impressed view, where the designer prescribes each $F_i$, the order in general changes the torque itself, because $\bar J$ changes with it (checked on a feasible stack, Section 19). Neither effect occurs if the stacked task inertia $\Lambda$ is already block diagonal ($J_iM^{-1}J_k^\top=0$ for all $i\neq k$; for example, hand tasks on two arms mounted on a fixed base with no joint in common): then $\bar J=J$, $\Theta=I$ and $F=\lambda$ in every order.

13.5 Strict priority and the leak

Suppose a lower-priority task $i$ wants to apply a force $f_i$, for example $f_i=-\nabla U_i(x_i)$ from its potential $U_i(x_i)$. Written with its own Jacobian, the torque spreads over all levels at and above $i$: by (88), $J_i=\sum_{j\le i}\Theta_{ij}\bar J_j$ (with $\Theta_{ii}=I$), so

$$ J_i^\top f_i=\sum_{j\le i}\bar J_j^\top \Theta_{ij}^\top f_i . \tag{93} $$

The terms $j<i$ disturb higher-priority tasks. Strict priority keeps only $j=i$, i.e. applies $\bar J_i^\top f_i$. This has a consequence for the variational picture: the resulting torque is in general not the gradient of a potential on $q$, because the projected Jacobians are not integrable. There is then no single potential, and hence no single $W$ of the form (69), whose descent certifies the whole hierarchy. The stability analyses of hierarchical passivity-based controllers accordingly work level by level and handle the remaining couplings explicitly (Ott et al., 2015; Dietrich & Ott, 2020; Garofalo & Ott, 2020). In the language of Part I, strict priority is lexicographic (as in hierarchical quadratic programming; Escande et al., 2014), not the argmin of one remaining action.

14. Inequality Tasks in a Hierarchy

Joint limits, obstacle distances and contact conditions enter whole-body control as inequality tasks, at any priority level. This section extends Section 13 to them, with the unilateral forces of Section 9.4 as the building block.

14.1 The lexicographic problem and its active set

A strict hierarchy with inequalities is a lexicographic optimization: level by level, minimize the violation of that level’s equalities and inequalities while keeping the optimal violations of all levels above, with Gauss’s function closing the hierarchy as the last level (Kanoun et al., 2011; Escande et al., 2014). Two facts organize the rest of this section.

  • For a fixed active set it is an equality hierarchy. Active inequality rows (the set $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal I$ held at their bounds) are kept, inactive ones are dropped, and Section 13 applies to the active stack $J_{\mathcal A}$ (the active rows inserted at their levels): one factorization $J_{\mathcal A}M^{-1}J_{\mathcal A}^\top=\Theta_{\mathcal A}\bar\Lambda_{\mathcal A}^{-1}\Theta_{\mathcal A}^\top$, extended Jacobians $\bar J_{\mathcal A}=\Theta_{\mathcal A}^{-1}J_{\mathcal A}$ and decoupled forces $F=\Theta_{\mathcal A}^\top\lambda_{\mathcal A}$.
  • A feasible stack is a single QP. If all tasks can be met at once, the lexicographic solution is the Gauss problem with every row as a constraint, (66); the order then decides only the attribution, as in Section 13.4. The multipliers carry the signs of (66), and the solution of this strictly convex QP depends continuously on its data as long as the active rows stay linearly independent.

Garofalo (2024) uses this structure in the passivity-based setting: a weighted hierarchical QP extracts the set of active tasks, and the stack of active tasks defines the coordinate transformation that inertially decouples them, with the natural inertia preserved.

14.2 Activation and deactivation at any level

An inequality row switches in two ways, both read off its complementary pair in (66), its multiplier and its margin (its entry of $J_{\mathcal I}\ddot q-a_{\mathcal I}$): it activates when its margin reaches zero while its multiplier is still zero, and it deactivates when its multiplier returns to zero while its margin is still zero. These are events at the acceleration level, not at the position level: with an acceleration bound that is a feedback law (Section 9.4) a row can become active before its coordinate reaches its position bound, and it can be active while its coordinate recedes, as in the example of Section 9.4. At the switching instant both complementary quantities vanish, so the solution with the row dropped, given a zero multiplier for the row, also satisfies the KKT conditions with the row held at its bound; by strict convexity the two solutions coincide there. Hence in the constraint view, for a feasible stack whose data $M$, $\ddot q_{\rm free}$, $J_{\mathcal E}$, $J_{\mathcal I}$, $a_{\mathcal E}$, $a_{\mathcal I}$ vary continuously in time and whose active rows stay linearly independent (Section 14.1), the torque is continuous across the switch. This excludes impacts. A row imposed only on the boundary, as for a contact, enters the problem without a jump only if its coordinate arrives there with zero velocity: a contact that closes with nonzero approach velocity cannot be kept from penetrating by any finite force, and the impulse it needs makes the velocity, and in general the data of (66) and the torque, jump. Impacts are not treated here (Sections 9.4 and 17). The active stack, and with it the basis of Section 13, changes discontinuously. Since Gram–Schmidt orthogonalizes each level only against the levels above it, a switch at level $i$ leaves $\bar J_j$, $\zeta_j$ and $\Theta_{kj}$ for $j<i$, and hence by (92) the decoupled forces and powers of every level above $i$, untouched; it re-orthogonalizes level $i$ and everything below.

In a fixed-seed example ($n=7$; random inertia, Jacobians and free acceleration, and a random joint velocity $\dot q$ for the powers; a two-row equality task at level 1; at level 2 a one-row inequality $J_2\ddot q\ge a_2(t)$, an upper bound on a task coordinate $x_2$ imposed at the acceleration level, $J_2=-\partial x_2/\partial q$; a three-row equality task at level 3; feasible) the bound $a_2(t)$ rises and falls so that the row activates at $t=1/6$ and deactivates at $t=5/6$. Along the sweep $\lambda_2\ge0$ throughout, the torque changes by at most $1.8\times10^{-2}$ per step of $3.3\times10^{-4}$ in $t$, and by $2.9\times10^{-3}$ across either switch. In the impressed view, where the designer prescribes the $F_i$, the task torque $\tau_x=\bar J^\top F$ jumps at a switch unless the forces are re-mapped through (91), because $\bar J$ changes. Let $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal A'$ be the active sets before and after the switch, and $\lambda_{\mathcal A}=\Theta_{\mathcal A}^{-\top}F$ the stacked multipliers of the prescribed forces. A rule that drops the row when its own decoupled force $F_i$ returns to zero does not ensure $\lambda_i=0$: by (91) it leaves $\lambda_i=-\sum_{j>i}\Theta_{ji}^\top\lambda_j$, which is nonzero in general.

14.3 A sign on the multiplier is not a sign on the decoupled force

The multiplier of an active inequality row is non-negative, (66). Its decoupled force is $F_i=\lambda_i+\sum_{j>i}\Theta_{ji}^\top\lambda_j$ by (91), and the second term has no sign. In the example of Section 14.2, over the whole active interval $\lambda_2$ ranges from $0$ to $31.06$ while $F_2$ stays between $-11.50$ and $-4.02$: the force that the hierarchy attributes to the inequality task points against the only direction in which the constraint can push. With the row oriented the other way ($J_2=+\partial x_2/\partial q$, a lower instead of an upper bound on $x_2$), $F_2$ ranges from $+11.50$ to $+18.98$ over the same range of $\lambda_2$; the multipliers of the other levels change with the orientation. The sign of $F_i$ is set by the lower levels’ multipliers through $\Theta$, not by the inequality. Together with Section 9.4: the sign of a unilateral multiplier survives neither a change of basis nor a pairing with velocity.

14.4 Conflicts and soft priorities

When the stack is infeasible, the lexicographic solution is no longer a single QP. Each level minimizes its own violation inside the optimal set of the levels above, and the sign condition $\lambda\ge0$ belongs to the level at which an inequality is decided. A weighted problem, all levels in one QP with weights $w_i$, replaces strict priority by soft priority, and strict priority is its limit as the weight ratios grow. In a fixed-seed example ($n=7$, a two-row inequality at level 1 in conflict with a seven-row posture task at level 2) the weighted solution approaches the lexicographic one like $0.1218/w$, with $w=w_1/w_2$ the weight ratio: the distance is $1.2\times10^{-3}$ at $w=10^2$ and $1.2\times10^{-6}$ at $w=10^5$, where the inequality is violated by $2.0\times10^{-6}$. Soft priorities within a level, as in weighted hierarchical QPs, are this weighted problem restricted to the tasks of one level: the block Gram–Schmidt picture applies between levels, while within a level the weights decide how a conflict is shared. With conflicts, continuity across active-set changes needs care; Mansard et al. (2009) analyse the irregularities that unilateral constraints create in task hierarchies and show how to insert them at any priority level. Our continuity checks cover the feasible case only.

15. Power, Ports and Energy Tanks

Sections 13 and 14 attributed forces to tasks in a basis that depends on the priority order and on the active set. This section asks which power flows survive that choice, and what this means for energy tanks.

15.1 Total power is invariant

Combine (88) and (91): the power injected through the task channel is

$$ \tau_x^\top\dot q=F^\top \zeta=\lambda^\top\dot x , \tag{94} $$

because $\tau_x^\top\dot q=F^\top\bar J\dot q=F^\top\zeta$ and $F^\top \zeta=\lambda^\top\Theta\,\zeta=\lambda^\top\dot x$. This is the pairing of a covector with a velocity (Section 13.1): the total is the same in every basis and for every priority order. Its split into levels, $F_i^\top \zeta_i$, is not. In the example of Section 13.4 the total is $-0.391$ in both orders, while the per-level powers are $(3.057,\,-3.416,\,-0.032)$ for tasks $(1,2,3)$ in the order $1\succ2\succ3$, and $(0.033,\,-0.446,\,0.022)$ for the same tasks in the reversed order.

15.2 Power with unilateral forces and at switches

Three statements, each checked on the examples of Sections 9.4 and 14.2:

  • Totals are continuous, attributions are not. In the constraint view, for a feasible stack, the torque, and with it the total power (94), is continuous across activation and deactivation. The levels above the switching level keep their power; the switching level and the levels below exchange it by re-attribution ($-89.066$ on the last level becomes $-13.476-75.590$ in the example).
  • The power of a unilateral force has no sign. It vanishes on a persistent contact, and it is positive for an active, feedback-bounded inequality task whose coordinate recedes ($+0.805$ in the example).
  • In the impressed view the total jumps as well, because the torque does: it changes by $\Delta\tau_x^\top\dot q$, nonzero in general, unless the prescribed forces are re-mapped through (91) at the switch (at a deactivation this also needs the stacked multiplier of the leaving row to be zero, Section 14.2). In the first example of Section 14.2, with $F_1$ and $F_3$ held and $F_2=0$ at the activation, the level powers are $-10.024$, $0$ and $-75.590$: the total $-99.090$ becomes $-10.024-75.590$, a jump of $+13.476$, which is minus the power $-13.476$ of the re-mapped $F_2=-11.501$.

15.3 Consequences for tanks

Energy tanks make a controller passive by routing every non-passive power flow through a storage element that must stay positive (Dietrich et al., 2017; Garofalo & Ott, 2018). Sections 15.1 and 15.2 say what such a tank observes:

  • a single tank on the total task port sees $\tau_x^\top\dot q=\lambda^\top\dot x=F^\top \zeta$ (94), which for a given task torque $\tau_x$ does not depend on the priority order used to attribute it. In the constraint view with a feasible stack the torque itself is the same for every order (Section 14.1) and continuous across active-set switches (Section 14.2), and so is this flow. In the impressed view, with prescribed $F_i$, the torque $\bar J^\top F$, and with it the flow, in general changes with the order and jumps at a switch, because $\bar J$ does, unless the forces are re-mapped through (91) (at a deactivation only if the stacked multiplier of the leaving row is zero, Section 14.2);
  • per-level tanks see $F_i^\top \zeta_i$: flows that depend on the order and jump when the active set changes, even when the torque does not. A level can appear with a nonzero decoupled force and zero multiplier ($F_2=-11.50$ in Section 14.2), and an inequality task’s own power can be positive.

Chattering. In discrete time an active set can toggle from one sample to the next near a bound. Every toggle re-attributes power at and below the switching level even when the torque barely changes, so per-level tanks would see spurious exchanges, and a single tank on the total port would not. Hysteresis in the activation, or a bound placed at the velocity or position level instead of the acceleration level, are natural remedies. Tanks for strict hierarchies exist (Dietrich et al., 2017), but we know of no analysis of tank bookkeeping across inequality active-set switches.

Design question this raises. If per-level tanks are used (to make each level individually passive), the bookkeeping at switches must transfer energy between levels consistently with the change of basis (91): at an activation or deactivation at level $i$ only level $i$ and the levels below are affected, and the transfer must also absorb the power of unilateral forces, which has no sign. How to do that while preserving strict priority, and without chattering, is, to our knowledge, not settled in general; it is the most concrete open problem suggested by this viewpoint.

16. Windows, Priorities and Whole-Body MPC

Part I shrank a receding window to an instant; the preceding sections split one instant among priority levels. This section puts the two side by side.

16.1 Is whole-body control the zero-window limit of whole-body MPC?

Parts I and II give a qualified answer.

  • With an energy-type boundary term, only if the stiffness is put in by hand. The torque enters the $\dot W$ of (69) only through the power $\dot q^\top\tau$, so the infinitesimal rule (70) adds pure damping, $-R^{-1}\dot q$, to the torque on which the stage cost is centred (Sections 10.2–⁠10.3). A task-error penalty in $\ell_0$ does not depend on $\tau$, so it drops out of the argmin. The stiffness it gives a whole-body MPC over a finite window is therefore lost in the zero-window limit. The penalty then enters only the certificate, which it violates at rest away from the target (Section 10.3). Centred on $\tau_{\rm imp}=g-\nabla U_s$, with $U_s$ the potential in $W$, the rule is the passivity-based law with natural inertia of Section 10.2. Its stiffness comes from the centring, not from the minimization, and the energy certifies only the damping (71). Under strict priority the centring torque is in general not of this form, and no single $W$ of the form (69) certifies the result (Sections 13.5 and 16.3). The minimization itself produces stiffness only with a $W$ that has position–velocity cross terms (Section 11), or with a finite window. In the latter case the resulting gains depend on how the torque is held over the window (zero-order hold, impulse, or time-varying), not only on the window length. Take the one-degree-of-freedom setup of Section 9.1: mass $M=2$ kg, $W=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12K_eq^2$ with $K_e=30$ N/m, Gauss’s weight with $T_0=0.1$ s centred on zero torque ($\tau_{\rm imp}=0$, so the spring sits only in $W$ and the infinitesimal rule $\tau=-Mv/T_0$ has no stiffness), no other stage cost, window $\vartheta=10$ ms. With the torque held constant over the window, the window problem is a scalar quadratic in the torque, and its minimizer has the first-instant stiffness $K_e\vartheta/{\big[2\big(T_0+\vartheta+K_e\vartheta^3/(4M)\big)\big]}=1.36$ N/m. With the optimal time-varying torque, the window is solved by the Riccati differential equation that (18) becomes in phase space for a quadratic boundary term (as in Section 5.2), started at $P_0=\mathrm{diag}(K_e,M)$, and the first-instant stiffness is $(P_\vartheta)_{12}/T_0=2.86$ N/m.
  • With the value function of a longer horizon as $W$, yes in spirit. A one-step rule whose boundary term is the value function of a longer-horizon problem is the construction behind value-function-based whole-body control in cascaded MPC (Li & Wensing, 2025). With a control Lyapunov function in that role, the one-step rule is a pointwise controller built on that function, the kind of law that Primbs et al. (2000) generalize to receding horizons (Section 4.5). In continuous time it is the infinitesimal-window rule (20), the zero-window end of the receding window that keeps the function as boundary term (Section 4.4), the terminal-cost scheme of Chen & Allgöwer (1998) and Jadbabaie et al. (2001). Primbs et al. generalize by a different construction. In their window problem the control Lyapunov function enters as constraints, not as a boundary term: a decrease condition on the first move, and an end-of-window condition that its value at the end of the window not exceed its value at the end of the path the pointwise min-norm controller would follow over the same window. As the window shrinks to zero their scheme reduces to pointwise min-norm control, which prescribes a rate at which $W$ must decrease and takes the least-norm velocity that achieves it; under further conditions it approaches optimal control as the window grows without bound. The rule (20) is of that kind, with $L$ in place of the norm and with the rate it delivers itself: for every $v\in\mathcal V(q)$ with $\nabla W\cdot v\le\nabla W\cdot v^*$, minimality of $v^*$ gives $L(q,v)\ge L(q,v^*)+\nabla W\cdot(v^*-v)\ge L(q,v^*)$. The two zero-window ends therefore agree where the prescribed rate equals $-\nabla W\cdot v^*$, and need not agree elsewhere.
  • Multipliers. In a multiple-shooting transcription the defect multipliers are discrete momenta (Section 8.7), and the first torque equals the first velocity momentum over $T_0$: exactly in continuous time; in discrete time, exactly under symplectic Euler when neither $b$ nor the stage cost depends on $v$, $T_0\tau_0=\pi_{v,0}=-\nabla_vV_N(z_0)$ with $\pi_0$ the multiplier of the initial value; as the mean $T_0\tau_0=\tfrac12(\pi_{v,0}+\pi_{v,1})$ under zero-order hold with constant $M$ and $b$; and to $O(h)$ otherwise (Section 9.1). The mean is why the held-torque stiffness above is about half the time-varying one. Per unit initial displacement, the velocity momentum of the held window gives $-\pi_{v,0}/T_0=2.864$ N/m at its start, essentially the time-varying first-instant value 2.86 N/m, and $-\pi_{v,1}/T_0=-0.136$ N/m at its end; the held torque takes their mean, 1.36 N/m.

16.2 Invariant versus attributed: windows and priorities

Two design choices in this document leave a certificate-level quantity untouched and redistribute something else. The parallel is structural, not a theorem; this subsection states what is exact and where it stops.

Window length. With an exact pair, $W=V_\infty$ for the discrete problem and no active constraint, the fixed-point property of Section 6.3 gives $V_N=V_\infty$ for every $N$: the window length only moves the cut between the computed part of the remaining action (the window) and the estimated part (the tail $W$). With an inexact pair that passes the one-step test (8) the total itself changes with $N$, downward by (10), while the certificate $(W,\Omega)$ stays the same; a pair that fails (8) has no certificate to keep, and its total can rise with $N$ instead (the textbook weight of Section 6.5). The table of Section 6.5 shows both: $V_N(q_0)=0.5000$ for every $N$ with the exact pair, against $0.5857$ at $N=1$ falling to $0.5269$ at $N=20$ (towards $P_d/2=0.5256$) with the inflated rectangle-rule pair.

Priority order. For a given task torque $\tau_x$ (in either view), every priority order gives the same total power (94), and only its split among levels changes (Section 15.1). In the constraint view with a feasible stack the torque itself does not depend on the order, with or without active inequality rows: the stack is then a single QP (Section 14.1), whose solution, and with it the active set, is the same for every order. The order is then a pure change of basis, and an active-set switch only re-attributes forces and power at and below the switching level, with the torque continuous (Sections 14.2 and 15.2). Under a conflict, that is, when the tasks cannot all be met at once (incompatible targets at a singularity of the stack, Section 13.3, or an inequality bound that cannot be held together with the other tasks, Section 14.4), or in the impressed view where each level’s force is prescribed, the order can change the torque and the motion.

16.3 Toward hierarchical certificates

For hierarchical natural-inertia controllers the value-function construction of Section 16.1 meets the obstacle of Section 13.5: there is no $W$ of the form (69) for a strict hierarchy, and no general construction of a strict one is known to us. A hierarchical receding-horizon certificate would therefore most naturally be cascaded level by level: a boundary term per level in the decoupled coordinates $\zeta_i$, with the Coriolis couplings $\bar C_{ij}$ of Section 13.3 treated as interconnection terms, as the level-by-level stability analyses cited in Section 13.5 do. Whether per-level descent defects can be made to add up, and how the per-level power of Section 15 enters them at active-set switches, is open.

17. Floating Base and Contacts (Outlook)

For a floating-base robot the reading needs one more constraint. Six coordinates give the pose of the base; write $(\cdot)_{\rm base}$ for the six base rows of a vector and $M_{\rm base}$ for those of $M$. With $J_c$ the Jacobian of the closed contacts and $f_c$ their wrenches, the equations of motion are $M\ddot q+b=\tau+J_c^\top f_c$, and the base is unactuated, $\tau_{\rm base}=0$. The impressed torque must then be a joint torque too, $(\tau_{\rm imp})_{\rm base}=0$: gravity can be compensated in the joint rows only, and the weight has to be carried by $f_c$. The base rows $$\big(M\ddot q+b-J_c^\top f_c\big)_{\rm base}=0$$ are the Newton–Euler equations of the whole robot. In inertially decoupled coordinates that separate the centroidal dynamics from the joint dynamics (Garofalo et al., 2015) they say that only gravity and the contact wrenches change the centroidal momentum, which is the statement behind momentum-based whole-body control (in the decoupled velocity coordinates the angular part still keeps Coriolis coupling with the joints).

The Gauss problems of Sections 9.2–⁠9.4 do not contain these rows. With the contacts as rows (Section 9.3, multipliers $\lambda_c$) the correction is $\tau_c=J^\top\lambda+J_c^\top\lambda_c$. Read with $f_c=\lambda_c$, it asks the actuators for the base rows $(J^\top\lambda)_{\rm base}$, which do not vanish in general: a task expressed in the world frame, such as a hand position, depends on the pose of the base. The base rows therefore enter Gauss’s problem as constraints, with $f_c$ as an additional unknown and $\lambda_{\rm base}$ as their multiplier. Stationarity in $\ddot q$ gives $M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})=J^\top\lambda+J_c^\top\lambda_c+M_{\rm base}^\top\lambda_{\rm base}$, and stationarity in $f_c$ makes $\lambda_{\rm base}$ orthogonal to every base wrench the contacts can exert, $\lambda_{\rm base}^\top(J_c^\top f)_{\rm base}=0$ for all $f$. Two cases follow.

  • The contacts can exert any base wrench (as equalities: one foot or both feet flat on the ground, or three point contacts not on one line). Then $\lambda_{\rm base}=0$, and the motion and the multipliers are those of the stack without the base rows. Their reading changes. The contact wrench is $f_c=\lambda_c+\Delta f_c$, with $(J_c^\top\Delta f_c)_{\rm base}=(J^\top\lambda)_{\rm base}$ and $\Delta f_c$ unique up to internal forces, and the actuators add $J^\top\lambda-J_c^\top\Delta f_c$ to $\tau_{\rm imp}$, not $J^\top\lambda$. On persistent contacts ($J_c\dot q=0$) the difference does no work, so (94) still gives the power that the actuators inject on top of $\tau_{\rm imp}$.
  • They cannot (one or two point contacts, a line contact, flight). Then the base rows bind, in general, in the directions of base wrench that the contacts cannot exert, and the task multipliers $\lambda$ change. In those directions the base rows are equality rows like a task’s, with their Jacobian taken from $M_{\rm base}$ and, in place of a task velocity, the momentum $(M\dot q)_{\rm base}$ conjugate to the base coordinates, the total momentum of the robot. Placed at the top of the stack with the contact rows, they make Sections 13–⁠15 apply to the enlarged stack, and on persistent contacts the power that the actuators inject on top of $\tau_{\rm imp}$ is $\lambda^\top\dot x+\lambda_{\rm base}^\top(M\dot q)_{\rm base}$, not $\lambda^\top\dot x$.

In both cases the sign and friction-cone conditions below bind $f_c$, not $\lambda_c$. The contact wrenches are the multipliers of the contact constraints in the plant’s own Gauss principle (of a contact that slides with friction, only $f_n$ is; see “Contact modes” below), with the applied joint torque as the impressed torque. They are constraint forces supplied by the environment, not by the actuators, yet the Gauss function (62) counts them: they are part of $\tau_c=M(\ddot q-\ddot q_{\rm free})$, as the unilateral forces of (66) are. The centroidal momentum, finally, is part of the mechanical momentum $M\dot q$, not of the costate momentum $\pi=-\nabla_zV$ of Section 8. The two coincide (up to sign, $\pi_v=-Mv$ with $v=\dot q$ along solutions) for the infinitesimal window, where $\pi=-\nabla_zW$, when $W$ is of energy type (69), which certifies only damping (Section 10.3). Over a finite window $V_\vartheta$ is in general not of energy type: in the example of Section 16.1, $\pi_v=-\nabla_vV_\vartheta$ has the position component $-(P_\vartheta)_{12}\,q$ that gives the stiffness there.

A contact is an inequality constraint with three parts.

  • Normal complementarity. Gap $x_c\ge0$, normal force $f_n\ge0$, $f_nx_c=0$ (Signorini), imposed on a closed contact at the velocity level (Moreau) or the acceleration level (Gauss) as in Section 9.4. Persistent contacts do no work; with Moreau’s impact law, impacts lose energy for restitution coefficients up to one (Moreau, 1988; Brogliato, 2016), a property of that law, not of every restitution model.
  • Friction. Coulomb’s law $\|f_t\|\le\mu_cf_n$, with tangential force $f_t$ and friction coefficient $\mu_c$, is a second-order cone, not a polyhedron; QP-based controllers replace it by an inscribed pyramid, that is, by more linear inequality rows. Sticking contacts do no work; a sliding contact with $f_t=-\mu_cf_n\,v_t/\|v_t\|$ dissipates the power $\mu_cf_n\|v_t\|\ge0$.
  • Contact modes. A mode says which contacts are closed and which of them stick or slide. In a mode without frictional sliding (every closed contact frictionless, or sticking with its force in the friction cone) the active contact constraints are ideal: their forces lie in the span of the transposed constrained rows, as in (66). Sections 13, 14 and 15 then apply to them verbatim: (91)–⁠(94), the attribution rules and the sign caveats. On a floating base the stack is the one described above (the contact rows, the base rows where they bind, and the tasks), with $f_c$ and the actuator power read as stated there. A contact that slides with friction is not ideal. For a point contact, write $J_n$ and $J_t$ for the normal and tangential rows of its Jacobian $J_c$, $v_t=J_t\dot q$ and $\hat v_t=v_t/\|v_t\|$. Only the normal acceleration $J_n\ddot q$ is constrained, while the force $(J_n-\mu_c\hat v_t^\top J_t)^\top f_n$ is not in the span of $J_n^\top$, so Gauss’s principle (61), which would apply $J_n^\top f_n$, no longer yields the motion. The friction torque $-\mu_cJ_t^\top\hat v_t\,f_n$ can be booked as impressed instead, but it depends on the multiplier $f_n$, so the free acceleration in (61) is no longer given. Eliminating $\ddot q$ maps the multipliers to the constrained accelerations through $JM^{-1}\tilde J^\top$, where $\tilde J$ is $J$ with each sliding row $J_n$ replaced by $J_n-\mu_c\hat v_t^\top J_t$. This matrix takes the place of the mobility $JM^{-1}J^\top$ of (87), and it is not symmetric once another row is active. For a single sliding contact and no other rows it is the scalar $J_nM^{-1}(J_n-\mu_c\hat v_t^\top J_t)^\top$, which can be negative when $\mu_c$ is large; the contact problem then has no solution or two (Painlevé’s paradox; Brogliato, 2016). When the multipliers exist, (91)–⁠(94) hold only for the ideal part $J^\top\lambda$ of the constraint torque. The friction torque, booked as impressed, is attributed to no level, and its power $-\mu_cf_n\|v_t\|$ is missing from $\lambda^\top\dot x$ in (94), to which the sliding row contributes $f_nJ_n\dot q=0$. Opening a frictionless or sticking contact (by the cone, its tangential force vanishes with $f_n$) and closing a frictionless one without impact are active-set switches in the sense of Section 14.2; across them the torque stays continuous only in the constraint view. Stick–slip transitions and the closing of a frictional contact are not. A frictional contact that closes without impact generally starts by sliding. At the onset of slip the tangential rows leave the active set with their multiplier on the boundary of the cone, not at zero. At a slip-to-stick transition the friction force in general jumps from the boundary of the cone into its interior (for a block that slides to rest on a level floor with no tangential load, from $\mu_c$ times its weight to zero), so the contact force and, in the constraint view, the torque jump without any impact.

A careful treatment of frictional sliding, impacts, friction-cone switching and tank bookkeeping across contact modes is left for later.

Part IV · Synthesis and verification

18. Synthesis

The receding-window results grew from one root, the principle of optimality of Section 2.2: its monotone recursion gave descent (Section 3), its limit the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the momentum (Section 4), its characteristics the extremals (Section 5), its fixed points exactness (Section 6.3), and its phase-space form the momenta, the multipliers of the equations of motion (Section 8). The whole-body results draw on two further ingredients that do not follow from it: Gauss’s principle with tasks imposed as constraints (Sections 9.2–⁠9.4), in which no boundary term enters and whose multipliers are the task forces; and one block factorization of the stacked mobility (Section 13.2), from which (89)–⁠(94) and the attribution rules of Sections 14 and 15 follow by linear algebra. Gauss’s weight ties both to the root: with a boundary term, Gauss’s problem is the infinitesimal-window rule (63); with a constraint, it is operational-space control (64); and its metric $M^{-1}$ is the inner product of the factorization, which rewrites the stacked multipliers as decoupled forces (91). Strict priority, finally, is lexicographic, not the argmin of one remaining action (Sections 13.5, 14.1), and the stability arguments built on the mechanical energy (Sections 10.3, 11.4 and 12) are classical results, not consequences of the root. Six threads ran through the construction; this section collects them, then the instances of the second one, the multipliers met on the way and a single dictionary.

18.1 Six threads

  1. Multipliers are physical quantities. The multipliers of the equations of motion are momenta (Sections 8.3, 8.4 and 8.7); the multipliers of tasks are forces (Sections 9.2–⁠9.3), those of contacts are contact wrenches (Section 9.3; on a floating base, in the plant’s own Gauss principle, Section 17), and in a hierarchy the decoupled task forces are the same multipliers written in another basis (Section 13.4). All of them are covectors: their values depend on the coordinates, their pairing with velocities, a power, does not (Sections 13.1, 15.1).
  2. Argmin versus certificate. One minimization, of the stage cost plus the rate of change of the boundary term, yields a controller (its argmin) and a certificate (the sign of its minimum, equivalently a Hamilton–Jacobi inequality). They answer different questions, and a good controller can fail the certificate (Sections 4.6, 6.4, 10.2, 11.4). A certificate belongs to the pair of Lagrangian and boundary term.
  3. Weak versus strict Lyapunov functions. A Lagrangian that penalizes position can only be certified by a strict Lyapunov function. The mechanical energy is weak and certifies only damping (Section 10.3); the composite energy of Slotine and Li is strict (Section 11); for a strict hierarchy no single energy-type certificate is available, and the analysis is cascaded (Sections 13.5, 16.3).
  4. Invariant versus attributed. The window length does not change the certificate, and, for a given torque, the priority order does not change the total power injected through the tasks. What they change is how a total is split: between the computed window and the estimated tail (Sections 3.4, 6.5, 16.2), or among priority levels (Sections 13.4, 15).
  5. Two Lagrangians. The variational Lagrangian (the cost we choose) and the physical one (the robot) are different objects (Sections 8.3, 12). Controllers that act through impressed forces shape the physical one and keep the natural inertia; controllers that impose tasks as constraints in a variational principle shape the inertia (Sections 10.1, 12.5).
  6. Inequalities: signs and switches. Inequality constraints keep every structure above: the descent proof, the multipliers, the Hamilton–Jacobi certificate (with the supremum taken over the admissible set) and the block Gram–Schmidt picture (for each active set), for a fixed contact mode without frictional sliding and a feasible stack (conflicts and contacts: Sections 14.4 and 17). They add two things. Multipliers acquire a sign, but the sign lives in one basis and at one level: it carries over neither to the decoupled forces of a hierarchy nor to the power (Sections 9.4, 14.3). And active sets switch. In the constraint view, for a feasible stack, a switch moves the attribution of forces and power at and below the switching level, never the torque or the total power. In the impressed view, where the $F_i$ are prescribed, the torque, and with it the total power, jumps in general; re-mapping the forces through (91) at the switch removes the jump at an activation, and at a deactivation only if the stacked multiplier of the leaving row is zero there (Sections 14.2, 15.2). An active constraint also changes which boundary term is exact (Sections 7.1, 7.3).

A strict hierarchy adds a separate obstruction: its projected task forces are in general not the gradient of one potential, so not even a single $W$ of the form (69) certifies the whole hierarchy (Section 13.5). The natural candidate there is a boundary term per level in the decoupled coordinates $\zeta_i$; since every $\zeta_i$ vanishes at rest, each level’s term would need its own cross term, and whether the per-level descent defects then add up to a certificate is open (Section 16.3).

19.2 Literature checks

Every reference was checked against publisher, repository, library-catalogue or index records and the reference lists of citing papers, found through web search. A DOI is given only where it appeared in such a record. Only one full text of a cited work is relied on: the open-access preprint of Burgos & Paternain (2022). Its §1 and its reference list are the source of the history given in the rows for smooth and for real-analytic potentials in the table of Section 12.2: Painlevé’s 1904 example and Wintner’s version of it, the two-degree-of-freedom example of Laloy (1976), the Newtonian result of Palamodov (1977) and Taliaferro (1980b), its extension to an arbitrary kinetic energy by Kozlov (1981), the instability at a non-strict minimum proved by Laloy & Peiffer (1982) and the resulting two-degree-of-freedom analytic converse, and the statement and open status of Arnold’s problem 1971-4. These statements rest on that account, not on the original works. Entries marked ○ were confirmed only through secondary sources (citing papers or tables of contents). For these entries, and for the statements just listed, the specific statement attributed should be checked against the original before being relied on. The descriptions of the cited hierarchical controllers are kept at the level of their published abstracts and standard constructions; specific weightings and proofs should be taken from the papers themselves.
